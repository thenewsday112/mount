Chevy’s 2027 Silverado Police Truck Is Built for 112-MPH Pursuits

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Chevrolet is continuing its strategic push into the law enforcement sector with a first look at the 2027 Chevy Silverado Police Pursuit Vehicle (PPV). As police departments across the country increasingly phase out traditional sedans in favor of larger, more capable trucks, this pursuit-rated pickup is heavily positioned as a versatile fleet option. Building directly on the successful foundation established when the 2023 Chevy Silverado PPV was revealed, the 2027 iteration introduces updated front-end styling and heavy-duty interior features specifically engineered to accommodate bulky utility belts and complex radio setups.

The broad transition toward full-size trucks for daily patrol duty allows departments to utilize vehicles fully capable of clearing curbs, safely transporting hundreds of pounds of tactical gear, and withstanding severe daily wear and tear. Equipped with heavy-duty steel wheels, massive 16-inch Brembo brakes, and a standard locking rear differential, the 2027 Silverado PPV is expressly built for demanding municipal use.

Emergency Vehicles

V8 Power and Proven Reliability

While several competing automakers are actively introducing electric options to municipal fleets, Chevrolet is maintaining a strict focus on traditional internal combustion for highway patrol units that require sustained power and rapid refueling capabilities. Specs from a report from GM Authority show that the 2027 Silverado PPV is powered by a robust 5.3-liter V8 engine producing a reliable 355 horsepower. This proven powerplant is mated to a heavy-duty 10-speed automatic transmission and a high-capacity alternator necessary for continuously running emergency lighting and communication equipment over grueling shifts.

Fleet managers heavily prioritize proven, reliable platforms to minimize vehicle downtime. Following highly publicized reports that the some Police Squads are having bad luck with their Dodge Durangos due to unexpected mechanical issues, proven powertrain options remain a major selling point for Chevrolet’s commercial and heavy-duty truck division.

Chevrolet

Pursuit-Rated Performance

To meet strict law enforcement standards, the Silverado PPV utilizes a specially tuned suspension system that noticeably lowers the truck’s ride height for substantially improved high-speed stability. It is officially pursuit-rated by the manufacturer to reach top speeds of 112 mph, having undergone rigorous independent testing to ensure it can safely handle aggressive cornering and extreme braking without excessive brake fade.

This dedicated factory tuning is absolutely essential for officer safety and departmental liability management. In sharp contrast to standard civilian performance models occasionally pressed into service, like this California Camaro cop car that is banned from sport and track modes due to restrictive departmental policy, the Silverado PPV is explicitly engineered and fully warrantied to operate safely at its mechanical limits during active pursuits. By effectively balancing heavy-duty utility with pursuit-rated high-speed stability, the 2027 Silverado PPV provides modern departments with a highly capable, purpose-built patrol option.

Drought and wildfire are quietly weakening Earth’s carbon defenses

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

When drought and wildfire hit an ecosystem at the same time, something quietly breaks in the relationship between plants and water. 

A global satellite study has found that vegetation hit by these compound events becomes markedly more sensitive to rainfall. Yet, it is simultaneously less efficient at turning that water into carbon gain. 

The two processes, which normally move together, start to come apart. This finding’s implications for the health of the world’s forests are not reassuring.

The study was conducted by Huipeng Xi and Qunming Wang at Tongji University in Shanghai.

The carbon sink under pressure

Every year, vegetation absorbs enormous quantities of carbon dioxide through photosynthesis.

Much of that carbon eventually returns to the atmosphere, but the portion that remains stored in plants and soils helps slow climate change.

This service is not guaranteed, though. It depends on ecosystems remaining functional, which in turn depends heavily on water. 

Precipitation is the primary driver of plant growth across much of the globe. How sensitively vegetation photosynthesis responds to rainfall variability is a key indicator of ecosystem health.

After drought and wildfire, however, a sharp increase in that sensitivity may signal that vegetation is struggling to recover.

Nearly 200,000 compound events

The researchers analyzed global satellite data from 2001 to 2022. They used three vegetation proxies to track photosynthetic activity at fine spatial resolution. 

These measurements included faint light emitted by plants during photosynthesis and two satellite-based indicators of vegetation greenness and growth.

Drought was identified using two complementary indices. One was based on precipitation deficits (meteorological drought). The other used terrestrial water storage anomalies derived from satellite gravimetry (hydrological drought). 

Fire was tracked using ESA’s burned-area dataset.

The team identified 72,088 compound meteorological drought–fire events across the globe between 2001 and 2022, and 108,373 compound hydrological drought–fire events. 

A compound event was defined as drought and fire striking the same location in the same month. To keep the signal clean, no other major disturbance could have occurred within a year before or after.

More rain sensitivity, less efficiency

Across the full study period, global vegetation sensitivity to precipitation declined. But in the one to three years after a compound drought–fire event, that sensitivity jumped back up more sharply than after drought alone. 

Hydrological drought–fire events drove the strongest response: sensitivity increased by 7.89×10⁻⁵ mm⁻¹ using the fluorescence measure, compared to 3.20×10⁻⁵ mm⁻¹ for meteorological drought–fire combinations. 

Around 54.6% of all vegetated areas showed increased sensitivity in the aftermath of compound events.

At the same time, water-use efficiency – the ratio of carbon gained to water consumed – declined across most of those same areas. 

The two processes, which in a healthy ecosystem track each other reasonably closely, were pulling apart.

“Our findings reveal a critical vulnerability: after compound drought–fire events, ecosystems become more dependent on rainfall but less efficient at converting water into carbon gain,” Wang said. 

“This decoupling suggests that vegetation may be entering a fragile recovery state, where increased rain sensitivity does not translate into healthier carbon–water balance – a warning signal for ecosystem resilience under escalating climate extremes.”

What’s driving the shift

To understand why some areas showed stronger responses than others, the team applied a random forest model to 22 environmental variables. It explained 79% of the spatial variation in sensitivity changes. 

The top predictors were atmospheric aridity and soil nutrients, particularly nitrogen and phosphorus. Where the air was drier and soils more nutrient-depleted, post-disturbance sensitivity rose more steeply.

The finding points to a compounding feedback. Drought dries the air and depletes soil moisture. Fire consumes organic matter and disrupts nutrient cycling. 

Recovering vegetation may therefore face drier atmospheric conditions and disrupted nutrient supplies.

Together, these pressures could help explain why rainfall becomes more influential even as water-use efficiency declines.

A warning for the carbon cycle

Compound drought–fire events are not rare anomalies. The study catalogued nearly 200,000 of them across two decades. 

Climate projections suggest they will become more frequent as temperatures rise.

If each one leaves the affected ecosystem more dependent on precipitation while less capable of using it efficiently, the cumulative effect on the terrestrial carbon sink could be substantial.

Vegetation that has decoupled its carbon and water processes is also harder to predict. 

Earth system models that assume a stable relationship between rainfall and photosynthesis may misrepresent these ecosystems.

They could potentially overestimate the carbon sink in regions with high compound disturbance frequency. 

The authors suggest that vapor pressure deficit and soil nutrient status could serve as early-warning indicators.

The satellite record reveals a troubling pattern: when drought and wildfire strike together, their effects on vegetation can persist for years after the immediate disturbance ends.

Even when plants begin to recover, they may become more dependent on rainfall while growing less efficient at using water to absorb carbon.

As climate extremes intensify, that imbalance could weaken the ability of forests and other ecosystems to help regulate Earth’s climate.

The study is published in the Journal of Remote Sensing.

When Electric Cars Were for Women

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →


At the turn of the last century, the automobile industry was still new and evolving rapidly. In 1898, there were about 50 carmakers in the United States. A decade later, that number had increased fivefold. In 1901, the companies produced 7,000 vehicles. By 1910, production had reached 181,000.

For several decades, electric cars vied with those powered by steam and internal combustion, and nobody was sure which technology would come out on top. Steam had the legacy of locomotive technology behind it but came with a risk of fire. Steam-powered vehicles could also become exceedingly hot, depending on the placement of the boiler and the steam piping. Both gasoline and electric cars faced logistical challenges: How far could you travel without refueling or charging? You could always throw an extra can of gasoline in the back of the car, but charging a battery involved more planning. The infrastructure for long-distance travel was not yet in place.

Before the invention of the electric starter in 1911, gasoline-powered engines had to be started with a hand crank; once running, they were loud and emitted noxious fumes. Fully electric vehicles, by contrast, were easy to start; you just turned on the main battery switch, often with a key, and there was no foul exhaust to deal with.

Electric carmakers looking for a way to differentiate their products decided that these attributes made their vehicles perfect for women. (Electric cars were also marketed to doctors so that they could make house calls in the middle of the night without waking the neighbors.) General Electric, which made car chargers based on mercury-arc rectifiers, was so captivated by the idea of women driving electric cars that the company created a series of advertisements featuring stylishly dressed women plugging in their cars and posing with their charging stations.

In ads for its electric car chargers, General Electric featured women using the equipment.miSci—Museum of Innovation and Science

A young woman named Fannie Goodnow was an early EV adopter. She owned a 1908 Bailey Victoria Phaeton, an open two-seater that really did look like a horseless carriage, and she used a GE mercury-arc rectifier to charge it.

Who Invented the Mercury-Arc Rectifier?

Electrical engineer Peter Cooper Hewitt invented the mercury vapor lamp, which became the basis for the mercury-arc rectifier used in car-charging systems. Bettmann/Getty Images

Electrical engineer Peter Cooper Hewitt is generally credited with the invention of the mercury-arc rectifier. During the first decade of the 20th century, Hewitt received several U.S. patents for electric lamps and other apparatus for transmitting and using electric currents. U.S. patents 968,896 and 1,163,706, granted to Percy H. Thomas, advanced Hewitt’s designs. Thomas was chief electrician for the Cooper Hewitt Electric Co., which Hewitt had cofounded with George Westinghouse to manufacture and sell his lamps.

The mercury-arc rectifier functioned as a diode. In a car charger like the one Fannie Goodnow used, it converted alternating current from the power grid into direct current needed to charge the car’s battery. A small amount of liquid mercury contained in a glass tube would vaporize and glow as electricity passed through it. Coupled with the carbon anodes, the vapor allowed current to flow in only one direction. This unit took about 12 hours to charge a drained battery.

A Carriage Maker Pivots to Electric Cars

I couldn’t find any records as to why Fannie Goodnow opted for an electric car. But as a well-to-do woman living in the Boston area, she had more than a half dozen automobile producers to choose from. The one she chose was S.R. Bailey & Co. of Amesbury, Mass.

This early electric car charger used a mercury-arc rectifier to convert AC to DC.Heritage Museums & Gardens

According to Galen Handy, who maintains the Early Electric Vehicles website, Samuel Robinson Bailey started in the carriage and sleigh business as a teenager, winning a best-in-show prize at a carriage exhibition in Portland, Maine, in 1855. Bailey developed a reputation as an inventor and designed processes to streamline the manufacture of his vehicles. He went into business for himself in 1882. Just over a decade later, one of Bailey’s carriages won a gold medal at the 1893 World’s Columbian Exposition in Chicago.

Bailey’s son Edwin attended the exposition, where he likely would have seen a vision of the future: the Morrison electric wagon. This six-passenger, battery-powered vehicle ferried visitors and VIPs around the fairgrounds. It wasn’t long before automobiles began eating into the carriage business. In December 1906, with the carriage industry collapsing, S.R. Bailey & Co. announced it was getting into electric vehicles.

Thomas Edison Designed a Bad Battery

Bailey’s first model, the Victoria Phaeton, used an Edison alkaline battery. Thomas Edison had been working on a lighter, more reliable, and more powerful battery for the auto industry since the 1890s, and announced the achievement with great fanfare in 1903. Unfortunately for Bailey and other carmakers who deployed it, the battery leaked and wouldn’t hold a charge. Edison was forced to go back to the drawing board. By 1910, he had a better battery in production, but by then his friend Henry Ford had introduced the Model T, setting the stage for the internal combustion engine to take over the market.

While Edison toiled to improve his tech, Bailey pivoted to a battery from the Gould Storage Battery Co. of Depew, N.Y. Gould also supplied batteries for railroads, utilities, subways, elevators, and farms. Goodnow’s Victoria Phaeton used a 30-cell lead-acid battery housed in a wooden box that was suspended under the chassis. The standard battery had a range of 64 to 80 kilometers; a larger battery with 50 percent more capacity was available for an upcharge. With the standard battery, the Victoria Phaeton cost US $2,000 (about $73,000 today, or about the list price of an upscale EV like the Audi Q8 or BMW i5).

Fannie Goodnow’s electric car charger now resides at the Heritage Museums & Gardens in Sandwich, Mass. But her 1908 Bailey Victoria Phaeton has been lost to history. Shown here is a 1911 version of that model.Sarah Thornington/Heritage Museums & Gardens

Goodnow installed a mercury-arc rectifier charging station in the barn adjacent to her parents’ summer home in Hull, Mass. Bill Lillie of Vintage Electric Cars notes that the charging plug was of an unusual design. This is, of course, one of the banes of any early adopter: nonstandardized plugs and cables. The Electric Vehicle Association of America, which formed in 1910, tried to put forward a standard so that electric vehicles could be charged at any charging station. (Modern EV owners can no doubt relate.) But savvy electric car owners knew to carry spare plug adapters and cables when traveling.

When Edison’s improved battery was finally available, Bailey began using it in the Victoria Phaeton and then went on a publicity blitz. In September 1910, the carmaker sponsored a round-trip tour from New York to Boston, with a side trip up New Hampshire’s Mount Washington, totaling about 1,600 km. Sadly, the “This Car Climbed Mt. Washington” bumper sticker hadn’t yet become popular.

Thomas Edison supplied the batteries for an electric car made by S.R. Bailey & Co., which then went on a publicity road trip from New York to Boston. miSci—Museum of Innovation and Science

Although Thomas Edison posed for a photo with the new Bailey Victoria Phaeton that featured his new battery, he himself chose a Detroit Electric Victoria for his own use. (Edison was known to be a terrible driver, so someone else likely chauffeured him around.)

Unable to compete against the rising tide of Model Ts, S.R. Bailey sold its factory in 1915, having produced about 500 to 600 electric vehicles.

Although the vehicle Fannie Goodnow chose turned out to be just a blip in automotive history, I like to imagine her driving about town in a beautiful car that embodied all the quirks of early adoption. I hope her friends didn’t make fun of her for investing in electric just before internal combustion took off. I hope she didn’t get stranded without the proper charging cable. I do not know what happened to her or her Victoria Phaeton, but her charging station remained in situ until 2022, when it was donated to the Heritage Museums & Gardens in Sandwich, Mass. Now it stands ready to tell stories of technological change and adoption that continue to resonate today.
