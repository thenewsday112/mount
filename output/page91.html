In Brooklyn, a phone-free room of news junkies agreed a blue wave is coming — and debated the data-center identity crisis

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

I checked my phone into a pouch at the door of 100 Sutton St. in Greenpoint on Thursday night, along with about 300 other people, five days before the midterms. It was deep in the so-called “commie corridor” that runs from northwest Queens through northern Brooklyn, and the crowd for Newsreel’s “Midterm Mania” panel and campaign staff, consultants, organizers, founders and journalists were gathered: the people who make a living off the feed, spending a night without it.

Chi Ossé, the youngest member of the New York City Council and a democratic socialist, got the first big reaction. “Make some noise, Brooklyn,” he said when he walked onstage. When he called for Democrats to come out against the war in Iran and part of the room didn’t clap, he had a ruling ready: “Everyone who’s not clapping is supportive of war.”

Newsreel founder and CEO Jack Brewster had asked everyone who RSVPed, “What’s the one election question you want answered?” More than 150 answered, and the walls were covered in Post-it notes. Brewster set up the night’s last panel with his own: “Democrats are on track to win big according to polls, but they still cannot agree on who they are.”

Over 90 minutes, the people onstage made his case. They didn’t agree on whether the party is socialist, or on whether data centers are a jobs program or a menace. They didn’t agree on what to do about AI beyond taxing it. What they shared was the goal of winning Tuesday, and the odds look good. The New York Times/Siena poll released Oct. 21 had Democrats up 10 on the congressional ballot and Trump’s approval at 36%. That leaves the party most likely to take the House without a position on the AI buildout that’s eating up corporate capital spending, at a moment when half of Americans oppose a data center within three miles of their home, according to the POLITICO Poll, conducted by PF Global.

Whatever you need to do

Ossé, who represents Bed-Stuy and Crown Heights, dropped a primary challenge to Hakeem Jeffries last year after the DSA wouldn’t back him. Forbes’ Isabel Togoh asked whether it matters if a progressive candidate calls himself a socialist. “The number one thing that I care about within our party is taking back this country back from the right,” Ossé said. “Whatever you need to do in order to do that at this point in time, you know, I do support that.”

That covered Abdul El-Sayed, the Michigan Democrat who won his Senate primary with Bernie Sanders’ and AOC’s backing and has since insisted he isn’t a socialist. Whatever El-Sayed is doing, Ossé said, “is one that I support in order to beat this fascist, far-right government.” He also said he believes “the direction of this party will go towards democratic socialism.”

On AI, Ossé said he doesn’t “really understand it that much” and barely uses it himself. He still had a plan for it. He’d call big tech and AI CEOs to testify before Congress and “maybe be put behind bars for a little bit,” and on Sanders’ proposed one-time 50% stock tax on the biggest AI companies, he said: “Cosign and tax some more.”

The data center lane

The data-center panel was where the room was most united, and where the party’s own record looked shakiest. Moderator Dominic-Madori Davis of TechCrunch passed along what a private-equity executive had told her: “people are not upset at data centers, they’re just mad at AI.”

Hilary Flint, who organizes against data centers in Pennsylvania for the Center for Oil and Gas Organizing, said the right tends to worry about eminent domain and the left about the environment, but “I’ve never seen people of all beliefs be on the same page about an environmental issue the way they are on this one.” The Pennsylvania Data Center Resistance group on Facebook now has over 44,000 members from across the political spectrum, she said. Maia Woluchem, who has tracked the buildout in the state for Data & Society, put data centers in “a long legacy of industrial obfuscation,” landing in towns that already have fracking wells in backyards and families living next to coal mines.

Nationally, the environmental footprint is small. Data centers account for about 1.5% of global electricity demand and 0.5% of global CO2 emissions, according to International Energy Agency data compiled by Carbon Brief. In the U.S., they used 4.4% of the country’s electricity in 2023, according to a Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory report, a share it projects could reach 6.7% to 12% by 2028. The load is concentrated, though. In Virginia, data centers already use 26% of the state’s electricity, according to figures Carbon Brief cites.

Which party is really anti-data center?

The Democrat running Pennsylvania has been on both sides of it. Gov. Josh Shapiro announced Amazon’s $20 billion data center investment in the state, per the AP. After “incredible pushback,” Woluchem said, Shapiro “tried to reverse that decision” a couple of months ago. Flint said activists have pushed the governor to the point of “saying we have the strictest data center laws in the country, which isn’t quite true, but we’re going to get him there.” Her plan for him: “We’re going to move him slowly until he reflects his constituents.”

Morris Katz, a partner at Fight Agency and Mamdani’s lead media strategist, later offered Republicans the issue outright. “Republicans can feel free to have the pro-data-center lane if they’d like,” he said, adding that Trump-plus-25 districts are in play because of data centers.

The other side of the argument is the party’s old base. Batya Ungar-Sargon, the NewsNation host who calls herself a “MAGA leftist” and voted for Trump in 2024, said national trade unions are “crossing party lines for the first time and endorsing Republicans” because data centers mean good jobs for electricians and pipefitters. Katz said the unions “have been forced to the table by failed policies for decades,” by the same people, he argued, who have been screwing workers for 30 years.

Ungar-Sargon noted that those screwed-over workers have gone to the right. Trump won most voters earning under $100,000, she noted, and Democrats represent nine of the 10 richest counties. “The far-left has only accelerated the problem of the political realignment,” she said. She pointed to Medicare for All, which she called “extremely popular with working class voters. Until you tell them that their taxes will have to go up.” Katz responded that he didn’t think taxes would go up.

Katz called himself “a populist who believes we should have a big tent party.” Astead Herndon, host of Vox’s America, Actually, argued that 2024 wasn’t a realignment so much as “an establishment collapse.”

Ask again Wednesday

Katz threw cold water on a breakthrough for the left’s identity crisis. “Even in the best-case scenario where we win big,” he said, “I think we still need to be ready to have harder conversations about where we go from here.”

The rapid-fire round showed how far off those conversations are. Brewster asked for a yes or no on whether enough new Democratic senators would win to push Chuck Schumer out of leadership. Katz said, “I hope we get as many Democratic senators as we humanly,” before Brewster cut in: “Can not answer.” Asked about Mamdani’s next move, Katz said the mayor would wake up “laser focused on New York City.” Herndon, asked who controls the Senate in January: “I don’t want to make a prediction.”

The most specific forecast of the night came from an AI company. Aryan Ranjan, co-founder, CFO and head of AI at the campaign-software startup Baraza, called 231 House seats and 52 Senate seats for Democrats. Ungar-Sargon was the only panelist willing to call the Senate: “I think the Republicans hold on to the Senate, but I don’t know. I could be wrong.”

Then the chairs were cleared, the DJ started, and I got my phone back.

United exits Mexico's new Tulum airport, adds 2 more routes from San Francisco

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

It has been less than three years since Mexico's newest airport in Tulum welcomed passengers for the very first time.

Now, United Airlines is pulling out.

The carrier on Friday confirmed it will end all service to Tulum International Airport (TQO) just after the holidays.

The news was first spotted by Ishrion Aviation.

  • United's flights from Newark Liberty International Airport (EWR) will run for the last time Jan. 2.
  • Nonstop service from Houston's George Bush Intercontinental Airport (IAH) will end after the Jan. 4 flight.
A United Airlines plane on final descent into Dulles International Airport (IAD). SEAN CUDAHY/THE POINTS GUY

In a statement, United said it makes "regular adjustments" to its flight schedule "based on seasonality, demand and other market factors."

It's not the first carrier to have second thoughts on Tulum.

Another blow for new Mexico airport

The brand-new airport first welcomed international guests in March 2024, and airlines quickly added flights from major U.S. hubs.

The airport was billed as a more convenient link to Tulum's resorts versus Cancun International Airport (CUN), which sits more than two hours to the north by car.

SEAN CUDAHY/THE POINTS GUY

But within months, carriers started slashing service, citing disappointing demand.

  • United had previously ended its Tulum service from Chicago and Los Angeles before this week's decision to exit the market entirely.
  • JetBlue has also pulled out of Tulum.
  • Both American Airlines and Delta Air Lines have cut back service.

Delta this winter will offer flights to Tulum only from Atlanta. It previously had routes from Detroit and Minneapolis.

American still has flights from Dallas and Miami, though it once offered service from Charlotte, too.

SEAN CUDAHY/THE POINTS GUY

All told, air service from the U.S. to Tulum will be down more than 50% versus just two years ago, according to data from aviation analytics firm Cirium.

2 more routes from SFO

While United flyers will lose nonstop service to one beach destination in Mexico, the carrier continues to expand its largest West Coast hub.

The carrier on Friday confirmed it's adding two new transcontinental flights from San Francisco International Airport (SFO) — both to Virginia.

SEAN CUDAHY/THE POINTS GUY

Twice-weekly flights to Richmond International Airport (RIC) will begin on March 28. Service will run on Thursdays and Sundays.

That same day, the carrier will launch service to Norfolk International Airport (ORF). Flights will operate on Wednesdays and Saturdays.

It's United's second SFO expansion in as many weeks. Just days ago, the carrier announced a trio of new domestic routes from its Bay Area hub.

These latest new routes from San Francisco come as the airline prepares to open its newest United Club — which even features an outdoor terrace — later this year.

HTTP/3 is faster with some work and help from Cloudflare

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

When you're ordering food from your phone, even time spent navigating the app is time wasted. At the P99 2026 virtual conference, Elvis Chidera, a staff engineer at Germany-based global delivery service Delivery Hero, explained how the company moved most of its user base to HTTP/3, and consequently cut its apps’ total end-to-end response time of the home screen by 17 percent.

Chidera, who managed the project, explained that, as promised on the tin, HTTP/3 delivers the web faster than HTTP/2, though it has slow bits that need to be adjusted to work properly.

Uber eats Delivery Hero

Delivery Hero is all but unknown in the US, which is dominated by Uber Eats and DoorDash. But it is a company with global reach outside the US, with an estimated user base of 60 million across Europe, Latin America, Africa, the Middle East, and Asia. Uber is currently acquiring Delivery Hero for $14.8 billion, with plans to unite the company with Uber Eats.

With this purchase, Uber would expand the number of markets where it offers both ride-hailing and delivery services, though not necessarily under the Uber Eats nameplate. Delivery Hero centrally manages separate apps for different regions, all of which it built or acquired. Asians can order poke bowls and sundries through Foodpanda. Turks can order kebabs through Yemeksepeti. Throughout Spain, Italy, Poland, Romania, Ukraine, and much of Africa, the Glovo app is where to ring up the late-night deliveries. And in Latin America, users dial in to PedidosYa to get what they need from nearby farmacias, restaurantes, and mercados.

All these regions have widely varying network connectivity, a problem Delivery Hero wanted to minimize.

Last mile networking was most often the bottleneck. Apps in Pakistan, for instance, could take nearly a second (at p90) to establish a secure handshake with a server. The engineering team looked at HTTP/3 as a way to lower, even eliminate, that latency.

A QUIC-er HTTP

The Internet Engineering Task Force (IETF) formally introduced the third version of the internet’s Hypertext Transfer Protocol in 2022. HTTP is the application layer protocol that connects browsers and other resources connected to servers over the Internet.

The previous versions of HTTP were built on the Transmission Control Protocol (TCP), which offered guaranteed delivery and flow control. In conjunction with the Internet Protocol (IP), TCP/IP built the modern internet.

HTTP/3 replaced TCP with Quick UDP Internet Connection (QUIC), developed by Google, to address some of TCP’s perceived sluggishness as a transfer protocol.

UDP stands for User Datagram Protocol and, unlike TCP, it does not guarantee packet delivery or flow control. UDP is faster because it doesn’t wait around for a signature (QUIC adds in its own flow control on top of UDP).

But the UDP-based HTTP/3 reduces, and sometimes eliminates, the round trips needed to secure connections. The app can cache a session ticket and, on a later connection, use QUIC's 0-RTT capability to start sending eligible application data while the handshake is still underway, rather than waiting for it to finish.

“Essentially, you can eliminate that one second” of latency that plagued slower networks, Chidera said. “You don't want to pay the cost of two round trips just to exchange data, if you could do it faster.”

And unlike HTTP/2, HTTP/3 doesn’t get hung up when packets are lost or delayed. There’s no head-of-line blocking because packets are sent in multiple streams, not just the single string that HTTP/2 offers.

Multitask during initialization

Setting up HTTP/3 turned out to be surprisingly easy, Chidera found. Delivery Hero used Cloudflare’s content delivery network to serve its global user base, so moving to HTTP/3 involved a simple reconfiguration.

Adding HTTP/3 support to the clients, mostly mobile, proved almost as straightforward. Apple has supported HTTP/3 since iOS 15 and most native browsers already support HTTP/3 out of the box.

Android proved a bit trickier. The engineers used Google’s Cronet library for the networking stack, initially wired into their Android apps with the Cronet Transport for OkHttp wrapper to minimize rewriting their existing code, originally written for the OkHttp HTTP client.

Out of the box, Cronet helped support the HTTP/3 rollout, which reduced network-level request failures. But it also came with a significant catch: the engine took approximately 800 milliseconds to initialize at P90, eating into some of HTTP/3's 0-RTT speed benefits. So the team forked the code into their own bridge using a library called OKCronet. They also did some additional engineering work, which involved overlapping Cronet initialization with other background tasks.

Overall, they slashed the P95 latency time from 1.2 seconds to 120 milliseconds, achieving a 10x improvement in read latency.

Choose your congestion control algo wisely

Overall, the engineering team moved 95 percent of the users over to HTTP/3. Those devices that couldn’t use the protocol (largely due to networks blocking UDP traffic) automatically fell back to HTTP/2. Handshake latency between the apps and the server was cut by 51 percent. Pakistan’s response time was cut in half.

One hurdle remained, however. Secondary screens on some of the apps (pages/screens that aren’t the home page) experienced latency issues. These issues only happened in certain markets that were known to be prone to packet loss, such as Pakistan.

Delivery Hero’s engineers attributed the issue to Cloudflare’s choice of the congestion control algorithm for HTTP/3, Cubic, which historically has not handled packet loss well. The engineering team tested some of its traffic with Google HTTP/3-aware servers, which use another congestion protocol, Bottleneck Bandwidth and Round-trip propagation time (BBR), and did not get hung up on the packet loss.

Delivery Hero is currently working with Cloudflare to resolve this specific congestion algorithm issue, Chidera said.
