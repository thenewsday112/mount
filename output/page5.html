Amorim analyses how Milan were able to overpower Inter: “High pressure and low blocking”

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

By: Oliver Fisher

AC Milan head coach Ruben Amorim refused to be drawn into discussions about the Scudetto, even if he does see improvements from his side.

Milan were in dreamland after 45 minutes of their derby clash against Inter, going in 3-1 up at the break thanks to goals from Christian Pulisic, Adrien Rabiot and Diego Moreira. As it would turn out, that would be enough to win the bragging rights.

It marked a fourth straight win for Milan in the derby and it reduces the gap to the city rivals back down to four points. While there is still work to be done for the Rossoneri, it feels like a big step forward mentally for the squad.

Amorim spoke to Sky after the full-time whistle to reflect on what was a memorable night for him as a head coach and for the team, with his words relayed by MilanNews.

Is this the most important victory of the start of the season?

“It’s important. The atmosphere is fantastic, and you can tell after a match like that, especially on a Wednesday like this. It’s clear, even more so after the defeat against Bologna, we wanted to celebrate even more.

“I’m really proud of what my players did, and with so little rest between matches, and especially once again after Bologna, they really fought for the entire match. I’m really proud.”

A nearly perfect team: do you have anything to complain about? You seemed calmer today…

“I try to read and interpret what the team tells me: from the first minute I had the feeling that we were ready to be competitive. The team has always been focused and calm, and so I always had this confidence.”

Photo by AC Milan

Is AC Milan a different Milan with Modric, Rabiot, and Pulisic?

“It’s not just those three players, I think it’s the whole atmosphere, the team’s focus. I like the way everyone plays and expresses themselves. For example, I’d like to talk a little about Pavlović and De Vrij.

“They performed well in every game, especially against such a strong team, even in the penalty area. And then, obviously, players like Rabiot, Pulisic, Moreira… they all did very well. All together, I have to say, we were a better team.”

Why Gila on the right and De Winter in the centre?

“Looking at Inter’s stats, we didn’t know if Dimarco would play. They play and cross a lot from the wings and come to create a lot of density in the box, so I think Pavlović and De Winter are the best for crossing.

“That’s the idea: to match our defenders with their attackers, so everyone can play in slightly different defensive roles.”

How did you manage not to concede anything to Inter in the second half?

“I think the lines were closer together; you could see that throughout the match in terms of high pressure and low blocking. The lines were always close together, so the distances were shorter, which obviously also allowed us to run less and stay fresher throughout the game.

“We were very aggressive on the outside, intercepting many crosses before they were launched, which is very difficult against a team like Inter. We defended well by pressing high up, but we also defended well on the sides. We were more aggressive than we usually are.”

Is the Scudetto possible?

“What Cardinale was saying is that the objective is the Scudetto, but it’s important to win these games to make the fans understand that things won’t change in a month, two months, or a year.

“We have to go through this process: you can’t just hire a new coach, replace all the players, and have a new squad for each new coach; that takes time. Then we’ll need magical nights and evenings, and obviously we’ll need to get through difficulties like Wednesday’s and the joy of tonight.

“I want to say that the situation won’t change overnight; it’s not possible. Is the Scudetto possible? I don’t know when, but we still have to be very honest with the fans, and I know that people aren’t stupid; they understand and know the growth this team and this club are in.”

1000+ Installs Later: DRY for your Storage with Filament

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

A year ago, I released Dedupler - Laravel package that prevents duplicate file storage using SHA-1 hashing. The idea was simple: don't repeat yourself (DRY) shouldn't just apply to code; it should apply to your storage too. Why store the same 10MB PDF five times just because it's attached to five different models?

To my surprise, the package crossed 1,000+ installs. It turns out that dealing with storage bloat and slow backups is a pain point many of us share.

However, the relevance of deduplication isn't fading - it's becoming more critical. With SSD and cloud storage prices surging over the past year, every wasted gigabyte has a real cost.

But I noticed a barrier: some developers wanted to use the package but found the setup a bit too manual. They just wanted a simple file upload field in their admin panel that magically doesn't create duplicates.

So, I built a solution for the Laravel community's favorite admin panel.

🚀 Introducing filament-dedupler-uploader
It's a lightweight Filament plugin that gives you a ready-to-use UI for Dedupler. It brings the DRY principle to your Filament forms.

Instead of writing custom logic, you can now just use the component:

use Maxkhim\FilamentDeduplerUploader\Forms\Components\DeduplerFileUploader; DeduplerFileUploader::make('attachment') ->multiple()

What happens under the hood?

The user uploads a file.

The plugin checks the SHA-1 hash via Dedupler.

If it exists wherever else in Storage, it links the existing physical file to your model.

If it doesn't, it stores it once and links it.

No duplicates. Cleaner database. Smaller cloud bill.

If you are running a SaaS, a CMS, or any Filament app with file uploads, give it a try.

Justin Lamson's 5 first-half touchdowns lead Montana State in 61-7 rout of Cal Poly

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

BOZEMAN, Mont. (AP) — Justin Lamson led a first-half onslaught with three touchdown passes and two on the ground, powering Montana State to a 61-7 victory over Cal Poly on Saturday.

Carter Dahlke returned the opening kickoff 98 yards for a Montana State touchdown, then the Bobcats, ranked No. 3 in the Stats Perform FCS Top 25, rolled up 336 yards on offense in the first half. They scored touchdowns on all six of their full possessions in the first half and led 48-7 at halftime.

Lamson completed 15 of 19 passes for 177 yards with touchdowns of 44 yards to Dahlke, 2 yards to Luvens Valcin and 5 yards to Dane Steel. Lamson had touchdown runs of 1 and 20 yards. He did not play in the second half.

Backup quarterback Patrick Duchien was 8 of 10 passing for 56 yards with a 26-yard touchdown pass to Jordan Reed for the game's final score late in the third quarter. Earlier in the third, Colson Coon had a 9-yard touchdown run for the Bobcats (8-1, 5-1 Big Sky).

Dahlke caught six passes for 103 yards and had 201 all-purpose yards.

The only score for Cal Poly (4-6, 3-4) came on a 28-yard pass from backup quarterback Bo Kelly to Tayvion McCoy in the final minute of the first half. Kelly was 14-for-26 passing for 128 yards and was intercepted once. Starter Anthony Grigsby Jr. was 1-for-8 for 11 yards and threw two interceptions.

Montana State had a 518-258 advantage in total yards on offense.

The Universal Frontend Nightmare: "Cannot Reproduce"

Every frontend engineer and engineering manager has lived this scenario: A user reports an obscure bug. They upload a 20-second screen recording showing a broken checkout modal or a glitchy video canvas. The QA ticket gets created, and an engineer spends 3 hours trying to recreate the exact environment, state, and click sequence.

Half the time, the ticket gets closed with the dreaded label:
Closed: Cannot Reproduce (Works on My Machine).

What if a bug report didn’t just contain a video, but automatically converted itself into a reproducible Playwright E2E test, diagnosed the codebase, patched the bug, and opened a ready-to-merge GitHub Pull Request?

That is the vision behind GhostPR (AutoFix AI).

In this post, I’ll walk through how I used Kiro Desktop IDE and Spec-Driven Development to stitch together video streams, headless browser automation, and AST code healing into a unified CLI engine for the Kiroween 2026 Frankenstein Category.

Why GhostPR is a "Frankenstein" Creation

The Kiroween Frankenstein Category challenges developers to take seemingly incompatible, disparate components and weld them into a coherent, powerful creation.

GhostPR brings together four wildly different systems:

The Senses (Video & DOM Streams): Browser screen recordings stored on Cloudflare R2 via QuickCast.
The Laboratory (Headless Automation): @playwright/test synthesizing deterministic TypeScript E2E specs.
The Brain (Spec-Driven AI): Kiro steering directives enforcing invariant assertions and defensive patching.
The Hands (Version Control): GitHub Octokit automating isolated git branch creation and verified Pull Requests.

None of these tools were designed to talk to each other out of the box. GhostPR acts as the neural bridge connecting them into an autonomous, closed-loop pipeline.

The Secret Weapon: Kiro Spec-Driven Development

When building an autonomous tool that writes and modifies code, LLM hallucinations can be catastrophic. If an AI agent attempts to patch code without proving the bug exists first, it risks breaking unrelated features.

To solve this, I built GhostPR strictly using Kiro's Spec-Driven Development paradigm:

  1. Architectural Boundaries in .kiro/steering/steering.md

Before writing a single line of JavaScript, I created .kiro/steering/steering.md. This established non-negotiable architectural guardrails:

Zero Cloud GPU: All video processing must run locally via native FFmpeg binaries ($0.00/mo infrastructure cost).
Deterministic Time Sync: Video keyframes and DOM interaction markers must be anchored to relative timestamps.
Invariant Scoped Syntax: Playwright specs must use scoped test.step() syntax with strict DOM assertions.
Defensive AST Patching: Patches must favor optional chaining (?.) and safe fallback states.

  1. Formal Requirements in .kiro/specs/ghost-pr-spec.md

I codified the 4-stage pipeline into 6 verifiable requirements (REQ-1 through REQ-6), ensuring each stage had a defined contract:

REQ-1: Ingest .webm stream from Cloudflare R2 without cloud GPU compute.
REQ-2: Extract 4 visual keyframes and parse interaction telemetry.
REQ-3: Synthesize tests/repro_.spec.ts.
REQ-4: Search target repository AST for error tokens.
REQ-5: Apply defensive patch and verify green execution.
REQ-6: Create Git branch fix/ghost-pr- and dispatch Pull Request.
How Kiro IDE Caught 4 Critical Architectural Gaps

One of the most impressive moments during development was when I loaded GhostPR into Kiro Desktop IDE and ran an architectural audit.

Kiro didn’t just check for syntax errors; it analyzed the entire dataflow against our steering directives and identified 4 subtle architectural gaps:

The "Disconnected Execution" Gap (Critical):
synth-test.js was generating the Playwright spec, but had hardcoded failureConfirmed: true without actually executing Playwright. Kiro pointed out that our self-healing loop was asserting a false positive:

"Stage 2 is asserting a lie. REQ-5 depends on running the test to confirm red state before diagnosing."

The Fix: We wired executePlaywrightSpec() directly into synthesizeTest(), setting failureConfirmed: !execResult.passed.

The Late pageerror Listener:
The error trap was declared inside a test.step() block after page.goto(). Kiro caught that any exception thrown during initial page boot or hydration would be missed.

The Fix: Hoisted the listener to the very top of the test body before navigation:

typescript
test('Autonomous E2E reproduction and state verification', async ({ page }) => {
// Hoist error trap BEFORE navigation to capture boot & hydration exceptions
const pageErrors = [];
page.on('pageerror', err => pageErrors.push(err.message));
await page.goto(targetUrl, { waitUntil: 'domcontentloaded' });
...

CWD Relative Path Drift:
Normalized all input and output paths upfront with path.resolve() so GhostPR runs identically from any working directory.

Stale Script Cleanup:
Removed a dead "start": "node src/server.js" entry in package.json and pointed it directly to src/cli.js.

After these 4 fixes were applied, Kiro inspected the codebase again and delivered the official sign-off:

"GhostPR is ready for competition submission. The red-to-green self-healing loop is now genuinely closed."

The 4 Stages in Action: A 30-Second Walkthrough

Here is what happens when you run a single CLI command:

bash
node src/cli.js --session "qc_bt6mrt8n" --repo "./" --dry-run
Stage 1: Zero-Cloud-GPU Visual Ingestion

GhostPR streams the .webm recording from Cloudflare R2 and extracts visual keyframes at key intervals using local FFmpeg binaries. It parses embedded interaction markers—capturing the exact DOM targets clicked and input values entered.

Stage 2: Autonomous Playwright Spec Synthesis (Red State)

GhostPR generates tests/repro_qc_bt6mrt8n.spec.ts. It immediately runs the spec in headless Chromium. Because the unpatched bug exists, Playwright fails—confirming that the test genuinely reproduces the failure.

Stage 3: AST Diagnosis & Defensive Patching (Green State)

With the exact error message and failing selector in hand, GhostPR scans the codebase, locates the offending line, and synthesizes a defensive AST patch (e.g. shielding null pointer exceptions with optional chaining).
It reapplies the Playwright test. The test passes green.

Stage 4: Verified Git Pull Request Dispatch

GhostPR checks out an isolated branch (fix/ghost-pr-qc_bt6mrt8n), commits the reproduction test and the patch, and uses Octokit to open a GitHub Pull Request with the video keyframes and Playwright test proof embedded in the PR description.

Key Takeaways from Building with Kiro
Spec-Driven Development Prevents AI Drift: Defining .kiro/steering/steering.md upfront acted as an architectural anchor. Whenever I asked Kiro to review or refine code, it evaluated changes against our specifications rather than hallucinating new patterns.
Local-First is Faster and Cheaper: By keeping FFmpeg and Playwright local, GhostPR incurs $0.00/month in cloud compute costs and executes complete bug reproductions in under 25 seconds.
Closing the Loop Matters: AI that generates code without running tests is just an expensive auto-complete. GhostPR's superpower is the Red-to-Green verification loop—it never submits a PR unless the synthesized test fails first and passes after.

We compressed 28 photos at 15 settings: here's where quality stops paying off

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Most advice on image quality comes down to "use 80 or so." We wanted numbers, so we ran 28 photos through 15 JPEG and WebP settings and recorded the file size and an SSIM score for each one.

The short version: going from JPEG quality 95 to 82 cut the typical photo by about half, and the SSIM score only moved from 0.998 to 0.978. Below that, each step saved less.

Disclosure: we make BananaFast, a free set of in-browser photo tools, and this test uses the same encoder libraries as those tools. The data is free to reuse either way.

The results

Each row is the median across all 28 photos, which were 10.8 megapixels at the median. "Saved" compares each photo with its own JPEG at quality 95. SSIM runs from 0 to 1, where 1 means identical to the source.

Format Quality Typical size (KB) Saved SSIM JPEG 95 2,608 0% 0.998 JPEG 92 2,076 15% 0.996 JPEG 90 1,932 20% 0.997 JPEG 85 1,446 41% 0.986 JPEG 82 1,172 51% 0.978 JPEG 80 1,071 54% 0.974 JPEG 75 832 65% 0.967 JPEG 70 720 69% 0.961 JPEG 65 628 73% 0.954 JPEG 60 570 76% 0.944 JPEG 50 463 80% 0.934 WebP 90 1,511 38% 0.985 WebP 80 840 65% 0.969 WebP 70 568 76% 0.957 WebP 60 487 78% 0.949

What stood out

The first cuts are the cheapest. Quality 95 to 82 saved about 51% for 0.020 of SSIM. Quality 60 to 50 saved only about 4 more percentage points for another 0.010.

WebP helps, but compare results rather than setting numbers. WebP at quality 80 was about 26% smaller than JPEG at quality 82, with an SSIM about 0.009 lower. The two quality scales aren't the same.

The same setting gives very different sizes. At JPEG quality 82, plant photos ran about 178 KB per megapixel and food photos about 85 KB. Those groups are small (4 and 2 photos), but detailed scenes do cost more.

Quality alone rarely hits a small file limit. Upload forms and web guides often ask for under 500 KB or 1 MB. Here's how many photos got there at their full pixel size:

JPEG quality Under 500 KB 1 MB or less 82 1 of 28 7 of 28 75 4 of 28 17 of 28 60 10 of 28 25 of 28 50 17 of 28 26 of 28

Even at quality 50, 11 of the 28 were still over 500 KB. If you have a small limit, you'll usually need to reduce the pixel dimensions too. This test didn't measure resizing, only how far quality alone gets you.

How we tested

  • Photos: 28 photographs from Wikimedia Commons, all marked CC0: people, landscapes, cities, night scenes, plants, food and indoor scenes.
  • Encoders: MozJPEG and libwebp through the jSquash libraries, run on a Mac on October 30, 2026.
  • Similarity: SSIM on brightness only, with an 11-pixel window.

The limits

  • It's a small sample. 28 photos can't represent every kind of picture, so treat this as a guide.
  • The sources were already compressed. These are JPEG files as published, not camera originals, and that can flatter the scores at high settings.
  • SSIM isn't your eyes. It's a math score, and we didn't run a test with human viewers, so we don't claim which setting "looks the same." Brightness-only SSIM also ignores color.
  • Medians don't always move smoothly. JPEG 92 scored slightly below 90 in the table above (0.996 and 0.997). We haven't looked into why, so the per-photo data may be more useful than that one row.

What we'd do with this

These are starting points from this test, not rules:

  • For general web photos, try JPEG around quality 82, or WebP around 80 if your platform takes it.
  • For photos you plan to print or edit again, stay at 90 or higher.
  • For a hard file size limit, resize first and then compress.
  • Check low settings with your own eyes on your own images.

Get the data

The full results are a free CSV: every photo at every setting, with a link to each source image. The original write-up is here. Please link to it if you cite the numbers.

If you run this on your own images and get something different, we'd like to hear about it in the comments, especially from anyone testing with camera originals or a color-aware metric.
