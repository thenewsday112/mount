Seabirds racing home over the ocean can get jet-lagged

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Flying across several time zones often causes jet lag, a feeling when the body insists it's the middle of the night while the clock says it's lunchtime. Now it turns out this nuisance is not exclusive to humans.

A new Science study on Manx shearwaters, seabirds that can fly hundreds of kilometers a day over the North Atlantic, shows they jet-lag themselves during their excursions. And because these birds navigate by the Sun, that jet lag leaves a measurable mark—it skews their compass.

Clockwork compass

Patrick Lewin is a behavioral and sensory ecologist at the University of Oxford and lead author of the study. “Manx shearwaters are medium-sized birds, about the same size as a pigeon,” Lewin said. “They are white underneath and black on top, but they have very long wings, and they are closely related to albatross.” Manx shearwaters nest in burrows on coastal islands around the North Atlantic. Most breed around the UK and Ireland, with about half of their population living in Wales.

Ongi Etorri: Welcome to the Twin Fin Invitational

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

When William Aliotti first debuted the Twin Fin Invitational (TFI), the collective response was, “Damn, that looks so fun.” Now, in its third year, it’s safe to say he has turned that feeling into a lasting impression, one the invited surfers all seem to share.

The concept is simple: surfers must ride twin fins and showcase their style, flow, and creativity. Performance is judged by the surfers and filmers over the course of the week, during two days of mixed heats (men and women surfing together), and a video recap presented at the closing ceremony determines the winners.

As an official media partner of the event, SURFER will be covering and hosting the four-episode series on our YouTube.

The first episode’s theme is “Ongi etorri,” which means “welcome” in Basque. It’s an introduction to how the TFI is so much more than a contest. Its essentially an incredible expression of hospitality, cultural celebration and, of course, twin fins.

William explains, “During this event, I love to show what I love to do. That’s why we decided to go to San Sebastián on that flat day, because I love good food and this is the capital of incredible food. It was such a joy to share this with all the riders.”

View this post on Instagram

Despite the early flat day, the forecast was promising for the whole week. Rather than having to force the event, the question was more so on cherry-picking the best windows for the big beach break in Zarautz. Aritz Aranburu’s Shelter Bar, located in the middle of the beautiful promenade in Zarautz, served as the headquarters for the TFI. In addition to the smorgasbord of healthy breakfasts, great coffee, delicious pintxos, and endless beers, Shelter played host to an epic welcome concert by The Currens.

Yes. Tom, Pat, and LeeAnn, held it down with a funky set that kept the bar grooving until late in the night. As a music snob, I can say that they played with passion, skill and exquisite taste. Tom and Pat switched off between playing drums and guitar, accompanying on vocals as LeeAnn led the set with her silky smooth voice and instrumental mastery.

View this post on Instagram

As for the surfing, Tom said, “It’s very interesting having this event and it specializes in boards that were from the early ’70s. That means that a long time ago, they had a good idea, a very exciting idea, and that it’s good enough in 2026 that you have some of the best surfers in the world here. They’re all surfing on the fish, which used to be a knee board. You see all kinds of amazing surfing on the fish now.”

Not the kind of energy you typically see before a heat. Lee-Ann Curren and Kaito Ohashi, showing nothing but love and respect.

Turning Trash Into Treasure: The Ups and Downs of a Miami Home Flipper’s 12-Year Career

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

For real estate investors and home flippers, one thing is key for remaining successful in a turbulent market: patience.

At least, that’s what South Florida investor, house flipper, and licensed real estate agent Branden Rivero thinks.

Rivero founded his company, Prop Hunters, in 2014. The Miami-Dade-based company has completed hundreds of flips, in addition to building and selling single-family homes.

“I started out just wholesaling, getting affordable properties and assigning the contracts until I was able to build some capital,” Rivero tells Realtor.com®. “I started flipping just one or two houses, and now we usually do about seven to 10 at a time.”

Prop Hunters flipped 25 homes last year, according to Rivero, but they’ve completed between 30 and 40 homes some years. This year’s gross profit is around $200,000 so far, accounting for both flips and new homes.

In his 12 years in business, Rivero has learned to go with the flow of the sometimes volatile Florida real estate market. Looking ahead 90 days to six months is a vital part of being prepared for anything.

“Everything is based on the market. Sometimes you can go full throttle when things are good,” says Rivero. “Then, once you see more turbulent times coming, you become more cautious, you underwrite a little more aggressive—you kind of have to play it by ear.”

Although Miami’s current market feels slow to some, Rivero is optimistic, saying that the real estate market is simply returning to the pre-pandemic norms.

“People are just used to the COVID rush, but I feel like it’s normal, just how it was in 2017, 2018, and 2019. Homes are taking a month, month and a half to sell,” he notes. “Between 2020 and 2024, a house wouldn’t last a week on the market. So, compared to that, it’s a big difference, but I just feel like it’s back to normal.”

In 2018, the median number of days on the market was 63, according to the Realtor.com March 2018 Monthly Housing Trends Report. As of the week ending Oct. 24, 2026, the median days on the market nationally was 64.

Miami comes in slightly slower, at a median of 83 days on the market as of October 2026. That number has decreased from both last month and last year, pushing the number toward the national norm.

Before: One of the homes that Branden Rivero prepared to flip through his company, Prop Hunters, which he founded 12 years ago. (Branden Rivero) After: The completed flip by Prop Hunters. They flipped 25 homes last year, Rivero says. Some years, it's 30 to 40 homes. (Branden Rivero)

These numbers are vastly different from the post-pandemic boom, when, in 2021, the typical home spent just 32 days on the market nationally.

A Realtor.com international report from June 2018 places Miami’s median listing price at $395,000, in line with October 2026’s national median listing price of $414,000. Again, Miami itself comes in a bit higher in 2026 numbers, with the median listing price sitting at $620,000.

One major difference between 2018 and 2026: mortgage rates. In 2018, the average 30-year fixed-rate mortgage was 4.7%. This week, the average 30-year mortgage rate is 7.2%.

While many aspects of the market are settling closer to pre-pandemic levels, mortgage rates are responsible for a major shift in affordability.

This is why Rivero has spent so much time getting to know the market and practicing patience.

The exterior of one of Rivero’s flipped homes. He says the most fulfilling aspect of working in real estate is seeing a home transform and become the perfect place for a family. (Branden Rivero)

He notes that, for new single-family homes, the permitting process is very slow. However, not many Miami developers are building single-family homes, so the wait is worth the payoff.

“In Miami, everything that’s getting built is townhouses and condos. So when you put a good product out there, a single-family home in a great neighborhood with great schools, it’s going to move,” Rivero says. “Patience is the key thing. I’d rather do one or two deals a year than jump into five, 10 bad ones.”

With over a decade under his belt, Rivero gets to focus on what he considers the most fulfilling aspect of working in real estate: seeing a home transform and become the perfect place for a family.

“To turn what most people see as trash into somewhat of a treasure, someone’s American dream where they’re going to raise their kids, is very satisfying,” says Rivero.
