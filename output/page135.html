Moultrie’s Edge 4 Solar took Gold in our annual Best Cellular Trail Camera field test. The camera, with the included solar panel, is priced right ($169.99), and I ran a pair on my Colorado deer dirt for two months. I added Moultrie’s $50 (aftermarket) Power Mag II battery pack, and with the solar panel, battery life never dipped below 80 percent. The cameras stood up to powerful wind, heavy rain, marble-ball-sized hail, and hellishly hot temperatures. Neither cell cam produced many false triggers, and both delivered crisp, clear images and video to my smartphone.

Last year, Moultrie’s Edge 3 Pro and Edge Solar came on the heels of the Edge 3. True to form, Moultrie follows suit in 2026. Dubbed the Edge 4 Solar, Moultrie’s latest cellular trail camera comes with a rechargeable lithium battery pack, an integrated solar panel, and an easy-aim mount. Pleased with the Edge 4 Solar’s performance, I was excited to give the Pro model an in-field test drive.

What Is The Difference Between The Edge 4 Solar And Edge 4 Pro Solar?

When I see “Pro” added to a product, I assume it has features that put it in a class above the standard model. That’s the case with the Edge 4 Solar and Edge 4 Pro Solar. The Pro captures high-resolution, high-detailed 4K video and 50MP photos. I was highly impressed with the 1080p video and 40MP photos the Edge 4 Solar captured, and I’m excited to see the enhanced detail.

According to Moultrie, “detail” drove the Edge 4 Solar Pro. Moultrie’s continued growth largely comes from its ability to meet consumer expectations. Consumers asked for more detail, and the Edge 4 Pro Solar is Moultrie’s response.

The Edge 4 Solar Pro trumps the Edge 4 Solar’s trigger speed by .1 second. That’s not much, but when a buck is hot on the heels of a doe, rapid trigger speed is important.

The internal interface makes setup quick and headache-free. A series of lights lets you know when the camera is ready to transmit images and videos. (Photo/Jace Bauserman)

Two Big Features Hunters Will Appreciate

Moultrie did an excellent job with its Edge 4 Series, and the Edge 4 Solar Pro looks to be the pinnacle. I’m a fan of Buck Shot. The Edge 4 Pro Solar uses Moultrie AI to detect big bucks. When the camera detects a big buck, it sends a tightly cropped, max-resolution image to your smartphone. This lets you immediately identify the buck and examine its headgear. And with the Pro model, Moultrie AI recognizes shooter bucks and switches the camera from photo mode to 4K video. Buck videos arrive immediately. Like the zoomed and cropped image, you don’t have to request or wait for the 4K video.

Setup And Deployment

The Edge 4 Pro Solar comes with everything you need. The solar panel is connected, and the rechargeable lithium battery pack is inserted. Log into your Moultrie account, add a new camera, scan the QR code, and start tinkering with the camera’s many settings. As I noted in my review of the Edge 4 Solar, I like the new internal panel. The twist-and-turn dial ensures a positive on/off, and Moultrie left the battery-life indicator, which I appreciate. Once powered on, all three lights (Not Connected-red, Connecting-yellow, Connected-green) turn solid. Next, the Connecting light flashes slowly, and after about 45 seconds, the green Connected light turns solid green. At that point, you can close the push-button door. The camera connects to the strongest cellular signal in the area and starts transmitting photos immediately. Best of all, perhaps, is that you don’t need to insert an SD card. I hate SD cards. They are a pain. I love Moultrie’s included storage.

In a partnership with onX, Edge 4 cameras are instantly pinned the second they are placed. I have multiple trail cameras—cellular and conventional—spread across the West and Midwest. Too often, I get in a hurry and forget to drop a camera pin when I’m using trail cameras. However, if I’m using Moultrie, I can forget, and the map-pinning technology saves me.

Built Tough

The Edge 4 Pro Solar has a quick trigger and captures game—even at night—at long distances.

My Edge 4 Solar withstood and continues to withstand rough conditions. We’ve had an extended monsoon season in my neck of the woods. Rain is one thing, but hail is another. All of my Edge 4 Solar cameras, including the new Pro, have zero damage to the housing or panels. Sadly, the Edge 4 Pro Solar did succumb to golf-ball-sized hail. The camera was facing straight into the storm. I’m not sure how many hailstones struck the photo lens cover, but one cracked it and some water got inside. The good news is that the camera is still functioning. The photos and video aren’t as clean and crisp as they were before the hail damage, but they are still solid. I called Moultrie and they are going to replace the camera. Moultrie has excellent customer service.

Moultrie Edge 4 cameras utilize waterproof IP67 waterproofing, and the housing and solar panel can take serious abuse. I was a little concerned that water would get into the removable solar-panel slot but that hasn’t happened and would have by now. If you want to remove the solar panel, push the unlock button on the back of the camera and pull the panel off.

Moultrie Edge 4 Solar Pro in the Field

Use the solar panel adjustments to ensure your camera gets as much sunlight as possible. (Photo/Jace Bauserman)

As of this writing, my Edge 4 Pro Solar has been in the field for two weeks. The hail damage occurred 11 days into my field test. Until that point, the camera’s images and video were excellent. The detail and color, especially the video, was incredible. I couldn’t tell much difference between the 40MP and 50MP photos, but I could absolutely see the difference between the 1080p and 4K video.

After two weeks in the field, battery life is 94 percent. That percentage dips a bit when the panel isn’t getting sunlight, but as soon as the panel gets sun, it charges the camera quickly. I like the direct-to-housing panel attachment. The system eliminates wires that tangle and hang up everywhere.  Be sure to take advantage of the twist-and-lock solar panel direction and angle. Both are adjustable, allowing you to set your panel to absorb as much sunlight as possible.

I’ve had zero image delivery glitches; deer and other game animals are centered in the frame, and Moultrie lowballs the camera’s max detection range. Like the Edge 4 Pro, the Edge 4 Solar Pro captured deer beyond 100 feet. Moultrie brands the Edge Pro Solar trigger speed at .3 seconds, and my testing agrees. I set up an Edge 4 Solar and Edge 4 Pro Solar in my backyard, and the Pro proved quicker to trigger when I ran past the camera at 50 feet.

Like the Edge 4 Solar, the Pro model improves Moultrie’s nighttime image capture game. Moultrie used enhanced No Glow IR LEDs to illuminate every image evenly. The result is clearer edge-to-edge images. The only issue I’ve noticed the more I’ve tested Moultrie’s Edge 4 Solar models is the flash. The IR is almost too powerful. If the subject is close to the camera, the image gets a bit blown out. However, I’ve yet to have a deer spook from the Edge 4 Pro Solar, and I’ve run the camera on open ag fields and tight trail crossings.

Final Thoughts

Moultrie has once again raised the bar with the Edge 4 Solar and Edge 4 Pro Solar, both of which have proven to be exceptional in the field. The Edge 4 Solar, awarded Gold in our annual Best Cellular Trail Camera test, has consistently impressed with its performance, durability, and affordability. At $169.99, it offers outstanding value, especially when paired with the aftermarket Power Mag II battery pack. The solar panel and robust battery life combine to deliver a seamless experience in demanding conditions, from heavy rainfall to extreme heat.

Moultrie’s IR LED’s capture amazing photos and video, don’t spook game, and provide excellent edge-to-edge nighttime image quality. (Photo/Jace Bauserman)

The Edge 4 Pro Solar takes things a step further, delivering enhanced detail and high-resolution 4K video, perfect for capturing all the goings-on in your deer woods. Moultrie’s focus on user feedback has highlighted the importance of detailed imaging, and the Pro model reflects their commitment to customer satisfaction.

For setup, the straightforward interface and reliable battery performance let users maximize their time in the field. Both models have proven resilient in harsh weather, reinforcing Moultrie’s reputation for durability. If you’re looking for a dependable trail camera that excels in both performance and image quality, the Edge 4 Solar series is undoubtedly worth considering, and if you want Moultire’s cream of the crop, then go with the Edge 4 Pro Solar.

Organizations running regulated and security-sensitive workloads need trusted evidence of which authenticated users performed management operations on their hardware security modules (HSMs), what actions they took, and when. Today, we are announcing Hardware Audit Logging for Azure Cloud HSM, which collects audit records generated by the HSM itself for supported management operations. Security, compliance, and operations teams can use this hardware-originated evidence for monitoring, compliance reporting, and forensic investigations.

Learn more about Azure Cloud HSM

Why hardware-generated audit evidence matters

HSMs protect some of an organization’s most sensitive cryptographic assets. The security of those assets depends not only on how keys are generated and protected, but also on how the HSM is configured and administered over time. Authentication events, user changes, policy updates, backup operations, and key lifecycle activity can all be important when teams investigate an incident, validate operational controls, or prepare evidence for an audit.

Traditional platform and application logs remain valuable, but regulated organizations often want evidence that originates as close as possible to the protected cryptographic boundary. By sourcing audit information directly from the HSM, Azure Cloud HSM provides records tied to the device performing the protected operations. This gives customers an additional layer of evidence for understanding administrative activity and validating the records incorporated into their monitoring and compliance processes.

Monitor HSM management operations with hardware-generated audit logs

Hardware Audit Logging captures a broad range of HSM management activities, including:

  • User authentication and session events.
  • User and administrator management.
  • Policy and configuration changes.
  • Key lifecycle operations.
  • Backup and restore activities.

Together, these events help organizations build a more complete view of how the HSM is being operated. Security teams can examine authentication and administrative activity, compliance teams can use the records as evidence for control assessments, and operations teams can investigate configuration or lifecycle changes that may affect a protected workload.

Independent, end-to-end audit verification

Customers can access human-readable audit records, the corresponding HSM-signed binary audit data, signatures, and HSM certificates needed to independently verify the authenticity and integrity of signed audit batches. This customer-controlled verification path helps connect the records used in monitoring and reporting back to the signed evidence produced by the HSM.

This approach is important because auditability is not only about collecting readable events. It is also about establishing confidence in where the records came from and whether the signed audit batches remain intact.

Maintain consistent audit coverage for supported management activity

Audit logging is enabled as an immutable HSM partition policy. This design helps maintain consistent coverage of supported management activity and reduces the risk that logging could be disabled as part of routine HSM administration. For organizations that separate cryptographic administration, security monitoring, and compliance responsibilities, continuous hardware-originated logging can support clearer oversight across those functions.

The capability is designed to complement an organization’s existing governance model. Customers remain responsible for configuring access to their monitoring environment, defining retention requirements, establishing alerting and review processes, and determining how audit evidence should be used within their control framework. Hardware Audit Logging provides the HSM-originated records and verification materials that those processes can use.

Integrate HSM audit data into security operations

Hardware Audit Logging is designed for organizations with stringent compliance and security requirements, including financial services, payment processing, government, critical infrastructure, and other highly regulated industries. These organizations may need to demonstrate that sensitive cryptographic systems are administered through controlled processes and that important management activities can be reviewed after the fact.

Audit events are delivered to Azure Monitor Log Analytics, enabling customers to incorporate HSM management activity into broader monitoring, investigation, and security operations workflows, including Microsoft Sentinel. Centralizing these events alongside other relevant operational and security data can help teams correlate HSM activity with events elsewhere in the environment, apply queries and analytics, and support established investigation and reporting workflows.

For example, a security operations team may want to review unexpected authentication activity, an administrator change, or a policy update in the context of related Azure activity. A compliance team may need evidence that privileged management operations were logged during a defined assessment period. An incident response team may need to reconstruct the sequence of HSM management events surrounding a security investigation. Hardware-originated audit records provide an important source for each of these scenarios.

Strengthen HSM auditability with verifiable evidence

Azure Cloud HSM provides more than log visibility. Readable audit records support day-to-day operations. It also provides HSM-signed binary evidence, signatures, certificates, and verification utilities behind those records, which lead to an independent way to trace each record back to the HSM that signed it.

It also allows for keeping that evidence outside the logging workflow and checking it cryptographically whenever an audit or investigation calls for it, instead of relying only on delivered text records. Teams get an audit trail they can verify for the workloads that matter most.

What does Azure Cloud HSM Hardware Audit Logging provide

It provides hardware-generated records for supported HSM management operations, sends audit events to Azure Monitor Log Analytics, and gives customers signed binary audit data and verification materials for independent validation.

With 20mm F2.4 and 40mm F2.2 options, the new Swift series gives Sony E, Fujifilm X, and L-mount users more budget-friendly choices.

Sirui has announced a new series of prime, autofocus lenses. The Swift lineup includes two focal lengths at launch – 20mm F2.4 and 40mm F2.2 – both of which are compact, lightweight offerings that are available in L, E and X mounts for just $149.

Unlike Viltrox’s similarly affordable 20mm F2.8 and 40mm F2.5 Air lenses, both Swift primes include useful on-lens controls. That includes a clicked aperture ring, a manual focus ring, a customizable Fn button and an AF/MF switch.

Sirui

Both of the Swift lenses are compatible with 58mm filters. A stepper motor drives autofocus in each lens, with Sirui promising minimal focus breathing. They each feature a nine-bladed aperture diaphragm. The company also says the lenses are weather-resistant, with protection against dust and splashes.

The 20mm F2.4 lens features an 11-element in 9-group optical design, with 2 aspherical, 2 ED and 2 HRI elements. It can focus as close as 0.2m (0.65′). Sirui doesn’t provide weights or measurements for the X-mount model, but the L-mount version weighs 212g (7.5oz), while the E-mount comes in at 207g (7.3oz). They measure 60.3mm (2.4″) and 62.3mm (2.5″) long, respectively.

Sirui

The 40mm F2.2 lens features a slightly different optical design, with 11 elements in 8 groups. That includes one aspherical lens and two ED elements. Its minimum focusing distance is 0.4m (1.3′). Its size and weight are very similar to the 20mm lens, with the E-mount version weighing 210g (7.4oz) and measuring 62.3mm (2.5″). The L-mount model weighs 215g (7.6oz) and measures 60mm (2.36″).

The new Swift 20mm F2.4 and 40mm F2.2 are available to pre-order now for $149.

Shape the ends of the legs

Using a compass with a pencil, draw 1-1/2-inch diameter half-circles at the bottom of each leg (B) to match the 1/2-inch width of the leg.

Use a disc or belt sander with a coarse-grit sandpaper (80 or 100-grit) to shape the ends of the legs by sanding up to the marked pencil lines for your arch. Once you have sanded to the line, work your way up to 220-grit sandpaper for a smoother finish.


Drill holes in the legs and Guide rails

Use a drill press to drill:

  • Hole 1: Drill a 1/4-inch hole through each leg, in the middle of the 14-inch-long leg, and centered from side to side in all four legs (B).
  • Hole 2: With the first hole drilled, separate the legs (B) into two inside and two outside legs. On the outside legs, use a Forstner bit to drill 5/8-inch x 1/2-inch-deep holes, centered 3/4-inch from the top, on the side of the legs that will face inward. Then, drill a 1/4-inch hole all the way through the leg from the center of the 5/8-inch hole. The 5/8-inch x 1/2-inch-deep holes countersink the nylon lock nut and allow the legs to collapse into each other without obstruction.
  • Hole 3: On the inside legs (B), use a Forstner bit to drill a 3/4-inch wide holes through the legs. Center these 1-1/2-inch down from the top of the leg and centered side-to-side.
  • Hole 4: On the inside legs (B), use a Forstner bit to drill a 1/2-inch wide hole through the legs. Center these 2-3/4-inch up from the bottom of the leg and centered side-to-side.
  • Hole 5: On the guide rails (C), drill a 1/4-inch hole through the guide rail, 1-inch in from the side and centered top-to-bottom.


Round over the edges

Using a router table or a router equipped with a 1/4-inch round-over bit, round over all the edges of the step (A) and all four legs (B). Be sure to use clamps to hold the pieces while making your passes with the router.

With all the parts machined, sand all the components to 220 grit and apply a couple of coats of a wood finish of your choice. Allow the finish to dry before moving on to assembly.


Connect the inside legs

Spread wood glue in hole 3 at the top of each inside leg (B). Insert the ends of the 3/4-in. dowel cross brace (D) and tap the legs on with a rubber mallet until D is fully seated.

Set the 1-1/4-in.-square cross brace (E) between the bottoms of the legs, with its bottom edge 2-1/8 in. up from the bottom of the legs. To keep the legs parallel while you drill, clamp a scrap the same length as the cross brace (E) between them at mid-height. Check for square.

Using hole 4 in each leg as a guide, drill a 1/2-in. hole about 1 in. deep into the center of each end of the cross brace (E). Glue 1/2-in. dowels, cut longer than the holes are deep, and drive them through the legs with a mallet until they bottom out in the cross brace (E).

Lock each of the four joints with a single brad nail through the leg into the dowel. Trim the protruding ends of all the dowels flush with a pull saw.


Attach the guide rails

Flip the top step (A) upside down. Position the guide rails (C) 1/2-in. in from each side and centered front to back, leaving 1-1/2-in. at the front and back. Trace around each rail.

Drill two 1/4-in. holes, 3/4-in. deep, centered on each outline. Keep the bit plumb when drilling (or drill at the drill press).

Insert dowel centers in the holes. Align each rail with its outline and press down to mark the hole locations. Drill matching 1/4-in. holes in the rails, about 7/8-in. deep. That’s slightly deeper than the dowel needs, which leaves room for glue.

Glue and insert 1/4-in x 1-1/2-in. dowels and secure the guide rails (C) to the step (A).


Connect the outside and inside legs

Thread 1/4-inch x 3-1/2-inch carriage bolts through hole 1 in the center of the legs to connect an outside leg to the outside of each of the connected inside legs. Put a washer between each pair of legs and secure with a nylon lock nut on the inside of the inside legs.

Connect the legs and step

Connect the two outside legs (B) to the sides of the guide rails (C) by threading 1/4-inch x 2-1/2-inch pan head screws through hole 5 in the guide rails and through hole 2 on the outside legs. Use a washer on the outside of the guide rails and pliers to hold the nylon lock nuts while you tighten them into the 5/8-inch countersunk holes.

Attach upholstery strap

The strap acts as a stop, limiting how far the legs can open. Wrap one end of an upholstery strap around the middle of the top cross brace (D) and staple it in place with a narrow-crown stapler. Open the stool until the seat sits level, then pull the strap snug and staple the other end centered on the underside of the top step (A). Use several 1/2-in. staples at each end so the strap holds securely.


FAQ

How wide should a step stool be?

A step stool should be about 11 to 16-in. wide to fit both feet comfortably side by side. For a portable or compact version, stay toward the narrow end of that range.

How tall should a step stool be?

A single-step stool should be 6 to 9-in. tall. That’s a good height for reaching into a truck bed, helping kids reach a tall bathroom sink, or getting to the top pantry shelves.

How did your drone collection collide with your architecture interest?

Walt Disney Concert Hall.

Sony a7R VI with a Canon TS 17mm lens.Andrew Keithly

“My grandfather gave me my first camera for my 15th birthday. It was a Sony A77, back in the old A-mount days. He loved photography and wanted to encourage me in it. I photographed my dog, flowers, my family, landscapes and anything else that caught my eye.

In 2017, I picked up a DJI Phantom 3 Advanced, the first of many drones, and a year later I was occasionally shooting aerials for real estate agents. I’d planned a career in drone surveying and LiDAR because I didn’t think I could make a living taking photos, even though I was interested in that.

In the summer of 2020, a friend of a friend of a friend who knew I flew drones asked me to photograph a resort in the Smoky Mountains, and I realized right then this might actually be a career. I launched a real estate photography company that summer, but agents were hard to work with and weren’t willing to pay for my slower, more careful style. So I reached out to a few architects, and they preferred exactly that approach and gave me the freedom to experiment.

By January 2021, I had completely made the switch to architectural photography.”

How has your photography changed since you started?

Suiden Terrasse.

“Tilt-shift lenses are extremely useful for architectural photography, and these days I own three. They let you control perspective from a fixed position. Some shots simply would be impossible without them. But I no longer see them as what defines me as an architectural photographer, because that becomes a crutch.

Sometimes my humble Sony 24-105mm lens frees me from the constraints of “making” a shot and lets me find the true vision and meaning behind the project.

When you slow down and take in the light and structure from every angle, you start to see what only you can notice.

Slowing down matters even more, which I’ve noticed several of your other spotlight guests have said. Three of the 20 images in the printed portfolio I give prospective clients came from sitting in a single spot for nearly an hour. When you slow down and take in the light and structure from every angle, you start to see what only you can notice, instead of the “good” images that every other photographer visiting the same spot will also shoot. Your own perspective can shine through, and your work starts to take on a flavor of its own.”

Even so, on the gear side, moving from an old APS-C camera to the Sony A7 III drastically improved image quality and gave me far more flexibility. The sensor in the A7 III still holds up today and reigns supreme in low light (The R3 has noise reduction on raw files).”
