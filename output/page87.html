we drove volvo’s electric EX60 to russel wright’s modernist woodland home at manitoga

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

an autumn drive into the hudson highlands

 

Volvo‘s new all-electric EX60 took us from Manhattan to Manitoga, the woodland home of American industrial designer Russel Wright in Garrison, New York. The drive north brought us out of the city and into the tree-lined roads of the Hudson Valley, glowing with autumn color, where the Swedish automaker had organized a design experience around its newest electric SUV.

 

During the mid-twentieth century, Wright spent decades transforming a former quarry into a 75-acre woodland estate. The experimental and iconic modernist home incorporates the surrounding forest into its architecture, with the resulting space feeling like an architect’s fairytale.

 

Volvo’s designers have been exploring similar ideas for the EX60, studying natural materials and the changing colors of the landscape to shape the experience inside the car.

images courtesy Volvo

 

 

the all-electric ex60

 

The EX60 translates the familiar proportions of Volvo’s popular XC60 into a fully electric SUV. Its low front end and gently sloping roofline contribute to a drag coefficient of just 0.26, while beneath the body sits the company’s new SPA3 electric architecture.

 

The platform introduces megacasting and cell-to-body battery integration to reduce weight and improve efficiency. For the U.S. market, the P10 AWD offers 503 horsepower and up to 330 miles of EPA-estimated range, with charging from 10 to 80 percent taking as little as 16 minutes under optimal conditions.

the EX60 builds on the familiar XC60 with a dedicated electric platform and more aerodynamic proportions

 

 

material and color choices

 

Inside, the EX60 introduces a 15-inch curved OLED center display with Google Gemini integrated into the infotainment system. The cabin has a fairly simple layout, with tactile finishes and natural textures softening the presence of its digital technology.

 

Once we arrived at Manitoga, we met Sara Erichsen Susnjar, Volvo Cars’ Senior Color & Material Design Manager, who has spent twenty years with the company and oversees color and material design for its midsize models.

 

Presenting a palette of materials, Susnjar drew our attention to the EX60’s available tailored wool-blend upholstery. Wool, she explained, naturally responds to body temperature, remaining comfortable through changes in weather. Its breathability is especially useful when returning to a parked car on a hot day. The material comes with challenges around production costs and customer expectations, although its practical qualities make a compelling case. ‘I think it’s a little bit brave, but also it has a function,‘ she told us.

the EX60’s interior pairs digital technology with natural textures and Scandinavian restraint

 

 

The conversation soon turned to color. Susnjar explained that developing a new automotive finish can involve an entire year of evaluation, with painted vehicles taken outdoors in different seasons to observe how their colors respond to changing light.

 

‘We have to check all the light,‘ she explained. This means moving between controlled lighting environments and outdoor settings, from warm summer sunlight to the colder light of winter.

the EX60 achieves a drag coefficient of 0.26, with efficiency influencing its exterior design

 

 

russel wright’s manitoga is a house shaped by the forest

 

There was an interesting historical parallel waiting inside Manitoga. Wright approached color as something that should respond to the seasons, changing elements of his home’s interior throughout the year. Warmer colors appeared during winter, while cooler tones were introduced for summer. Best known for his American Modern dinnerware, introduced in 1939, Wright had long been interested in how design could make domestic environments more comfortable and enjoyable.

 

In 1942, Wright and his wife Mary purchased the former quarry and logging property in Garrison. The estate would eventually become home to Dragon Rock, completed in 1961 with architect David Leavitt. Touring the house, we found interiors filled with unusual handmade experiments.

 

Large boulders extend into the rooms, while actual pine needles are embedded in green plaster walls. Wright even pressed butterfly wings or wildflowers between sheets of translucent plastic to creating decorative screens from materials gathered in nature.

exterior colors are tested across seasons to study their response to changing natural light

 

 

immersed in wild nature

 

Outside, a waterfall drops into the former quarry pool, while trails wind through the surrounding woodland. Our guide explained how Wright encouraged native moss to spread by removing competing plants to gradually transforming the forest floor.

 

Today, Manitoga is a National Historic Landmark, designated in 2006, and is maintained by the nonprofit Russel Wright Design Center, which continues to preserve and restore the house and landscape.

Sara Erichsen Susnjar discusses the EX60’s wool-blend upholstery and its temperature-regulating qualities

 

bringing the forest into the electric cabin

 

One of the EX60’s more unusual interior features is its Forest Light ambient lighting system, which projects a pattern of leaves onto the rear seats and center console. Susnjar described the feature during our visit, explaining that the design team wanted to bring some of the experience of nature into the vehicle. As the presentation took place during the day, we could only get a partial impression of the effect, which is designed to be experienced in darkness.

 

After exploring Dragon Rock, Wright experimented extensively with lighting. Large expanses of glass bring the changing woodland into view from almost every room. His home responds directly to its surroundings, with natural materials appearing throughout the architecture. Volvo takes a different approach, using contemporary technology to introduce an impression of the forest into the enclosed space of a car.

the EX60 introduces a new digital interface with a 15-inch curved OLED display

sunlight filtering through leaves inspired Volvo’s Forest Light ambient lighting system

Wow: American Airlines Pilots Warned Not To Eat Crew Meal Tiramisu Because It Contains Alcohol

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

American Airlines pilots have been warned to skip the tiramisu in some crew meals out of Phoenix because it contains alcohol. That is an awkward ingredient to find in a meal provided to people who are supposed to be flying airplanes…

American Airlines Crew Meal Tiramisu Prompts Alcohol Warning

JonNYC shared a warning attributed to the Allied Pilots Association’s Aeromedical–HIMS Committee concerning meals supplied by American’s contracted caterer in Phoenix:

“The contracted catering provider has included tiramisu containing alcohol on some crew meal trays. Because the dessert is not cooked after the alcohol is added, alcohol may remain in the finished product.

“We recommend that no crew member consume this dessert.

“APA cannot determine the amount of alcohol contained in an individual serving or what effect consumption could have if a crewmember were subsequently required to undergo DOT alcohol testing.”

AA (get it?)

"PHX Crew Meal Alert – Tiramisu Containing Alcohol

The APA Aeromedical–HIMS Committee has been made aware of an issue affecting crews based in or transiting PHX.

The contracted catering provider has included tiramisu containing alcohol on some crew meal trays.…

— JonNYC (@xJonNYC) October 30, 2026

 

The notice says the committee is coordinating with the Phoenix Flight Office to have the dessert removed and replaced. In the meantime, crews are advised to accept an incomplete meal rather than eat it.

Pilots who have already consumed the tiramisu and have concerns about their fitness to fly are instructed to contact their union domicile representatives immediately. The warning expressly tells them not to let schedule pressure influence that decision.

A Catering Mistake That Creates An Unnecessary Problem

To be clear, the notice does not establish that a serving would cause impairment or a failed alcohol test. The committee says it does not know the alcohol content or the potential testing consequences. But crews should not have to evaluate that uncertainty while eating an airline-provided meal!

The FAA explains that a confirmed alcohol concentration of 0.02 to 0.039 requires temporary removal from safety-sensitive duties, even though that result is not itself a federal testing violation. A confirmed result of 0.04 or greater carries more serious consequences.

CONCLUSION

American’s Phoenix caterer apparently supplied an alcohol-containing dessert in crew meals, prompting a union warning to leave it untouched while a replacement is arranged. I like tiramisu, but this is one setting where the alcohol-free version is the obvious choice. Pilots have enough to think about without wondering whether their crew meal could complicate an alcohol test…

The fight over border construction in Big Bend National Park has reached the U.S. Supreme Court after the Trump administration asked justices to override a federal judge who had ordered work to halt.

On October 29, plaintiffs in an ongoing lawsuit over the border construction, including the Center for Biological Diversity, Friends of the Ruidosa Church, and a local Rio Grande river guide, urged the justices to keep construction on hold. They warned in a 50-page filing that roads and barriers could permanently scar the fragile Chihuahuan Desert ecosystem surrounding Big Bend National Park, threaten river access and local livelihoods, and damage historic sites. The document cited earlier testimony from local residents, environmental researchers, and other officials who oppose the construction.

An excavator and bulldozer sit near Santa Elena Canyon in Big Bend National Park, Texas, on August 13, 2026. The Trump administration is asking the Supreme Court to allow border construction to resume after a federal judge halted the work. (Photo: FangXiaNuo/Getty Images)

“If this actually occurred, I think it would destroy the tourism that Terlingua, Presidio, Marfa… all those towns depend on,” Billy Miller, a local rafting guide who takes tourists on sections of the Rio Grande river, said in the document. “I don’t think they’ll have an experience anymore. I think it will be a tour of the military-industrial complex, not a tour of the wilderness.”

The Supreme Court’s decision could determine whether construction resumes across a vast stretch of West Texas that includes Big Bend National Park, Big Bend Ranch State Park, and protected stretches of the Rio Grande.

The construction plans vary across the region. Inside Big Bend National Park, Customs and Border Protection has proposed access roads, surveillance technology, and roughly 17 miles of smaller vehicle barriers designed to stop cars and trucks from crossing. Elsewhere in the Big Bend Border Patrol sector, the government plans to build 30-foot walls.

Security Claims Meet Judicial Pushback

The question at the heart of the lawsuit is whether the government can bypass environmental reviews to build in Big Bend.

U.S. District Judge Kathleen Cardone halted construction on October 2. She noted that Border Patrol had recorded more than 89,000 apprehensions across the 517-mile sector over five years, yet Big Bend had the lowest apprehension rate per mile of any sector along the southern border.

Towering 1,500-foot cliffs, the Chisos Mountains, and waterless desert expanses make travel through Big Bend exceptionally punishing, leading Customs and Border Protection to acknowledge in May that the topography of the area naturally deters foot traffic across the border.

On October 21, the Trump administration pushed back, arguing in a filing sent to the Supreme Court that delaying construction imperils national security.

“The Sinaloa and Juarez cartels operate south of this sector across the Mexico border and seek to exploit any vulnerabilities in United States border protection to traffic humans, drugs, and weapons,” Justice Department attorneys wrote in the filing, saying that leaving a “300-plus-mile gap in border defenses” could attract criminal organizations.

In an October 30 reply, the administration maintained that the judge lacked authority to halt construction and reiterated that the injunction threatened border security.

Centuries of Impact on Land and Lives

Opponents argue that allowing work to resume before the lawsuit is resolved could cause irreversible environmental damage.

Jeffrey Renfrow, an ecologist and director of Rio Bravo Restoration who has worked in the Big Bend region for more than two decades, testified that the damage already done could outlive generations.

“My opinion is that there’s been significant harm to hydrologic function, vegetation and soils and that that harm will probably persist for decades if not centuries, and, in some cases, never recover,” Renfrow told the court.

Bulldozers and other heavy machinery can crush biological soil crusts, delicate living mats that bind the desert floor together and prevent erosion. According to a U.S. Forest Service report, some organisms in these crusts can take around 100 years to regrow.

Local rancher Bill Guerra-Addington testified that construction traffic had already increased wildlife deaths along access roads. He also described the emotional toll of watching the landscape around his home change.

“I’ve cried a river of tears seeing what they’ve done to my home,” he told the court. “I just see this kind of like a dystopian nightmare in my very home, a place that I hold sacred.”

The administration had previously used special legal exemptions to begin construction without completing certain environmental reviews and meeting other legal requirements. Cardone’s order blocked further work under those exemptions.

If the Supreme Court lifts her order, construction could resume while the lawsuit continues. If the justices leave it in place, the government must comply with applicable laws before restarting the disputed work.

Anthropic VS code extension on Cursor backdoored for nearly 12 hours

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

An official extension for Anthropic’s Claude model was backdoored and threatened as many as 5,000 developers' machines, multiple sources reported Friday. Anthropic so far has yet to warn customers of the incident.

The compromised package was a VS Code extension available in the marketplace of Cursor, a maker of AI-assisted developer tools, the company said Friday. The malicious version, 2.1.313, subsequently affected “at most 5,000 users,” presumably when they either downloaded it or their systems automatically fetched and installed it. Anthropic pushed version 2.1.314 several hours after the previous version became available on Cursor, according to independent researcher Kevin Beaumont. Both Beaumont and the CTO and co-founder of security firm Phorian said that version 2.1.313 was distributed through Anthropic’s official account on Cursor. So far, neither Cursor nor Anthropic has explained how the attackers gained control of the account.

In search of cloud credentials

“It was a supply-chain compromise where the attacker gained access to the official distribution channel,” Phorian CTO and co-founder Luke Roberts told Ars. “The malicious version 2.1.313 was posted to the official Anthropic account, effectively superseding 2.1.312. Extensions auto-update in Cursor by default, but users can configure this in settings.” Phorian has more details here.
