Nika Grace on finding remains in deserts and writing a serial killer novel

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

It is a truth universally acknowledged that if you hike across the desert, intently looking at the ground, you will eventually find a dead body.

Let me back up a step to explain why an otherwise mentally healthy thirty-something woman would be hiking across the remote desert for so many thousands of trailless miles. It was because of #VanLife, actually, though this occurred before I understood how to use a hashtag (do I yet understand how to use a hashtag? Don’t answer that.) And, of course, I was never quite affluent enough to purchase a van.

The year was 2010. My husband and I had gotten married, acquired a mortgage, a master’s degree, and a matched set of Real Actual Careers. Reader, I lasted six months.

After that, I decided that business casual blouses and working for the weekends wasn’t for me, and we went on the run. We moved into a Toyota Tacoma truck nearly as old as we were, and worked seasonally to pay for enough ramen to keep rock climbing until we were too old and too square to get up the cliffs anymore.

After a short stint on the ice in Antarctica—a tale for another time—we landed in the Mojave Desert, tracking desert tortoises across the desiccated landscape. When I first got the job, I thought the stories of other tortoise bios finding dead bodies were some kind of urban myth. A form of hazing the new girl that was probably blown out of proportion thanks to mis-identified coyote bones and having too much time to think. Until we found a finger.

A woman’s finger; painted fingernail. No blood. No body.

It was found by a cheerful ex-raft-guide from Flagstaff, one crew over from where I was walking survey lines in the desert just outside of Las Vegas.

The chills that went up my spine followed me all the way home to where I was camping in the—unlockable—camper shell of my Toyota Tacoma, the dusty wind howling around its thin metal shell all night.

Listen, I wanted to make money for road tripping as much as the next girl, but exactly where did that finger come from? And where was the rest of that woman’s body? And why—where—but—didn’t they have police down here?

The questions were more plentiful than the answers. As the years in the Mojave cropped up and the body count rose along them, we never did hear the stories behind any of the human remains we turned in. Not the multiple skulls, not the drifter we found outside of Nipton with his uncashed welfare check in his pocket, not the “suicide” that I still swear was a very thinly-veiled mob hit (details redacted for the safety of the bios who found it). There were so many bodies found by tortoise biologists that eventually Outside Magazine did a feature on the phenomenon, though they didn’t cover half the tales I could have told.

One that stuck with me was three graves, complete with crosses and mounded dirt, armored with river-smoothed stones. We found them near an abandoned compound that used to house a meth lab, and a cult. Since five girls had gone missing in this area the local police in this case actually sent a crew out to exhume the sites. They dug them all the way to bedrock and found nothing, which always tickled my imagination.

I understand setting up crosses for a memorial, perhaps, but why go to the trouble to mound the dirt? Why lug so many stones in such a sweaty climate if you weren’t faking the graves for someone else’s benefit? I couldn’t stop thinking about it, and I never got answers, so I ended up writing a flash fiction story to give myself the resolution that reality was reluctant to allow me.

Picture courtesy of Pixabay, Dat 7.

The sluggishness of the authorities when it came to following our GPS coordinates to the corpses gave me a healthy respect for how much easier it was to get away with murder than I had previously thought, at least in the Mojave. The desert always felt like a place real adults had forgotten. What with its abandoned and half-burnt trailers jutting up alongside the sand-drifted highways and the graffiti laced bones of defunct water parks.

That’s why when I decided to write a book about a female serial killer, I set her story in the Phoenix desert. Because I wanted the metaphorical resonance of her rising from the ashes of her past, sure. But mostly because I wanted somewhere to hide the bodies.

I started writing this book in a desert, of course. When I was working a job outside of Barstow fence monitoring. Just me walking miles of fence to look for distressed tortoises not used to having their habitat chopped up by the dividing lines of men. On a day when I’d just written one of the first chapters from my serial killer’s POV, I found two murder weapons.

One assault rifle, one hunting rifle. Both rusty from seasons out in the elements, despite being tossed less than ten feet off a major highway. From author’s personal photo collection.

Do I know they were murder weapons? Of course not. When I called the police, they said it was county jurisdiction and hung up on me. The two young county sheriffs who eventually responded had to borrow a set of my latex gloves for tortoise handling just to take the guns into evidence, and never followed up with me about the results of the investigation, if they even did one.

But guns like these cost hundreds to thousands of dollars. Why toss them out the window unless you had to?

I never learned who the guns belonged to, or how they got there. What I did learn was that if you want to get away with murder, you better do it in the desert.

Alysandra Dutton on Writing Historical Noir and Investigating Ancient Roman Crimes

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

In 753 BCE something happened to the Sabine Women. Romulus, fresh off of founding Rome, could not make marriages for himself and his men, who were considered little more than a gang of particularly nasty bandits. He opted for stealing his brides from neighboring Sabinum during a festival, cementing Rome’s legacy in their wombs.

And when I was fourteen, sitting in the audience of a local high school production of Seven Brides for Seven Brothers, a romantic comedy retelling of the Abduction of the Sabine Women, listening to the cast sing jovially about how those women kissed and cried all over that Roman countryside, a feeling welled in me that I would later associate with the need to clear my desk and get writing: indignation. Or maybe, spite.

Ever since, the Sabine Women have haunted me, much the way true crime stories haunt my generation of American women; their pain felt cautionary, their story morbidly compelling, as they showed up again and again in my drafting notebooks. Sure, the crime was solved. We know who took the Sabine Women, and we know why.

But in time I realized I wasn’t able to let them go because the injustice was ongoing; historians writing seven hundred years after the founding of Rome claimed the women were happy to be kidnapped and in love with their captors, marking perhaps one of the earliest incel arguments that women secretly desire to be overpowered, that rape is not that violating after all. And until the record said otherwise, I couldn’t not write.

The Stolen Women came together for me, probably not surprisingly, as I discovered an interest in detective literature. I already felt like an investigator, cynical and working against the grain for my victims; it made sense to take years of notes and half-started stories and filter them through classic noir conventions.

My favorite noirs are about fighting—often futilely—against the system. That seemed to me a fitting framework by which to see a young Sabine woman, pitted against Rome. As contemporary readers, we know what’s coming—we know Rome wins.

But in noir, the the tragic end—often as foregone a conclusion as Rome—is an aside. The story is about what drives a person to leave their well-earned cynicism at the door and fight anyway. I wanted to give the Sabine that story.

Soon, Safinia appeared on the page. My classic, hard-boiled detective; at first, all she wants is to be left alone to put food on the table and protect her sisters. But then, the femme fatale, a woman in danger, in tears, draws her into the twisting, atmospherically moody world of a mystery.

The disappearance of an ex-lover, answers that lead to more questions, and a sidekick classical historians will immediately recognize as keeping vital secrets—it was all there. I may as well have given her a pipe to smoke on as slats of light shine through her dusty office (she does, at one point, smoke hemp in the fashion of the ancient world—one of my favorite scenes, and a send-up I couldn’t resist). But what would happen when her investigation led to Rome?

As Safinia descended further into her obsessive pursuit of mysteriously disappeared women, I also began to feel obsessive, my research notes looking more like a murder board every day. By this point, I was living in Rome, as a member of the awards-holder cohort at the British School at Rome. Before this moment, I had written a lot about trauma; my history necessitated it. Writing about violence, about violation, was my way of making sense of my own past.

In Safinia, I found cathartic satisfaction in her quest for answers and subsequent fight for survival. But what began as an intimate story about gendered violence began to take a larger shape during my days at the BSR. Suddenly, I realized I was writing a book not only about violence—but about violence in Rome. And the qualifier mattered.

Any contemporary archeologist will tell you that the study of Rome is also the study of fascism. I’ve written before about how it is impossible to extract fascism from its birthplace, and the reason for this is practical and material: fascism mucked up a lot of crime scenes in its day. Or, I should say, excavations.

Mussolini’s thirst to associate himself with the image of the Roman Empire meant large swaths of history were greedily uncovered, poorly documented, and then left to rot. Far from the conservation practices of archeological excavations today, even the Colosseum Mussolini was determined to see from his balcony, tearing down a historic medieval neighborhood to achieve this, was neglected by the 1970s. It was a known site for drug-users and criminals (and underground cinemas and raves, but that’s a story for another day), crumbling dangerously around its edges.

I note the irony in an essay at the back of The Stolen Women: Mussolini wanted to see the colosseum Emperor Domitian built so that he could feel like a God, too. But Emperor Domitian, my research found, proudly displayed an impossible to verify family tree that tied him to the original kidnapped Sabine Women, eight hundred years before his reign. This was the brag of important men in Rome, their Sabine ancestors promoted from victims to propaganda that proves a family’s interests in the Eternal City.

From here, my tact had to change; a moody, forget-it-Jake-it’s-Chinatown ending had no place in this world. I’d been so consumed by the crime of kidnapping, and the futility of Safinia’s hopes for solving it, that I was missing the crime of history, of rhetoric; not of how you tell the story of an Empire, but of how you justify it.

Ancient historians edged around the Sabine Women, acknowledging the crime but inventing, in the same breath, an ending that renders their kidnapping palatable. Titus Livius—Livy—claims in his histories that the women kept their freedom of choice and chose to stay with their Roman captors; Dionysius of Halicarnassus praises Romulus’ bold foreign policy in his decision to kidnap them in the first place. And Mussolini, in Italy’s fascist era, rips the protective shell off of history and lays it bare and eroded, absent any context that doesn’t spell empire.

In these days of writing and drafting, Safinia’s task looked enormous, insurmountable. But if nothing else, she taught me how to move doggedly, determinedly through history; to trust the way the Earth churns up new answers and new mysteries. They will never have all of you, a historian tells her at a low moment, and he’s right.

The truth has a way of breaking through.

How ADHD Science Fails Women in Menopause

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Menopause aggravates and elevates ADHD symptoms for a staggering 97.5 percent of women with the condition, according to a survey of 600 women cited in the Journal of Psychiatric Research.1 Complaints include increased difficulty with attention, emotional regulation, and managing everyday demands – symptoms that significantly degrade quality of life yet remain largely unaddressed in midlife ADHD treatment plans.

What Explains This Disconnect?

The Adult ADHD Self-Report Scale (ASRS) – one of the standard measures for assessing common ADHD symptoms – may not fully capture how much menopause interferes with daily life because it was not specifically designed to measure changes in ADHD across hormonal transitions.

While nearly all of the 600 women in the survey reported worsening ADHD symptoms during menopause, their ASRS scores were similar across different hormonal life stages. This apparent contradiction deserves attention. A woman may experience a major change in mental clarity, working memory, motivation, emotional regulation, and managing day-to-day responsibilities without necessarily showing a large change on a standard ADHD symptom checklist.

This points to a critical need for better ways to assess ADHD across the lifespan and to understand how hormonal changes may alter its presentation and impact.

Addressing Hormonal Changes

Today, we know about ADHD in menopause thanks, largely, to women’s personal accounts. Though surveys and self-reports cannot establish cause and effect in the same way as controlled clinical trials, they are an essential starting point. They can identify patterns and raise questions that researchers need to investigate.

One central question is regarding the safety and efficacy of prescribing hormone replacement therapy (HRT) and/or ADHD medication to women in perimenopause and menopause. Sadly, evidence specifically supporting combined HRT and stimulant treatment remains limited. We need well-designed studies to determine which women might benefit, what treatments are most effective, and how the medications may interact.

The voices of women with ADHD have brought this issue into view. Now clinicians and researchers have an opportunity – and a responsibility – to listen carefully, investigate rigorously, and translate emerging knowledge into better care.

In Postwar Rural France, Alexander Calder Found Respite from Pressures of Fame and a Chance to Dream Big

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

If you’ve ever looked long and slow enough at Alexander Calder’s mobiles made of wire-suspended, leaf-like metal shapes painted solid colors, you likely know that they are as much about their sculptural form and silhouette as they are about the breathing air, light, shadow, and nature all around them. They shiver, drift, and play with the unpredictable whims of the surrounding atmosphere, making the invisible ether newly visible. Calder’s alternately delicate and massive sculptures are in communion with much larger forces around us.

At Hauser & Wirth in Paris, several of these sculptures are now on view (until December 19) in the exhibition titled “Calder: François Premier.” The show focuses on the late artist’s return to France from the US from 1953 to 1954 and includes a few mobiles that haven’t been seen in over 70 years: Les Granettes (1953) and The Putney Mobile (1954).

They are joined by an upstairs gallery full of bold, abstract, and figurative gouaches made between a small hamlet near Aix-en-Provence, called Les Granettes (where the 1953 mobile was made and named for), and a 17th-century home in Saché, another hamlet in the Loire Valley, now an artist residency. The more figurative gouaches include mysterious imagery such as pyramids, pulsing, planet-like orbs and even some camels.

“I seemed to develop something new,” Calder said of this sojourn to France after a 20-year absence. His “main” home and studio remained anchored in Roxbury, Connecticut, but he ultimately spent time living and working in Saché until his death in 1976.

“Calder’s return to France in 1953 is essential in understanding his late period. He was really emboldened and really inspired by being back,” Alexander S. C. Rower, his grandson and president of the Calder Foundation, says in a film screened at the gallery. “In really an unprecedented way, he was drawing directly on the metal: fabricating, welding, crafting the works into existence in full-scale, and that didn’t happen before. It was an extraordinary moment, with fantastic works of tremendous vocabulary.”

Installation view of “Calder: François Premier,” 2026, at Hauser & Wirth, Paris. Photo: Nicolas Brasseur; Art: ©2026 Calder Foundation, New York and ADAGP, Paris/Courtesy Calder Foundation, New York and Art Resource, New York

But what had brought Calder to the French countryside in the first place, and how did the place change how he worked?

To find out, ARTnews spoke with Rower, who was 13 when his grandfather died. After he completed a quick, “energetic reading” on me, which first mistakenly concluded I had been a sculptor in my youth but was amended to predict I would become one later, possibly in my 60s, Rower started with recounting Calder’s postwar story.

“The backstory is that during WWII, all of Calder’s friends were trying to come to America and be refugees,” he told me. Calder wrote to the State Department and successfully urged them to let in artists from Europe, such as Fernand Léger and André Masson. He eventually housed Masson and his young family, who all slept in Calder’s Connecticut barn-studio. After the war, Masson insisted Calder come to Aix-en-Provence, but it didn’t take much convincing. With his wife, Louisa, and their two daughters, Calder packed their things and joined their friends in southern France by 1953.

Later that year, the Calders discovered Saché in central France, where another friend (and later son-in-law) was living: the French-American journalist Jean Davidson. Calder soon bought a house in Saché on Davidson’s land, in exchange for three mobiles. From that moment on, “Saché just very naturally evolves into this hub. They have grandchildren there, and it becomes their homestead,” Rower said.

This conversation has been edited and condensed for concision and clarity.

ARTnews: What was special and inspiring about the French countryside to Calder, that might have led to the works we’re seeing here in Paris?

Alexander S. C. Rower: I wouldn’t say that France, or the countryside, was inspiring to him. He wasn’t making works related to what he was experiencing in the village and region. He was using being there to quiet his nervous system. He’s already a super famous guy when he shows up in 1953, and he has lots and lots of commissions. He’s this worldly character, and he needs to have quietude. That’s what Saché really deeply represents. It wasn’t the architecture, the chateaux in the region.

In Calder’s own words, he said he was trying to return to the ‘medieval‘—he’s talking about simple things, like going to this one place with a wood oven for baking bread, and another place that has this flock of goats and they’re making cheese. He liked the people that had maybe more challenges in life, but also had more authenticity, and he felt he was that way. He grew up with absolutely no money. His mom was a painter. His dad was a sculptor. He had to fend for himself. So the countryside in France, it’s still quite like that.

Did that simpler life away from it all change his work?

It definitely changed his work, but didn’t inspire his work. I’d say that he then had the chance with the big open skies to dream about bigger sculpture. He also was achieving success. By 1962, he found a big industrial iron works to execute his major works. He already had a smaller one in Connecticut that was making all the big works, and now he needed one in France, and the very biggest works were executed here in France. For that first group of works completed in 1963, they were all made for his own account. He commissioned seven outdoor sculptures, because he had enough money to make what he wanted to make.

Alexander Calder, Les Granettes, 1953. ©2026 Calder Foundation, New York and ADAGP, Paris/Courtesy Calder Foundation, New York and Art Resource, New York

What was his studio in France like?

The wagon shed, or remise, he converted to his studio was a very small space. A highly productive space. He made so many works there of the late period. Some of the 1950s, but mostly ’60s. Most of the sculpture made in the 1960s was made in that studio. Everything else before that was made in Connecticut, which was his number one studio.

When he arrived in France in 1953, he made four outdoor sculptures, standing mobiles that you put in your garden, one of which was Les Granettes (1953). He made lots of smaller works, but he didn’t make any big sculptures [in France that year].

And yet he started to think more about making large-scale works in Saché?

Saché is this little town where Henri de Balzac used to go and write, and it’s very flat. There’s nothing mountainous in the region, and the idea of monumental sculptures coming up out of the landscape is pretty obvious. It’s big sky country. Maybe the sky, you could say, was inspiring, because a big dynamic black form against the sky is terrific.

Why did you love about this place as a child?

Seeing Calder hard at work in France was a great pleasure. To go to the foundry, and see how he talked to the guys. He loved the guys that helped him execute his works. He wasn’t dismissive. He wasn’t offhand. He wasn’t at a distance. He was really connected with them. He gave them all gouaches and stuff. The welder was the guy he really liked. He’d come bring him a bottle of wine and talk about welding.

I wonder whether they kept any of those artworks he gave them.

They’ve all sold them. They’ve all come to market, and the grandchildren have sold them for education or whatever. Calder would have loved that. It’s just as it should be.

A Retrospective Introduces Francis Picabia to a New Generation—with Funhouse Mirrors and a Peep Show

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

While midday crowds throng under a brilliant sky on Milan’s Piazza del Duomo, darkness gathers behind the windows of the nearby Palazzo Reale, where the vernissage for a major Francis Picabia retrospective has just begun. “Picabia. Open the Gates of Night” is curated by the artist’s great-granddaughter Anne Berest, who explained in her opening remarks that “to know [Picabia] is… to spend the night with him.” As if in a dream, the exhibition unfurls in a dusky twilight, low-lit galleries gathering works under associative themes that simulate an evening escapade with the playboy artist, evoking everything from ecstatic dance and nocturnal trysts to narcotic delirium and crepuscular epiphany.

Picabia (1879–1953) is one of the elusive figures of modern art, a Franco-Spanish painter and poet who moved freely between Impressionism, Fauvism, Orphism, Cubism, Machinism, and Dada, counting among his friends luminaries like Marcel Duchamp, Guillaume Apollinaire, Alfred Stieglitz, Erik Satie, and Gertrude Stein. Always pursuing a visual language for the forms that blossomed within his brain, Picabia believed in perpetual artistic evolution, an idea encoded in two of his oft-quoted aphorisms: “A conviction is a disease,” and, “If you want to have clean ideas, change them as often as you change your shirts.” In the exhibition, this heterodox ethos is manifest in the sheer stylistic variety of Picabia’s work, the walls a procession of representational compositions, geometric abstractions, mechanical diagrams, and syncretic paintings full of appropriated imagery.

Francis Picabia: Lano, ca. 1938. ©Archives Comité Picabia

“Picabia. Open the Gates of Night” makes psychoanalytic forays into the artist’s subconscious and daringly diagnoses his desires and disorders. The stage is set with a boyhood fixation—a gallery dedicated to “the Spanish Night,” also the title of a 1922 painting. Though Picabia was born and raised in France, his father was a Spaniard; during childhood visits to Seville, he witnessed Holy Week processionals, the rhythmic movements of Flamenco dancers, and the vital choreographies of the toreador. Emphasizing his “Andalusian blood,” the adult Picabia sublimated this self-exoticizing nostalgia into compositions on Spanish subjects. At the Palazzo Reale, an entire wall is punctuated by Picabia’s loose pencil and watercolor studies of the severe faces of Sevillian women, dark tresses piled high under lace mantillas. Across the room, a series of works showcases the persistence of dots—a common pattern on Flamenco dresses—across Picabia’s figural and abstract works, such as an untitled 1942 illustration of ballet dancers and his late geometric canvas Symbole (1950). Elsewhere in the room, comparison is drawn between photographs from the Picabia Family Archive of Andalusian architecture, with its lattice panels and arches, and the painted superpositions in Salicis, a 1929 work from his “Transparencies” series (1927–33) in which image planes interweave and interrupt one another as if in double-exposed photographs.

A later gallery, “Love Parade” (borrowing its title from a 1917 mechanomorphic painting), pathologizes Picabia’s lifelong libertinism as a response to the tragic loss of his mother at the age of seven. In their 2017 book Gabriële, the show’s curator and her sister Claire detail his all-male adolescence—he was raised by father, grandfather, and uncle—and observe that his family’s resulting nickname “les Quatre sans femme” (“the Four without a Woman”), might also be heard as “les quatre cents femmes” (“the Four Hundred Women”),” perhaps foreshadowing the vast number of lovers he courted over his lifetime.

Francis Picabia: Femmes au bull-dog, 1941. © RMN-Grand Palais/Philippe Migeat, Christian Bahier. Photo SCALA

Regardless of its roots, Picabia’s sexual drive was a galvanizing force in his work, from the machinic sketches à la Duchamp’s bachelor machine to erotic paintings such as Woman with the Bull (1940–41), which was collaged from found images of pin-up models. Picabia’s unchecked libido offers another interpretation for his “Transparencies,” which emerged at a time when, as the exhibition didactics euphemistically state, Picabia “lived through multiple loves simultaneously.”

Repeatedly, the wall texts underscore the idea that “every portrait is a self portrait.” There is a sense that the exhibition’s most significant revelation is neither synesthetic experiment nor vanguardist provocation but, merely and monumentally, Picabia himself. “Picabia. Open the Gates of Night” attempts to “escape chronology,” as Berest writes in her curatorial statement, setting aside the artistic canon and movement-based ideology in favor of character-study and lyric intuition. Despite their intrigue, these biographical reveries lose their place in history, splitting narrative threads and origin stories for bodies of work—including the “Transparencies” series—across the galleries. Rather than suggesting an overarching vision of the artist’s corpus and context, a dissolution into timeless simultaneity prevails.

View of the exhibition “Picabia. Open the Gates of Night” at Palazzo Reale, Milan. Photo Studio Nicama

Berest frames the exhibition as one for a “new generation,” the museum reimagined as a carnival or party. Within the decadent interiors of the Palazzo Reale, the exhibition operates theatrically as its own scintillating setpiece, the installation design completed by sparkling lights, funhouse mirrors, and a peep show that reveals some of Picabia’s more lascivious drawings. It’s almost as extravagant as the galas that Picabia hosted in the 1930s on the French Riviera, for which he orchestrated outrageous mise-en-scènes—zoo animals secured for “Une nuit à Singapore,” a grove of palms potted and arranged for “Un soir au Palm-Beach.” The exhibition addresses an audience for whom history has been superseded by the experiential, the embodied, and the personal. Perhaps this is as Picabia himself would have it. Another of his aphorisms, after all, celebrates the unbridled iconoclasm of an eternal present-tense: “Everything for today, nothing for yesterday, nothing for tomorrow.”

Max Dowman suspension leaves Arsenal facing another selection headache after appeal rejection

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

The Football Association has rejected Arsenal’s appeal against Max Dowman’s red card at Fleetwood Town, meaning the 16-year-old will serve a three-match suspension.

Dowman was sent off during Tuesday’s Carabao Cup defeat, leaving Mikel Arteta with another attacking selection headache. Arsenal are already without several wide players through injury.

In the 85th minute of the match, Dowman received a red card for responding to a heavy challenge by Fleetwood’s Harrison Neal. Although he did not make a full kicking motion, his foot made contact with Neal.

The decision was a costly one for Arsenal, who were already struggling in the game against their League Two opponents. They lost the game 1-1 and then saw Fleetwood win the penalty shootout 5-3.

They lodged an appeal on Wednesday but have now been turned down, meaning Dowman will miss the next three games.

Arteta loses another attacking option as Arsenal prepare for Liverpool

Dowman’s absence adds to Arsenal’s current problems in the wide areas.

That is three injured players with Bukayo Saka, Noni Madueke and Christos Tzolis all sidelined. It means there aren’t many attacking options for Arteta to choose from.

But they have a big game coming up on Sunday when they go to Liverpool. Arteta will need to decide who he is going to play.

Dowman had another chance to shine in the game against Fleetwood. However, he only started for the second time this season. It was somewhat of a controversial night for the teenager.

This is the second time Dowman has received a red card in a short space of time having been sent off while playing for England Under-19s earlier this month.

Second red card raises questions over Dowman’s discipline

The latest dismissal has also shone a light on how Arsenal deal with one of their brightest young stars.

As Dowman made his way to the touchline, Arteta looked frustrated. He gave a serious look to the young teen and appeared to say something in anger as he exited the pitch.

This suggests it was something that had been discussed prior to the game. Arteta later said that the coaching staff had spoken about the emotional demands of the game during their preparations.

The Arsenal manager said the players had received clear instructions about what they needed to respect and control emotionally.

The dismissal was due to violent conduct. The laws of football state that a red card can be shown if a player uses or attempts to use excessive force or brutality on an opponent, outside of a challenge for the ball.

This does not necessarily mean there has to be physical contact. In this case there was contact between Dowman and Neal.

The teen will miss the next three matches as a result of his ban. The focus will be on getting Arsenal ready for their Liverpool match with a lack of attacking options at their disposal.

For Dowman, it is important to avoid any further disciplinary issues.

He has made a few senior appearances already, thanks to his talent, but avoiding disqualification is also necessary for his growth as a player.

Eating disorders are on the rise. GLP-1s could make things worse.

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

GLP-1 weight-loss drugs are one of the biggest biotech breakthroughs of the last decade. But to some clinical psychologists, they can represent something more sinister—a potential trigger for eating disorders.

A new report suggests that the global incidence of anorexia nervosa and bulimia nervosa shot up between 1990 and 2023—by a whopping 71%. By 2023, there were an estimated 18.1 million people living with one of these two disorders.

What’s worse is that the number is likely to be much higher than that once we factor in other eating disorders. And, disturbingly, those in the field are already starting to see GLP-1 weight-loss drugs exacerbating the issue.

Cheri Levinson is a clinical psychologist at the University of Louisville in Kentucky who has specialized in eating disorders for almost 20 years. She says she’s now seeing more patients with more severe symptoms than at any other time in her career, and that many of them are misusing GLP-1s. “This is the worst I’ve seen things,” she says.

Eating disorders are mental health conditions in which people’s eating behaviors impact their health. They include anorexia nervosa (defined by a fear of weight gain), bulimia nervosa (in which people binge and purge), binge eating disorder, and others.

It’s thought that some people have a genetic predisposition to certain eating disorders, but that some external factor is what triggers their onset. That might be stress, or social pressure to be skinny. Often, simply restricting food intake can trigger an eating disorder, says Levinson.

Eating disorders—along with the pressure to look thin—were already prevalent in the 1990s (the heroin chic era springs to mind). To find out how things might have changed since then, Ruhai Bai at the Children’s Hospital of Nanjing Medical University in China and his colleagues pulled together data from studies and surveys conducted on populations around the world. The team used statistical models to fill in any data gaps.

That research allowed the researchers to estimate the annual incidence of two eating disorders—anorexia nervosa and bulimia nervosa—for each of 204 countries and territories between 1990 and 2023. They also estimated the impact those disorders had on global health by calculating their disability-adjusted life years (one DALY represents a year of healthy life lost).

The findings, published in JAMA Psychiatry on Wednesday, are unsettling. Between 1990 and 2023, there was a 71% increase in the incidence of those two eating disorders, the team found. Those conditions had a huge impact on global health, too. In 2023, they “cost the world the equivalent of about 3.83 million years of healthy life,” says Bai.

Bai and his colleagues expect these figures to continue to rise. In 2023, an estimated 18.1 million people had either anorexia nervosa or bulimia nervosa; by 2050, the team predict, the two conditions could affect 21.8 million people.

It gets worse. Those two conditions represent less than half of all eating disorders, says Levinson, who was not involved in Bai’s study. The true impact of these disorders is “probably three times the rate that they’re reporting,” she says.

Bai and his colleagues didn’t explore the reasons for this rise (although Bai points out that it can’t be entirely explained by population growth). So how did we get here?

Social media may have played a role. “Social media comparisons, influencers, challenges, diet trends can all be precipitating … events for an eating disorder,” says Cynthia Bulik, a clinical psychologist at the University of North Carolina, who was not involved in the new study. Bulik has been studying and treating eating disorders since the early 1980s. “Between 1990 and 2023 there has clearly been an upsurge of those types of environmental exposures,” she says.

Many social media users are exposed to filtered and modified images of skinny influencers who may have undergone cosmetic surgery or extreme dieting and who are promoting “thinspiration” or “fitspiration” content. But users with eating disorders appear to be more likely to engage with this content—and to be fed more of it as a result.

When researchers at the University of Melbourne investigated this phenomenon a couple of years ago, they found that the algorithms of TikTok users with eating disorders delivered 4343% more “toxic eating disorder videos” than those of users who didn’t have the disorders. They also got more content focused on dieting or exercising. Many were fed over 1000 problematic videos per month. Bulik describes social media as “quicksand” for people with, or at risk of, eating disorders.

The covid-19 pandemic—and the social isolation that accompanied lockdowns—also appears to have contributed to the rise in eating disorders. Research conducted in 2021 found that the number of people hospitalized with eating disorders in the US doubled between 2018 and 2020.

Researchers don’t yet have good data on how trends in eating disorders might have progressed since 2023. But anecdotally, things seem to be getting even worse. “There are more eating disorders, more severe eating disorders… and we’re seeing earlier ages of onset,” says Levinson. “We’re seeing eight- and nine-year-olds with eating disorders.”

“From what I’m hearing and seeing, I am not going to be surprised if that number isn’t at least doubling again,” she adds.

Levinson particularly worries about the impact of GLP-1 weight-loss drugs, which have exploded in popularity in recent years. A Gallup survey conducted earlier this year suggested that 15% of Americans had used one of these drugs for weight loss. And they’re becoming more accessible—drug companies are working on new formulations that are both easier to take and more effective, and many people can buy versions of these drugs from compound pharmacies or dubious online sources.

In a small study published earlier this year, Levinson and her colleagues found that 30% of people with eating disorders had used GLP-1 drugs. The drugs might benefit some of those people. But 10% appeared to have misused the drugs. And Levinson thinks that figure is also an underestimate.

Levinson says she and her colleagues have seen children develop eating disorders after being prescribed GLP-1s for medical reasons. They’ve seen others—even children as young as 11—buy versions of these drugs over TikTok.

“We’re very concerned that GLPs will lead to even faster and more extreme weight loss in people with anorexia nervosa, and that patients will come to us in even more advanced stages of illness that become difficult to reverse,” says Bulik.

Both Bulik and Levinson are continuing to study the impacts of these drugs in eating disorders. In the meantime, both urge medical professionals to consider the risks GLP-1s might pose to people with those disorders. Bulik contributed to a set of research and clinical guidelines, published earlier this month, that recommends doctors screen for eating disorders before prescribing the drugs, for example. The guidelines also recommend more patient education and more robust monitoring of people who take GLP-1s.

If you think you or someone you know might have an eating disorder, you can find information and support at the National Eating Disorders Association.

Opinion: How Congress can fix expert advisory committees

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

In September, Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. fired the two vice chairs of the U.S. Preventive Services Task Force. This move was both unsurprising and troubling. The task force is a critical advisory body to HHS, reviewing scientific evidence to provide recommendations on whether private health insurers should be required to cover services like cancer screenings at no cost to patients. Congress created the task force as part of the Affordable Care Act and require by law that it be staffed by people with “appropriate expertise,” and “to the extent practicable, not subject to political pressure.”

These firings are the latest chapter in a vast project of eliminating, ignoring, and replacing scientific advisory committees. In 2025, the Trump administration terminated 151 advisory committees at scientific government agencies, including 75 in HHS. It has altered or ignored those that are left. In 2025, Kennedy fired the entire Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices, which provides public recommendations on vaccines. His replacements are, to varying degrees, sympathetic to his unscientific views, and a majority lack substantial expertise in vaccines and immunology.

A customer buys a token on a U.S. exchange and the app shows the balance. Under the CFTC's December 14 framework, nothing has been delivered yet.

Under the SEC's crypto proposal, delivery doesn't decide the question at all. An October 27 comment letter asks the two agencies to settle on one answer.

Open almost any crypto exchange app after a purchase and you'll see a clean number: 1.0000 ETH, available. To the customer, the asset is theirs. To the exchange's database, it's a row in an internal ledger. To federal regulators, that row is now being judged by two different tests that don't agree on what it means.

The Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC) has opened an advance notice of proposed rulemaking on what it calls Regulation Crypto Asset Transactions (CTX) and Regulation Crypto Asset Markets (CAM). It sets out the agency's first formal view of "actual delivery" for crypto since it withdrew its 2020 guidance last December. Comments close on December 14.

The Securities and Exchange Commission's Regulation Crypto Assets proposal takes a different view of the same moment. Then, on October 27, a comment letter on the SEC's file raised the gap directly. It described the situation as "two conflicting definitions of the same physical facts" and asked the agencies to "please coordinate the definitions."

Why A Single Ledger Entry Now Has Two Meanings

Delivery matters because it decides which rulebook applies.

For the CFTC, the answer comes from Section 2(c)(2)(D) of the Commodity Exchange Act. A leveraged, margined or financed retail commodity trade is treated like a futures contract unless "actual delivery" happens within 28 days. If delivery happens in time, the trade falls outside futures rules. If it doesn't, the exchange is effectively running a futures venue.

For the SEC, the question is whether a token is still part of an investment contract. Under the August proposal, a covered investment contract ends when the issuer has finished or permanently stopped its essential managerial efforts and files a Form TR transition report. Whether the buyer has received the token is not part of that test.

So one agency treats delivery as the deciding question, and the other barely considers it.

How The CFTC Reads Actual Delivery

The CFTC's position is still preliminary, but it is detailed. Published in the Federal Register on October 13, the notice relies on the Monex case standard: actual delivery requires "the transfer of some meaningful degree of possession or control."

For crypto, the agency suggests that possession and control "may require possession of the credentials (e.g., private key(s))." It takes three positions that matter most to exchanges:

  • Internal records: a fully paid trade "that is only recorded internally by an exchange before actual delivery occurs" stays within CFTC jurisdiction.
  • Book-entry title: a customer receiving title "through a book-entry" does not count as actual delivery.
  • On-chain transfers: if the customer "takes possession of the credentials to the digital wallet address or account, then actual delivery occurs." On-chain protocol trades that send assets straight to a customer's wallet will usually meet the test.

The agency is also asking whether control has to be exclusive, whether delivery can happen while a lien or security interest is outstanding, and how financing offered in an exchange's standard account documents affects whether a trade is covered at all.

How The SEC's Proposal Treats Delivery

The SEC's proposal, released on August 18, deals with a different question. It creates offering exemptions, including a $5 million startup exemption over four years, and a safe harbor for when a crypto asset's investment contract ends. Neither depends on where the token sits.

Under the proposal, a token held in a customer's self-custody wallet and a token recorded on an exchange's books are treated the same way. In both cases, the investment contract question depends on the issuer's managerial efforts and its filings.

That is the gap the October 27 letter tries to close. Its author, who describes themselves as a fintech founder designing a consumer platform, argues that "where the customer holds the private keys or execution is on-chain, with custody evidence, the asset should be treated as delivered and the covered-investment-contract analysis should end there."

Running Four Trades Through Both Tests

Running four common exchange transactions through both frameworks shows where the tests split.

Trade one: a fully paid purchase left on the exchange.

  • CFTC: not delivered. The trade is still only a book entry. If the platform's account terms offer financing, the trade can stay within CFTC jurisdiction until delivery happens.
  • SEC proposal: where the token sits doesn't matter. Under the comment letter's model, it is not delivered, because the platform holds the keys.

Trade two: the same purchase withdrawn to a self-custody wallet on day three.

  • CFTC: actual delivery happened inside the 28-day window, so the trade falls outside futures-style regulation.
  • SEC proposal: the analysis doesn't change. Under the comment letter's model, the asset is delivered and the investment contract question ends.

Trade three: a margined purchase kept on the exchange's ledger for 30 days.

  • CFTC: no actual delivery within 28 days, so the trade is regulated as if it were a futures contract and must sit on a registered venue.
  • SEC proposal: the 28-day clock has no effect.

Trade four: an on-chain swap that settles directly into the user's wallet.

  • CFTC: this will usually count as actual delivery.
  • SEC proposal: no direct effect. Under the comment letter's model, the asset is delivered.

The pattern is consistent. Self-custody is the only position that clearly counts as delivery under the CFTC's view and also ends the analysis under the letter's proposed SEC fix. Everything left on an exchange's internal ledger stays in an unclear position. The CFTC treats it as undelivered and the SEC's text doesn't address it.

The Comment Letter Asking The SEC To Line Up

The letter was filed on October 27, a week after the SEC's October 20 comment deadline. It is anonymous and brief, but its main concern is practical. If the definitions stay separate, it warns, founders will have to design products around whichever rule is stricter.

It makes four requests:

  • Keep the startup exemption: preserve the $5 million exemption, and consider indexing it.
  • Base the safe harbor on custody: treat customer-held keys or on-chain execution with custody evidence as delivery.
  • Coordinate with the CFTC: align the SEC's approach with the CFTC's work on actual delivery.
  • Clarify non-custodial intermediaries: explain how platforms that route trades but never hold customer assets are treated.

It is the only filing on either docket so far that sets the two definitions side by side.

What Exchanges Should Watch Before December 14

The CFTC process is still at the advance-notice stage, so a formal proposal will follow. For U.S. platforms, though, the direction is clear. Under the CFTC's preliminary reading, an exchange that keeps customer assets in internal ledger accounts has not delivered them. If that exchange also offers margin or financing, it is on track to be treated as a futures venue.

The SEC's final rule could adopt the letter's custody-based approach or keep delivery out of its analysis entirely. Until one of those happens, the same ledger entry can be undelivered for one regulator and irrelevant for the other.

Exchanges and their customers have until December 14 to say which definition should prevail. The real decision is whether "delivered" ends up meaning the same thing at both agencies.
