Christian group touts 'ethical' tech features as concerns over out-of-control AI tools persist

The leader of a Christian ministry that built its own artificial intelligence application to guide users in their spiritual journey warns companies developing AI systems need to implement "ethical guidelines" to ensure their technology doesn't harm people.

Ligonier Ministries President and CEO Chris Larson says the true highest intelligence is God and shared a new tool his group created to help answer questions people may have from a faith-based perspective.

"It's interesting that [President Donald Trump] even referred to artificial intelligence now as 'superintelligence,'" Larson told Fox News Digital. "Of course, Christians — and, really, all of us — have access to the 'superintelligece,' the one who has created the universe."

CHRISTIAN UNIVERSITY AIMS TO PRODUCE 'IRREPLACEABLE' GRADUATES AS AI RESHAPES HIGHER EDUCATION

The mission of the Florida-based organization, founded by the late Christian theologian R.C. Sproul, is to proclaim, teach and defend the holiness of God "in all its fullness to as many people as possible."

Part of accomplishing that mission was building an app that the organization calls Grow.

"It's dedicated to helping Christians to better understand the Bible and theology and to go deeper in their faith, put down roots, if you will," Larson said of the new tool. "And, so, this app has 50 years of teaching in it from more than 800 different teachers, of course, grounded in Dr. R.C. Sproul's lifetime of teaching."

The app draws from a library of tens of thousands of pieces of content to help users in their faith journey looking to obtain answers to critical questions.

Inside Grow is an "intelligent answer assistant" that can answer questions from users from a faith-based perspective.

But building Grow for Christian users required putting a great deal of thought into the ethics of the model, Larson explained.

"Now, it's not trying to pretend it's a person or anything like that. It is just trained on our library, and then helping Christians to trust the answers, and [it] will only go so far," he said. 

"And, again, I think that's an important guard as well. Because, for us, making sure that we have trusted theological answers and that the intelligent answer assistant is acting with integrity and not going outside of that."

When users push the boundaries of the application, it directs them to real human support, a feature most large language model AI tools refuse to implement.

"And, so, we've given it a sandbox to work within. And any time that somebody tries to push it beyond the borders, we're going to deflect or defer to a real human. Imagine that," Larson said.

For example, if a user discusses suicide in Grow, the intelligent answer assistant will direct them to the suicide hotline or other real-world resources.

Over the past several years, there have been a flurry of suicide deaths that victims' family members say AI chatbots were at least partially responsible. 

In one case from last year, a teenager named Adam Raine died by suicide after months of discussing his suicidal thoughts on ChatGPT. According to a lawsuit from his parents, the chatbot provided him information about methods of suicide, validated aspects of planning the suicide and offered to help him draft a suicide note. OpenAI, ChatGPT's parent company, disputes the characterization and says the chatbot directed him to crisis resources multiple times.

"So, we understood that it's important to provide safety guardrails, but also we did not want this intelligent answer assistant to pretend that it was a person," Larson said. "We don't want it to take the place of a pastor or a local church elder or even a parent."

Larson said companies developing AI need to consider guardrails for their technology.

"Anybody developing AI tools today needs to think through ethical guidelines, and that's more of the self-regulation and guardrails that we've been talking about," he said.

"I think there's a real concern today because you have great power that's been entrusted to these different companies, and they're not really disclosing their worldview," he continued. "How do they know right from wrong?"

For Ligonier, the absolute truth upon which its AI model is built is the Christian faith.

"How are you determining what is right and wrong? As Christians, we believe we have a true answer — a source that we can turn to."

When Jimmy Chin was in his twenties, living out of a 1989 Subaru Loyale somewhere between Yosemite and the Tetons, he kept trying to explain climbing to his father. His father, a librarian at Mankato State University in Minnesota, would listen, and then say: “Our language is 5,000 years old, and there is no word for what you do.”

Chin has spent the decades since building a vocabulary for it anyway, as a climber, a photographer, and the Oscar-winning director of Free Solo featuring Alex Honnold, and his latest film, Everest: The Other Side, which documents Jim Morrison and Hilaree Nelson’s attempt to climb and ski down the North Face of the world’s tallest mountain.

His new book, Rules to Live By (Celadon), presents other people’s words, but is also part memoir. It’s built from ten conversations with people who have lived on their own terms: Yvon Chouinard, Yo-Yo Ma, Simone Biles, Ridley Scott, Kelly Slater, Annette Bening, Lynsey Addario, Kris Tompkins, Alex Honnold, and Bill King, a retired command master chief of SEAL Team 6. He asks each of them some version of the same question: In the end, what really matters? And in the end, we learn what matters most to Jimmy Chin as well.

Earlier this month, I sat down with Chin onstage at the Bell House in Brooklyn. It was his first book event, and he confessed he hadn’t reread the book since turning it in. “There’s a reason I’m a filmmaker,” he told the crowd. “Writing is really, really difficult.” What followed covered violin practice, an undisclosed military base, and a missing sock. The conversation has been edited and condensed.

Jimmy Chin in conversation with Outside Editorial Director Kevin Sintumuang. (Photo: Daniel Dumas)

“If you’re going to do it, do it well”

OUTSIDE: You write that you’re “the only son of an only son of an only son.” That sounds like a lot of pressure.

Jimmy Chin: I started violin when I was three, and it became two hours of practice a day. Then martial arts, two hours a day. Then homework. That was my weekdays, and it was all I knew. But I learned discipline, and there was a very high expectation for excellence. My dad always said, “If you’re going to do it, you might as well do it well.” I hated that. Sometimes I still struggle with it.

There’s a scene in the book where you’re practicing violin, staring out the window at the forest. When did the forest win?

Chin: Anytime I did well in school, my reward was running off into the woods. There were no rules out there. My parents were librarians, so I always had a lot of books, and later, when they asked how I ended up doing this, I said, “It’s your fault. You gave me all these books.” I found climbing in college, and it was the first thing that felt like a calling. Growing up, I literally thought there were three jobs: lawyer, doctor, or professor. So after graduation I told my parents I’d take one year off, get it out of my system, and then pursue my real career. Which was a total lie. From 21 to 28 I lived out of my car, or in a cave in Camp 4, or in a chicken coop in Jackson.

What were those phone calls home like?

Chin: My sister, who went to Stanford and worked at Yale for 18 years, was my huge advocate. I’d ask her how Mom was doing, and she’d say, “Mom just keeps saying, ‘I raised a homeless man.” With my dad, I deeply wanted him to understand me. People see the films and photographs and ask about the greatest risk I’ve ever taken. It definitely wasn’t a mountain. It was being in my early twenties, full of doubt. There are a lot of storm days, sitting in a cave with water dripping, soaking wet, thinking, What am I doing here? Meanwhile your friends are going to grad school and starting careers.

And during this time, you were in a reggae band.

Chin: I dropped violin after high school and picked up the guitar. The furthest thing I could think of from a concerto in D minor was covering Bob Marley. The whole band was classically trained kids. Everybody was technically skilled, so we could play whatever we wanted.

Did you ever put out an album?

Chin: [Laughs.] No…we weren’t that good.

A Constellation of Answers

How did you choose the ten people in the book?

Chin: My documentaries take four, five, six, seven years. You have to bleed for them. You can only make so many films in a lifetime, and there are so many people I admire who I’ll never make a film about. I wanted the book to be a constellation: totally different backgrounds, totally different careers. After these extraordinary lives, what have you discovered really matters? What did success look like when you started, and what does it look like now? I had just turned 50, so I was looking back at my life in phases and thinking about what was to come.

An Anomaly Among Anomalies

You almost didn’t make Free Solo.

Chin: After Meru, producers kept asking what was next. I mentioned to my friend Evan Hayes, who became a producer on Free Solo, that I’d been thinking about a documentary about Alex. I hadn’t even finished his name before he said, “That’s it.” National Geographic green-lit it immediately. I hadn’t even made a pitch deck.

I’d been filming and photographing Alex for ten years. I’ve worked with some of the greatest adventure athletes of our time, people at the pinnacle of their sports. All of them were anomalies within their sport. In my mind, Alex was an anomaly among anomalies. Even athletes at that level were confounded by what he was doing.

So I called him: “Good news, the film’s green-lit.” He said, “Great, because I’m thinking about soloing El Cap.” I said, “No. Not making that film.” I literally called Evan and said, “I’m out. Done.”

Why?

Chin: Among his friends, none of us would even talk about that idea. We didn’t want to put it in the ether. We’d seen the trajectory. He soloed Moonlight Buttress, which was outrageous. Then he soloed Half Dome. We were talking about breaking speed records on Half Dome, not free soloing it. It wasn’t hard to see what came after that. We just didn’t want to say it out loud. And then he dropped it in my lap. My fear was obvious: What if filming him causes him to fall? That’s not something I could live with.

What changed your mind?

Chin: He was going to do it anyway. I knew Alex really well. I took him on his first international expedition, and we’d done so much together. And I trusted him. Alex was the most calculated person I’d ever met. He had instinct, he was extremely smart, and it was never about external validation. He’s also straightforward, to a fault. He’s been well trained by now, with all the awards. But when I first met him, he’d say things and we’d be like, “You can’t say that.” If he isn’t comfortable doing something, he’ll tell you, and tell you why it’s stupid.

So we moved forward, and I gave my crew three rules. They were almost all elite professional climbers, because that’s the only team you could have: a short list of people who are both. Number one, we’re his friends first. Second, we’re climbers; you think like a climber before you pull out the camera. Third, we’re filmmakers. The film’s needs can never supersede Alex’s needs.

People saw the brain scan in the film and decided Alex was superhuman. You write that he resents that.

Chin: Because it’s just not true. He says, “I feel fear. I’ve always felt fear.” One of the reasons he started soloing was that he was so shy he was too scared to ask people to climb with him. So he climbed by himself. His process was to step outside his comfort zone a little at a time, in a regimented, disciplined way, until the things that scared him were well within it. The film took almost four years. But he’d been training for that climb his entire career. We left the brain scan open to interpretation, but that’s his process.

Alive, and Alive Inside

In 2017 you were trying to dial down the risk in your life. Then Conrad Anker and Alex asked you to come to Antarctica, and you went. You argue in the book that children need parents who are alive, but also parents who feel alive inside. Is that an argument or a good excuse?

Chin: Those are really personal decisions. It’s impossible to justify to a child that their parent died in the mountains doing what I do. I don’t know if there’s a right answer. I’ve lost a lot of friends in the mountains, some of them parents. After my friend Hilaree Nelson died, Bill King wrote to me, and I wrote back: “The mountains give us so much life, yet they take so much life away. It never makes sense, but we understand.” He quoted that line in his retirement speech. So it’s not an excuse. I believe your kids want you alive physically, and alive as yourself. For better or worse, this is my calling.

How do you tell fear that’s protecting you from fear that’s psyching you out?

Chin: Fear can sharpen your senses or paralyze you. When things are going sideways, panic never serves you. So you break it down: How much of this is perceived risk, and how much is actual risk? That exercise alone brings you back into the present. You set the perceived risks aside, focus on what’s real, and mitigate it. Then you make a plan and execute.

Jimmy Chin at work on the Northwest Face of Half Dome. (Photo: Courtesy of Mikey Schaefer via Celadon Books)

The Question at the Undisclosed Location

I love how all Bill King wanted to know was why you turned around 100 meters from the summit of Meru.

Chin: I got a surprise call from SEAL Team 6 asking me to come talk at the base. They gave me a date, a time, and an airport. I got on a plane, they flew me to an undisclosed location, took my phone. I hadn’t been nervous speaking in years, but these guys roll in and size you up. Someone told me every member of Team 6 had watched Meru. When I finished, a guy in the back cut to the chase: “How did you decide to turn around 100 meters from the summit?” I knew the entire reason I was there was to answer that question. That was Bill.

When you’ve put years into an objective and it’s within sight, and things aren’t right, it takes extraordinary discipline to walk away. On Meru we had seven days of food and were on the wall for 19. We crossed so many of our own lines, deliberately. You set a line, reach it, evaluate, set another. The more you do that, the more invested you are, and the harder it is to turn around.

You and the SEALs share a no-asshole rule. Tell me about that. 

Chin: I asked Bill what trait gets people through training. He didn’t flinch: “Humor.” If a guy is getting his ass handed to him and he’s still cracking jokes, they’re not worried about him. It’s the same in the mountains. Humor changes the environment and brings you back to good, rational decisions. My selection process isn’t as serious as theirs, but these are the people holding a rope for you. You can’t have people who only think about themselves. And you can feel it. In conversation, it’s “I, I, I, me.”

Yvon’s Wisdom

Yvon Chouinard is the closest thing you have to a guru. He tells you, “It’s so easy to complicate your life. It’s so much harder to simplify it.” Have you managed it?

Chin: On expeditions, yes. That’s part of why I love them: the best version of me comes out. We’re brutal about simplifying, because every ounce counts. Everything is about efficiency, even how much stress you allow, even your heart rate. I can stay really calm in the worst situations. But if I can’t find my matching sock at home, it’s all over. In the mountains, when things are going sideways, I’m like, oh, this is casual.

You have kids. Do you worry about whether the wild places you’ve spent your life in will still be there for them?

Chin: It’s what the last pages of the book are about. I think of it with Yvon on one end and Kris Tompkins on the other. They’ve both had a tremendous impact on my life, and I’ve talked with both of them about this a lot.

Yvon always says, “Everybody has different tools. Whatever tool you have in your toolbox, whatever you’re good at, that’s what you should contribute.” It’s not only activism. His example is that if you’re an accountant, there are a ton of grassroots organizations that need accountants. So you ask: What can I contribute? Contribute what you have.

And what’s in your toolbox?

Chin: Storytelling, and a platform. That’s why I made Wild Life after Free Solo. I had the chance to finance a film about conservationists, which I probably wouldn’t have had otherwise. It’s about Doug and Kris Tompkins, who preserved something like 17 million acres over a 25-year dream of creating national parks. I’ve brought my kids down there. Those are the things I hope will make a difference: telling these stories, and putting them in a book. What Yvon and Kris have to say is so much more compelling than what I have to say about it.

The coffee cups were almost dry again, and so were the hunting stories. Milt’s electric clock showed 6 a.m.—time to go. Moments later, the six of us were swallowed up in the gloom of a dense fog. Four inches of fluffy new snow muffled our footsteps as we began the trek to our deer stands.

There was a nervous excitement in the air that morning. We knew we were going to see deer. Rarely had we been so confident on an opening day. Almost 200 acres of willow swamp and fields belonged to us that morning and the preseason scouting we’d done plus the new snow added up to nothing but good news. The anticipation was almost crackling in the air around us.

I reached my stand first. I brushed off the loose snow and stood listening for a moment. The absolute stillness meant that my ears would be about as helpful as my eyes for the first few hours—longer if the fog remained. An inch of frost covered every limb of the big willow I occupied. I backed into a collar-high branch and a handful of the frozen dew fell, shaking any remaining sleep from me.

To my left was a standing field of short corn. To my right, the edge of a brushy swale wound its way down to the big willow swamp 200 yards away. In front of me, a fallen willow limb blocked my view. I settled comfortably against the trunk of the big tree and surveyed the fields. Sometimes the fog would allow me 80 yards of view, at other times less than 40.

When at last, according to my watch, it was 8 a.m., I knew that the sun had to be up. The light through the fog, however, had changed little. From the cornfield to my left came sounds of movement—short crunching noises. My ears strained to identify them. A cock pheasant crowed not far away and I relaxed a bit. A few moments later I watched a bluejay working over a cob. Birds were making the sounds, I told myself.

For nearly 30 minutes I continued to hear the sounds: a few crunches followed by silence. Then I saw the outline of a deer’s back moving slowly through the rows. Head down, the deer came closer, quartering across the field. At last it raised its head. For the first time I could see its crown of antlers. Not again, I said to myself, a bit of dread mixing with my excitement.

Seven times before, I’d managed to get shots at trophy bucks and seven times I’d missed—sometimes with a bow, sometimes with a shotgun. I’d got my chance nearly every season I’d hunted, and I’d gone home empty-handed every time. My hunting companions marveled at my incredible knack for being in the right place at the right time and at my equally incredible bad shooting. Despite hours of practice, something always seemed to go awry at the moment of truth. Not again, I groaned inwardly.

When it seemed that the buck was coming no closer, I raised my shotgun. The iron sights of my new slug barrel came to rest on the target—still more than 60 yards away. I fired.

The blast shook frost from the willow branches. For a moment, the buck disappeared. When I could see again, he was on the move.

Again I fired and again and again—as the buck crossed from left to right. As the big deer quartered toward me, he was hidden behind the frosty fallen branches of the willow. Suddenly he reappeared before me and I shot once more. The buck stopped just 40 yards away, his wide, dark rack swiveling like a radar antenna.

My gun was empty. I had to reload. Frantically I fumbled deep in my pocket for the shells. They were still in the box. This one time I hadn’t opened a second box just in case. Almost hysterically I tore the cardboard and threw a slug into the chamber. I took aim at the buck’s neck and fired. I thought I saw the deer cringe before he loped away down the swale toward the big swamp. Then I lost sight of him in the fog and frosty willows.

I can’t adequately describe my feelings. Despair is as close as any named emotion comes. I was beside myself. I couldn’t believe I’d missed again. The deer was right there, at the range I’d sighted-in my new barrel for, and still I’d missed. I scrambled down off the stand and began to search for the deer’s trail in hopes of finding blood, hair, anything that might mean the buck was hit. I reloaded the shotgun then and, to my disgust, found two shells in the magazine. In the excitement, I’d evidently put more slugs in the gun than I knew. I could have shot again—more despair.

In the snow, I followed the buck’s tracks across the swale, along the edge of the cornfield on the other side, way past the point at which I’d lost sight of him. For more than 200 yards I moved slowly beside the tracks. Then I was close to the swamp and my friend Steve’s stand. He’d heard the shots, but hadn’t glimpsed any deer passing by. “Maybe it was hidden in the fog,” he said.

I walked the trail many times that morning, tracing and retracing the deer’s movements in the snow. A sad realization was weighing more heavily with every step. Grudgingly, I had to admit that I must have missed number eight.

The next day the hunt ended and I went home, but the memory of the hunt stayed with me. When I tried to sleep, that morning’s events played back like a worn instant replay. Again and again I saw the buck cross and again and again I watched my shots as if I were looking for my mistake. I tried to spot and correct my error, but even in my dreams the buck escaped.

Five days later, a friend and I took to those same willows in search of pheasants. He’d heard my tale and seemed to understand. As we approached where I’d last seen the buck, I took a breath to call my friend. I wanted to tell him where the deer had been when my shots had missed. But before I could form the words, Tim stopped dead in his tracks.

“Oh no,” he said with a groan. “Oh no! There’s a huge buck lying over here.”

My first thought was that he was joking. But he wouldn’t have been, couldn’t have been. I was trembling as I waded the deep grass toward him. A sick sort of feeling began to knot my stomach. The sight of a shining antler almost put me to my knees.

There in the thin yellow grass lay my buck.

He lay there as he must have lain after the final shot that Saturday morning. He was crouched like a sleeping dog, facing the trail that I’d mistaken for his. My last shot had gone very low, into the left hindquarter and up into the body cavity. The wound hadn’t bled. That was why I’d found no blood in the snow.

I stood there in the silence thinking quickly, eyes burning. Tim shared the silence. He understood. I must have passed within a dozen yards of the buck time after time. Why hadn’t I seen him? How could a buck grow invisible in knee-high grass? Maybe he’d been deeper in the swale and had crawled out later when death approached. But why hadn’t we seen him on the second day? The trail to two of our other stands ran close by.

If only I’d gone into the swale on a hunch. If only I’d had a hunch. If only there hadn’t been such thick fog on that day, surely Steve would have seen the deer from his lofty perch. If only the wound had bled so I’d have stayed on the right trail. If only I’d shot truer. If only, if only…

But such musings were no help. I was beside myself with self-loathing. I wanted to throw up. I wanted to cry. Everything I was striving for as a hunter lay in ruin like the buck.

Tim and I returned that afternoon to take the cape and antlers. They were all that we could salvage. Silence returned as I guided the knife, wishing away the green that had begun to color the ruined venison. Forcing myself to speak, I asked Tim to age the deer. He was wildlife manager for the area’s Department of Natural Resources. He estimated 3½ years, give or take a season. That, at least, was a comfort.

I took the antlers as much out of respect for the animal as for myself. I knew the head would be a most worthy reminder.

“Well,” the taxidermist said, “at least you got the horns. You can always buy meat.” He didn’t understand.

Now the head hangs in a place of honor in my home. Beautiful as it is, I get no joy out of looking at it. It remains, however, a lasting monument to that day. I still think of the rotting venison and the slow death the buck may have had. I learned something then, something of deer hunting and of myself.

Many evenings, after the head was first hung, I would sit looking at it and recall that day. I had decided that my gun was to blame for the poor shooting, that the barrel wasn’t tightened down properly on the stock. I knew, too, that the deer had been lost because of poor tracking skills, fog, bad luck, all of the classic reasons. But I was only half right at best.

Finally I came to understand that the tale was told in those first moments when the buck raised his crown and I began to doubt myself. I forgot my skills then, thinking only of the glory or the humiliation that I faced. The strain of the moment, my self doubt and consequent loss of concentration cost me that buck. I was not a competent enough marksman to rise to the occasion, under pressure. Only more practice could be the cure. 

I haven’t faced a buck since then, although I killed a doe cleanly the following season just 100 yards from the spot where I’d seen that buck. Somehow the pressure had faded then, but does don’t carry antlers. I wonder about that day to come when I’ll face a buck once more. And I wonder if the lesson that began on a frosty morning not long ago will serve me then.

Amazon sends chills through its workforce with mixed message memo about AI use

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

There's a ridiculously simple test you can use to figure out whether AI mandates at a given company are well-considered, or are being shoved out the door by out-of-touch buffoons: What happens when an engineer documents that AI made a task worse? Are they taken seriously, or are they marginalized and eventually managed out?

Conveniently, this week Amazon published a treatise by Terk Terkowitz, a VP/Distinguished Engineer/Final Boss of Their Independent Contributor Technical Career Track, on the role of the developer in the age of AI. It was originally an internal missive sent to their software engineers and their managers back in September, which is an interesting lag for a document that sounds this confident. It also maps exceedingly well to what other organizations are struggling with.

I think it's a good document, but I can't help but view it with a somewhat cynical eye. I see it as an attempt to make the future of the profession of software engineering feel less bleak. And it's feeling mighty bleak for a lot of folks right now.

What's the context?

This treatise comes at a time of dramatic upheaval. Tech as an industry is no stranger to change (usually at 3AM, heralded by the pager), but something about the current AI-ification of the world strikes many of us as fundamentally dissimilar from previous revolutions. Folks are scared about their career prospects. "Should I give up tech and get into farming?" is a sincere question multiple people have asked me. I don't know! Tech is already a goat rodeo, so you've got a leg up if you do?

This anxiety isn't theoretical. In May, Amazon wisely shut down KiroRank, an employee-built cash incineration leaderboard that ranked people by token usage, after the obvious thing happened: people spun up junk tasks to climb the ranks. This is now called "tokenmaxxing," and the Terkowitz treatise specifically calls it out as being at odds with "frugality." Meta did the same thing with "Claudenomics" and 85K employees that same week. And Amazon reportedly announced another 250 job cuts this week. I can't tell you that these things are related. But I can tell you that every engineer who read the memo assumes that they are.

What's the thing say?

This document contains a lot of great stuff. I have a tremendous level of respect, both personal and professional, for Amazon's Principal Engineering community; I'm led to understand that that respect may not be reciprocated, but that's okay. I am, by their standards, a lapsed engineer who ran away to join the circus as one of its lesser clowns.

Terkowitz's memo emphasizes customer outcomes and engineering judgement, two things I would absolutely not delegate to AI any time soon. (Note: I am writing this in October of 2026, with the full understanding that previous comments on the capabilities where AI falls short tend to age like fine milk.) It documents work that (today) AI tools aren't improving. That's way better than assuming that every objection to AI inherently comes from some self-interested Luddite who's scared of being replaced. It also says something smart about measurement: look at the 90th percentile of AI adoption, at those teams burning the most tokens, and check whether their delivery actually went up commensurately. This is great! Shout it from the rooftops; we should all be doing such things.

But still, I wonder.

The memo says “If your work does not benefit from current AI tooling, that is a finding, not a failing.” Document it, bring it to your manager, work through the problem. I agree with every word of that! The question is whether anyone's promo doc is gonna survive citing it.

Here's what happens next. The memo concedes that input metrics are "deeply flawed," but then in the next paragraph says to go ahead and use them anyway, carefully. It then warns that non-adopters are falling behind. Those are three different positions within three sentences and they're not at all aligned with one another.

That's not muddled; I think it's cover. A manager can cite "a finding, not a failing" when a strong engineer skips the AI tool, and then cite "falling behind" when it's time to PIP someone. Both are congruent with their stated policy; you get to decide which interpretation you want to go with in a given situation.

How will managers handle justified AI non-use? Are promotion and performance decisions being made in a way that rewards these long term outcomes? Or are they instead taking the easy weasel way out, optimizing for what's cheap to measure, and rewarding tool activity? Because if you give me a metric, I will turn it into a target. So will an overwhelming majority of employees. When that happens, everyone loses — it's how KiroRank happened in the first place.

Everyone's selling tokens — including Amazon

I write this after two days of attending GitHub Universe, where they talked about how agents start roughly 40% of PRs now. And yet, Jay Parikh’s keynote argument was that useful output wasn't "more code," but rather completed outcomes: features shipped, bugs fixed, security issues addressed, loudmouth columnists silenced, etc. The problem here is that their entire business model cuts against that; they're billing in a model that charges customers on a consumption basis, not an output basis. There's nothing, today, that ties "AI spend" to "output that was worth the effort."

Which brings us back to Amazon. The memo says tokenmaxxing is at odds with frugality, and for Amazon’s internal cost line, it is. Meanwhile AWS bills Bedrock and Kiro customers by the token. Inside the building, tokenmaxxing is a problem to be stamped out. Outside it, it’s the revenue plan. ®

O'Sullivan Skoufoglou Architects creates "quiet yet present" garden studio in London

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

A low brick base anchors the timber structure of this pared-back garden studio, which London studio O'Sullivan Skoufoglou Architects created as a workspace for a creative couple in Highbury.

Nestled in a leafy garden of a Victorian home, the studio provides the filmmaker and artist with a calming, well-lit space with a strong visual connection to the outdoors.

O'Sullivan Skoufoglou Architects slotted it at the end of the garden to preserve views from the house over the mature garden, avoiding "compromising the sense of depth and continuity of the landscape", the studio said.

"It was important that the building felt distinct from the house while sitting comfortably within its setting, a short walk from the rear garden door," studio co-founder Amalia Skoufoglou told Dezeen.

"The intention was to create a building that felt quiet yet present in the garden, rather than competing with it or the Victorian house," she added.

"The form allows the quality of the space to emerge through its proportions, construction and relationship with daylight," Skoufoglou continued. "Its modest scale and subservient position give the studio the character of a garden building."

O'Sullivan Skoufoglou Architects designed the material palette and low profile of the studio to complement the character of the existing house and garden, ensuring minimal visual impact.

Its low brick plinth nods to the brick boundary walls of the Victorian home and also pays homage to Victorian glasshouses, traditionally supported on low brick walls.

Above the brick plinth is a lightweight timber structure, chosen to "allow the upper part of the building to remain visually light", Skoufoglou said.

Inside, the garden studio comprises a main open workspace and an adjoining bathroom. The focal point is its built-in desk that extends along the main east-facing window wall.

"The main window wall and the built-in desk form the focus of the interior. The desk runs alongside the generous east-facing windows, making daylight and the changing garden views an integral part of the working environment," said Skoufoglou.

"This arrangement brings the working surface into direct relationship with natural light and views of the garden."

Inside, the timber structure is left exposed at roof level, while the brick plinth is also left visible. Between, the walls are lined with painted pine.

Smaller windows are positioned strategically around the studio, framing views of trees while maintaining privacy from neighbouring homes.
