Donald Trump made more than $1.4 billion from crypto last year. On Friday, he said he has “always felt” digital money was “a little bit dangerous.”

Both facts are on the record. The first sits in his own financial disclosure. The second came from the Oval Office, a day after hackers stole about $6.3 billion from crypto exchange Binance.

Trump Called Crypto Risky Again on Friday

Reporters asked about the Binance theft. Trump answered by talking about paper checks.

“You know, the old-fashioned way when you used to sign a check… These are different. People never see the money anymore. So I’ve always felt it was a little bit dangerous,” he said.

He has said this before. In July 2019, he wrote that crypto was “not money” and “based on thin air.” In June 2021, he told Fox Business that Bitcoin (BTC) “seems like a scam.” That December, he called crypto “a very dangerous thing.”

Then the family went into business. Trump’s 2025 disclosure lists the income.

The largest line was $636 million from a license deal for the TRUMP meme coin, a token sold on his name and image. Another $515 million (or more) came from token sales at World Liberty Financial, the crypto firm he co-founded with his sons.

World Liberty also issues USD1, a token built to stay worth $1. Most of it has sat on Binance, as BeInCrypto reported in February on Binance’s grip on USD1.

In October 2025, Trump pardoned Binance founder Changpeng Zhao. The White House said the earlier prosecution reflected a “desire to punish the cryptocurrency industry,” according to PBS.

What Americans Think of Trump’s Crypto Money

“We’ll see.”

“The use of music is to remind us how short a time we have a body.”

In a lifetime of living in this body, I have known no more powerful a homecoming than music — nothing roots us more firmly into the house of being, nothing levitates us more buoyantly to that transcendent place beyond marrow and mind. Stripped of its nihilistic drama, there is an elemental cry of truth, for me at least, in Nietzsche’s pronouncement: “Without music life would be a mistake.” Even Edna St. Vincent Millay, for all her lyrical love of life, echoed the sentiment: “Without music I should wish to die. Even poetry, Sweet Patron Muse forgive me the words, is not what music is.”

Every writer unblinded by ego will concede this.

No writer has conceded it more beautifully or with more rapturous reverence for the life of the body in the life of music than Richard Powers in his exquisite 2003 novel The Time of Our Singing (public library).

One of the novel’s protagonists — a young black woman in 1930s Philadelphia — becomes an emissary of the power of music as an instrument of self-discovery and self-possession, a living testament to song as the pulse-beat of the soul:

Delia fell in love with singing. Singing was something that might make sense of a person. Singing might make more sense of life than living had to start with.

Delia sang fearlessly. She threw back her head and nailed free-flying notes like a marksman nails skeet. She sang with such unfurling of self that the congregation couldn’t help but turn and look at the teenager, even when they should have been looking skyward.

Delia could feel them as she sang, the hearts of the flushed congregation flying up with her as she savored the song’s arc. She sheltered those souls in her sound and held them as motionless as the notes themselves, in that safe spot up next to grace. The audience breathed with her, beating to her measure. Her breath expanded sufficiently to take her across even the longest phrase. Her listeners were in her, and she in them, so long as the notes lasted.

Art from German opera singer Lilli Lehmann’s 1902 field guide to singing

When Delia marries a German-Jewish physicist who plays the piano and consider music “the language of time,” music takes on a richer meaning — or, rather, it is stripped down to its elemental raw material — for without the arrow of time, without being able to tell one moment from the next, there could be no melody and no rhythm. This is what makes music our supreme laboratory for feeling and time.

Eventually, the couple’s sons discover music on their own terms, in their own time. One of them — the novel’s first-person narrator — encounters its power and tenderness harmonized in a soul-stilling performance of ancient music by a choir at The Cloisters — the medieval monastery turned museum in the uppermost reaches of Manhattan, just past Harlem. The small boy, untainted with concepts, experiences music in its purest form, pouring out of the singers like daybreak, like something of another world, yet saturated with pure translucent presence, in that peculiar way transcendent experiences have of taking us both beyond and deeper into ourselves:

Silence falls, erasing all separateness. Then the silence gives way to its only answer. This is the first public concert I will remember ever hearing. Nothing I’ve already lived through prepares me for it. It runs through and rearranges me. I sit at the center of a globe of sound pointing me toward myself.

It doesn’t occur to me, at the age of seven, that a person might luck upon such a song only once a lifetime, if ever. I know how to tell sharp from flat, right singing from wrong. But I haven’t yet heard enough to tell ordinary beauty from once-only visits.

There is a sound like the burning sun. A sound like the surf of blood pumping through my ears. The women start by themselves, their note as spreading and dimensionless as my father says the present is. Keee, the letter-box slots of their mouths release — just the syllable of glee little Ruth made before we persuaded her to learn to talk. The sound of a simple creature, startling itself with praise before settling in for the night. They sing together, bound at the core for one last moment before everything breaks open and is born.

Then reee. The note splits into its own accompaniment. The taller woman seems to descend, just by holding her pitch while the smaller woman next to her rises. Rises a major third, that first interval any child any color anywhere learns to sing. Four lips curve upon the vowel, a pocket of air older than the author who set it there.

I know in my body what notes come next, even though I have nothing, yet, to call them. The high voice rises a perfect fifth, lifting off from the lower note’s bed. The lines move like my chest, soft cartilage, my ribs straying away from one another, on aaay, into a higher brightness, then collapsing back to fuse in unison.

I hear these two lines bending space as they speed away from each other, hurling outward, each standing still while the other moves. Long, short-short, long, long: They circle and return, like a blowing branch submitting again to its shadow. They near their starting pitch from opposite sides, the shared spot where they must impossibly meet back up. But just before they synchronize to see where they’ve been, just as they touch their lips to this recovered home, the men’s lines come from nowhere, pair off, and repeat the splitting game, a perfect fourth below.

More lines splinter, copy, and set off on their own. Aaay-laay Aaay-laay-eee! Six voices now, repeating and reworking, each peeling off on its own agenda, syncopated, staggered, yet each with an eye on the other, midair acrobats, not one of them wavering, no one crashing against the host of moving targets. This stripped-down simple singsong blooms like a firework peony. Everywhere in the awakened air, in a shower of staggered entrances, I hear the first phrase, keyed up, melted down, and rebuilt. Harmonies pile up, disintegrate, and reassemble elsewhere, each melody praising God in its own fashion, and everywhere combining to something that sounds to me like freedom.

Tech stocks just lost more than $1 trillion in a day—a gothic horror test for the ‘moats’ Warren Buffett taught Wall Street to love

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Markets are ushering in a particularly gothic Halloween as the AI trade exposes a crumbling aristocratic pile of early 21st century capitalism. Wall Street has been describing itself in castle-adjacent terms for years.

Jamie Dimon has long talked to JPMorgan shareholders about the bank’s “fortress balance sheet.” His 2015 letter set aside a section to answer the question of what the phrase even meant. By 2021, he’d added a second castle word, warning that “the moats that protect this company are not particularly deep.”

The moat came from Warren Buffett. In a 1999 Fortune article with Carol Loomis, he argued that the key to investing is a company’s competitive advantage and how durable it is, and that the businesses worth owning have wide moats around them.

The gothic novel’s favorite picture of danger is a castle falling apart, and for about 250 years the two have traded images: the castle and the moat, the vampire, the creature built in a lab. This Halloween season has them rushing to the front of the conversation, with a two-day rout in tech stocks on Thursday and Friday turning on whether the AI giants’ moats will hold, as gothic horror booms on bookshelves and movie screens.

Horror took 15.5% of the domestic box office in 2025, according to The Numbers, and U.S. horror book sales rose 7% through the first nine months of 2025, per Circana BookScan, while adult fiction overall grew just 1%. The New York Times asked Friday why every book is gothic now, with publishers putting the label on everything from family sagas to space operas, and several film critics commenting that Nathan Fielder’s smash-hit business-scandal documentaryYou Can See Everything is a twisted, even gothic romance in the tradition of Alfred Hitchcock.

The moats get tested

The selling started with Alphabet. The stock fell 12.6% Thursday, wiping out roughly half a trillion dollars in market value, after the Google parent’s quarterly report. Revenue from the core search unit fell short, partly because of the popularity of less-profitable “AI mode” searches and the company burning cash faster than expected. Its free cash flow came in at negative $9.1 billion, against the negative $2.23 billion analysts expected, per FactSet.

The S&P 500’s worst performer was software giant ServiceNow, which dropped 27% the same day on slowing revenue growth and disappointing guidance. By Friday morning, it was Amazon’s and Intel’s turn, with Intel down roughly 25% before the opening bell. Big Tech lost nearly $900 billion in market value after another sharp rise in AI spending in the third quarter, the Financial Times calculated. Nasdaq 100 companies lost more than $1 trillion in market value Friday alone. Reddit, whose shares fell more than 22% premarket Friday, is pulling up the drawbridge: it’s moving to a logged-in experience as AI eats search.

The irony is spooking investors. AI is eating into the businesses that throw off the cash, and the cash is going into AI anyway. The hyperscalers have raised $326 billion in stock and bonds this year to pay for the build-out, more than triple 2025, according to S&P Global Market Intelligence data.

The question underneath it is the one Apollo chief economist Torsten Slok put to clients the morning Alphabet fell. “With AI disruption potentially around the corner, there is a lot of discussion about moats at the moment,” he wrote in his Daily Spark note Thursday.

He built on finance professor Aswath Damodaran, the “dean of valuation,” who compiled a list of sources of competitive advantage, including brand strength, switching costs, network effects, cost advantages and legal protection. Then he boiled it down to an AI moat checklist of three questions: does the company own data its rivals can’t get, is its product part of how customers do their jobs every day, and can it charge more without customers walking away?

Judging by that third test, the companies selling AI don’t seem to measure up. OpenAI’s new GPT-6.1 Terra model costs up to half as much per token as the version before it. An index from expense-management firm Ramp, which looked at what companies actually pay Anthropic and OpenAI, shows token prices down roughly 35% since the end of June, in what Axios called a price war.

The companies paying for AI aren’t getting much back yet, either. In an Oct. 24 note, Slok sent a tremor through Wall Street terminals with an argument that AI “has delivered zero financial payoff to corporate America outside of tech.” Revenue per worker and profit margins are rising for the Magnificent Seven and flat for the rest of the S&P 500, he noted, and mainstream companies are starting to treat token spending as a cost center that weighs on margins rather than a source of growth.

The first castle was a fake

Xavier Aldana Reyes, a researcher at the Manchester Centre for Gothic Studies, told the Times that interest in the gothic tends to surge during periods of social anxiety and political turmoil. Literary agent Mary C. Moore put the genre’s subject more bluntly, telling the paper that “Gothics are all about exposing what is rotten at the core of something shiny/wealthy.”

The genre was invented by a scion of wealth who knew the old order from inside and drew a salary without working at all. Horace Walpole was the son of UK Prime Minister Robert Walpole, and hisThe Castle of Otranto, published in 1764, is generally regarded as the first gothic novel. 

He passed it off as a translation of an obscure Italian work, found in the library of an old Catholic family in northern England and printed at Naples in 1529. But it was really his dramatization of the aristocratic system being usurped by the “greedy” new bourgeoisie, per a 2014 paper by Çankaya University’s Ertuğrul Koç and Neslihan Atcan Altan. The literary historian Robert Miles has written that the modern novel itself was understood to have been revolutionized by print technology, which made the circulating library possible, and explains Walpole’s pre-modern faux origin story.

We all know which gothic tales came next. In her 1831 introduction to Frankenstein, Mary Shelley said the idea came to her in June 1816 after she overheard Lord Byron and Percy Shelley discussing galvanic experiments. A 2018 piece republished by Live Science made the comparison directly: electrical life stirred up the same range of arguments then that AI does now.

Karl Marx made the trade official. In Capital, in 1867, he wrote that capital is “dead labour, that, vampire-like, only lives by sucking living labour,” an image that turns up across 30 years of his writing. Bram Stoker’s Dracula created the great modern vampire myth in 1897, landing as a depression in agriculture threatened the fortunes and estates of Britain’s noblemen, even as a cheap agricultural products flooded in from booming American farms.

The literary critic Franco Moretti read the count as a businessman. In a 1982 essay in New Left Review, he described Stoker’s Dracula as a rational entrepreneur who invests his gold to expand his dominion and conquer the City of London.

The undead on Wall Street

In more recent times, Matt Taibbi’s 2009 Rolling Stone story “The Great American Bubble Machine” called Goldman Sachs “a great vampire squid wrapped around the face of humanity.” The line stuck well enough that then-Goldman CEO Lloyd Blankfein conceded in August of that year that the article had “tapped into something.”

Economists got their own monster: zombie firms. Bank for International Settlements researchers Ryan Banerjee and Boris Hofmann define them as companies at least 10 years old whose earnings haven’t covered their interest payments for three straight years. Across 14 advanced economies, the zombie share of listed non-financial companies rose from 4% in the late 1980s to 15% in 2017. The researchers found that weak banks that roll over loans instead of writing them off keep zombie companies on life support, and that lower interest rates tend to push the zombie share up.

The newest candidates are in software. PitchBook counted $131 billion of leveraged loans priced below 80 cents on the dollar, a common threshold for distress and the most since May 2020. Software companies account for 32% of those distressed loans while making up just 12% of the index, and 84% of stressed software companies are backed by private equity. These are castles that can’t pay their own upkeep, kept standing by somebody else’s money, the dollar-princess arrangement with a credit line instead of a dowry.

The anxiety is big business for the haunted boom of 2026. Book publisher Zando launched a horror imprint, Evil Twin, planning six to eight titles a year. In Hollywood, Warner Bros.’ R-rated body-horror film Clayface opened No. 1 the weekend of Oct. 23 with about $24 million, according to The Numbers.

The machines are writing the stuff now, too. Inkfluence AI, a fiction-writing platform, says 3,929 horror books were started on it in the 180 days to Sept. 12, with haunted-house and ghost premises leading at about a third.

Investors pulled $643.33 million from US spot Bitcoin ETFs on October 30, according to SoSoValue data. The exit came one day after hackers drained about $6.3 billion from Binance, the world’s largest crypto exchange.

These funds let brokerage customers own Bitcoin (BTC) the way they own a stock. They hold $98.14 billion in assets, and Friday’s withdrawal snapped a four-day run of inflows.

Which Bitcoin ETFs Lost the Most Money?

BlackRock’s iShares Bitcoin Trust (IBIT), the largest of the funds, lost about $234 million. Funds run by Fidelity and Ark Invest posted the next-largest exits.

When investors cash out, large trading firms return fund shares in exchange for Bitcoin or cash. Either way, the funds give up coins that can then be sold on the open market.

Ethereum (ETH) funds lost another $156 million the same day. That marked their fifth straight day of outflows.

How Big Was the Binance Hack?

On October 29, an attacker moved roughly $6.36 billion out of Binance’s exchange wallets across 10 blockchains, according to analytics firm TRM. Most of the money left in about 20 minutes.

TRM linked the theft to TraderTraitor, a North Korean hacking unit. The haul is about four times the $1.5 billion Bybit hack of February 2025, the previous record.

Binance suspended deposits and withdrawals and said user funds are safe. Token issuers and network operators have frozen about $2.7 billion of the stolen assets, TRM said.

Is One Day of Withdrawals a Warning Sign?

Friday’s exit equals about 0.7% of the money held in the funds.

A similar exit came on October 22, when investors pulled $635 million in one session. Inflows then returned for four straight days starting October 26.

Bitcoin ETFs daily net flows chart showing the $643.33 million outflow on October 30, Source: SoSoValue

However, the last record hack was followed by heavier selling. A week after the Bybit theft, investors pulled a then-record $1 billion in one day, ETF.com reported. Outflows that week neared $3 billion.

Is Bitcoin Headed for a Second Losing October?

About $11 billion in Bitcoin options, contracts that bet on future prices, also expired on October 30. The Bitcoin price today sits at $77,189, down 1.7% in 24 hours, BeInCrypto data shows.

Monthly return data shared by trader Crypto Rover shows Bitcoin down 7.3% for October so far. The coin also fell 3.95% in October 2025.
