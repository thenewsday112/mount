The 12 strangest science stories we can’t stop thinking about

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →
A male Amazon river dolphin (Inia geoffrensis) urinating into the air

There are some stories that stay with you forever, the big scientific moments that change the course of history. These are not those moments – but we can’t stop thinking about them anyway.

Push my button

Surgeon Stuart Meloy was performing a pain-relief operation on a woman’s spine when she started exclaiming emphatically. “You’re going to have to teach my husband to do that,” the woman said. This inspired Meloy to invent an implant to help women who are unable to achieve orgasms in the usual way. New Scientist broke the news in 2001, sparking a frenzy of worldwide media excitement. But 13 years later, Meloy was still struggling to raise funds for a full clinical trial, and the “orgasmatron” never reached a climax.

Monkey pleasure

A long-tailed macaque Shutterstock/fiz_zero

We aren’t the only primates capable of coming up with creative ways to pleasure ourselves. In a study of long-tailed macaques (Macaca fascicularis) in Bali, Indonesia, Camilla Cenni at the University of Lethbridge in Alberta, Canada, and her colleagues filmed hundreds of examples of male and female monkeys using stone tools as sex toys, rubbing or tapping them on their genitals. According to Cyril Grueter at the University of Western Australia, the Balinese monkeys get plenty of food handouts from humans, and this frees up time for sexual experimentation. But some monkeys in other places have unusual kinks, too: another study reported a wild macaque in Japan rubbing its genitals on the back of a sika deer.

Head transplants

In 2015, surgeon Sergio Canavero announced plans to carry out the first human head transplant, intended to extend the lives of people with muscle and nerve degeneration or widespread cancer. He claimed his technique could overcome technical hurdles, including how to fuse the spinal cord and prevent the body’s immune system from rejecting the head. In the following two years, Canavero published animal experiments supposedly backing up his methods, but other surgeons remained highly sceptical. A human head transplant never did happen, but Canavero has reportedly moved on to an even bolder ambition: transplanting a brain into a non-sentient clone.

Rats driving

Have you ever wondered if a rat can drive a car? Kelly Lambert at the University of Richmond in Virginia has. She and her colleagues built a tiny car with three copper bars that would steer it left, forward or right. Sure enough, 18 rats learned to drive towards food rewards. They even found it relaxing, as measurements of their stress hormones indicated. Lambert proposed that driving challenges could replace traditional maze tests when using rat models to study neuropsychiatric conditions.

The sniff test

Humans are more similar to rats than we like to think. In a psychology experiment, Noam Sobel and his colleagues at the Weizmann Institute of Science in Rehovot, Israel, surreptitiously filmed 153 volunteers. After shaking hands with someone of the same sex, people smelled their shaking hand more than twice as much as they did before the handshake. Puzzlingly, after shaking hands with someone of the opposite sex, people were more likely to sniff their non-shaking hand. The team suspected that humans use chemical signals all the time without being aware of it.

Urine fountains

Communication through scent is, of course, widespread in mammals, but some never-before-seen behaviour in dolphins took biologists by surprise in 2025. While studying Amazon river dolphins (Inia geoffrensis), or botos, in Brazil, Claryana Araújo-Wang at CetAsia Research Group in Ontario, Canada, saw a male “flip his belly up out of the water, expose his penis and then proceed to urinate into the air”, she told New Scientist. They saw dolphins do this a total of 36 times, and most of the time another male was nearby and would pursue the stream of urine. The purpose of this behaviour hasn’t yet been established, but hormones in urine might signal a male’s social position or physical condition, said Araújo-Wang.

Giving the finger

As for human modes of communication, many of us struggle to put our smartphones down – so the last thing we’d want is for them to crawl across the table towards us. Nevertheless, Marc Teyssier at the University of Paris-Saclay in France and his colleagues gave phones this ability in 2018, with a robotic appendage dubbed the MobiLimb. It plugs into a device’s USB port and, for some reason, looks eerily like a human finger, giving it a spooky quality reminiscent of Thing from The Addams Family. The MobiLimb can stroke your wrist, wave, wag like a tail or, most disconcertingly, drag the phone around. Thankfully, the idea hasn’t taken off.

Horror frog

A taxidermied male hairy frog Gustavocarra/CC BY-SA 4.0

If you find the thought of a phone with a finger horrifying, wait until you hear about the nightmare-inducing hairy frog Trichobatrachus robustus. In 2008, researchers reported that these frogs can break their own bones to produce claws that burst through their skin. David Blackburn at Harvard University’s Museum of Comparative Zoology thought these weapons were for defence, but two years later, another team argued that they were used in fights between males – and that the frog’s unusual hairs were a kind of armour to protect its vulnerable parts from sharp claws.

Corpse shrinking

In 1957, when New Scientist was 1 year old, we reported on a new method for shrinking an adult human body to the size of a baby. At an exhibition of inventors in Winnipeg, Canada, the unnamed inventor of this process claimed it would make disposing of dead bodies simpler, as they could be slid into underground tubes and take up less space. The method involved applying high pressure to the corpse and squeezing out fluids so its flesh could be compressed into a mould. “All this is, of course, highly ingenious but it is uncertain why the inventor thinks that his newly proposed form of burial is in any way better than the current and very popular method of cremation,” the writer mused.

The brain has its own microbiome. Here’s what it means for your health

Transient anus

The warty comb jelly has an anus that comes and goes Andrey Nekrasov/imageBROKER.com​/Alamy

We may take it for granted, but the anus, which allows food to pass through the body, was an evolutionary breakthrough, both figuratively and literally. The earliest animals, like jellyfish today, had only one gut opening, where food went in and waste came out. In 2019, scientists discovered that the warty comb jelly (Mnemiopsis leidyi, pictured top) forms an anal opening when it needs to defecate, but it vanishes straight afterwards. This, they suggested, could represent how the anus evolved in the first place.

Fart science

Flatus – gas expelled from the anus – has been a topic of endless fascination for many readers (and journalists). In 2001, we featured the work of Michael Levitt at the Veterans Administration Medical Center in Minneapolis, a leading authority on the subject. He developed a “Toot Trapper” that reduces odours by trapping certain gases using activated charcoal. We also reported on a device for monitoring gas emissions from dogs, used to test dog snacks that were supposed to reduce flatulence. In 2016, researchers in Australia worked out which foods cause the smelliest farts by mixing faeces with different foods. They recommended eating less protein and more slow-release carbohydrates to minimise hydrogen sulphide production. In 2022, Nagarajan Kannan at the Mayo Clinic in Rochester, Minnesota, and his colleagues worked out that some poos float because of gas from gut microbes, confirming a hypothesis first put forward by Levitt 50 years earlier.

Brew your own

Even stranger things go on inside some people’s intestines, as we found out in 2019. A 46-year-old man started experiencing unexplained mental fogginess, dizziness and memory loss, and had to give up his job. Later, doctors found high levels of alcohol in his blood when he hadn’t been drinking. Eventually, they realised his gut contained high levels of Saccharomyces cerevisiae – brewer’s yeast – which got him drunk any time he ate a lot of carbohydrates. This rare condition, known as auto brewery syndrome, had been triggered when the man took antibiotics for a thumb injury, which disrupted his gut microbiome. This year, a study reported that another man with this condition fully recovered after getting a transplant of faeces from a healthy donor. Tether, Circle and Ripple could freeze their tokens within hours of the Binance theft, and they did.

There is no one to call for the stolen bitcoin, ether and TRX, which makes up most of the money Binance is unlikely to get back.

In most hacks, the stolen total is the headline. In the Binance breach, the more useful number is how much of the stolen money could be stopped.

On-chain trackers estimate the stolen basket at more than $5.6 billion. Binance itself has put the estimated impact at $6.3 billion and says nearly $3 billion is frozen. That freeze was possible only because of how the stolen money was made up.

Almost half of it was in tokens whose issuers can freeze them. The rest was in assets that no company, foundation or council can stop on their main blockchains.

That second half explains why recovery stalled at about $3 billion despite a fast industry response. It is also the part Binance will most likely have to pay for itself.

A $5.6 Billion Basket Split Almost Down The Middle

The per-asset breakdown from on-chain trackers is:

Sorting by whether the issuer can freeze the token gives a near-even split:

  • Tokens with a freeze button: USDT, USDC, RLUSD, WBETH and XAUT add up to about $2.61 billion, or roughly 46% of the $5.6 billion total.
  • Assets with no freeze button: BTC, ETH and TRX add up to about $2.56 billion, or roughly 46%.
  • The remaining $447.5 million is a mix of the two kinds, from stablecoins to ordinary DeFi tokens.

That one division shaped everything that happened after the theft.

The Issuers Who Could Hit Pause

The response on the freezable side was unusually fast:

  • Tether: CEO Paolo Ardoino said the company froze more than 1.567 billion USDT "related to a potential Binance exchange hack." That is close to the full $1.57 billion of USDT in the trackers' estimate.
  • Circle: CEO Jeremy Allaire said that, "in coordination with law enforcement," Circle had frozen more than 800M USDC associated with the incident.
  • Ripple: Jack McDonald, who leads Ripple's stablecoin business, said the company was "actively freezing affected funds" in RLUSD tied to the attackers.
  • World Liberty Financial: CEO Zach Witkoff said USD1 had moved to freeze the $27 million in USD1 linked to the hack. Binance co-CEO Yi He said a further $2 million in WLFI was also frozen.
  • First Digital: CEO Vincent Chok said the company had frozen the FDUSD involved and was working with Binance to stop the stolen funds from being moved.
  • WBETH and XAUT also appeared among the frozen assets. WBETH is Binance's own wrapped staked ether, and XAUT is Tether's gold-backed token.

The pattern is the same in every case. A company that issues a token on a public blockchain usually keeps a way to block specific addresses from moving it. Critics see that power as a central point of control, but in this theft it worked. Within hours, the largest dollar-value block of the stolen money could not be moved.

There was one notable intervention outside the token issuers. On Arbitrum, the Arbitrum Security Council took emergency action and moved 74,559,805 ARB and 8,425 ETH, about $30.5 million, out of an address connected to the exploit.

It shows that some layer-2 networks keep emergency powers their base layers lack. The amount was small next to the size of the theft.

The Assets Nobody Can Freeze

The other half is a different story. Bitcoin has no issuer. Ether on Ethereum's main network has no admin key. TRX is Tron's native coin, and no token issuer sits between the holder and the blockchain. Once these assets reached the attacker's wallets, stopping them depended on exchanges refusing deposits and investigators tracking the funds, not on any freeze function.

Together, the $1.45 billion in BTC, $875 million in ETH and $234 million in TRX make about $2.56 billion. That is close to Binance's own numbers: about $3 billion frozen out of a $6.3 billion estimated impact leaves roughly $3.3 billion still in play, and most of it is in assets nobody can freeze.

The bitcoin figure is the most contested part of the basket. Some BTC moving out of Binance-linked wallets during the incident may have been Binance moving its own reserves to safety rather than attacker withdrawals. So the true unfreezable total could be lower than $2.56 billion, but the split between the two groups is unlikely to change much.

The Attacker Raced To Swap Freezable For Unfreezable

The attacker appeared to understand this difference and acted on it right away.

On-chain records show the main sweep on Ethereum used an EIP-7702 delegation. This feature lets a regular wallet hand control to a smart contract. Within minutes, the drained hot wallet's contents, including more than 1.6 billion USDT, had moved to attacker-controlled addresses.

The clearest example of the next step is on Aave. According to Aave's governance forum update from risk manager LlamaRisk, one wallet in the exploiter's cluster acted between 20:58 and 21:01 UTC:

  • It supplied 60 million USDT to Aave V3 on Ethereum.
  • It borrowed 19,762 WETH, about $44.6 million.

That trade left freezable USDT locked inside Aave as collateral and gave the attacker wrapped ether, which no issuer can freeze. The position stays over-collateralized, and LlamaRisk said it "does not put Aave suppliers at risk." The ETH, however, was already gone.

Aave responded on a large scale. Between 21:42 and 23:04 UTC, its guardian multisigs froze:

  • Aave V3: 175 reserves across 16 networks.
  • Aave V4: 117 reserves across four networks.

The freezes blocked new deposits and borrowing. Reports also describe stablecoins being swapped for ETH on Uniswap.

The race was easy to see in the timeline. Each hour before an issuer froze its token was another hour to swap that token for ether. Tether and Circle reacted within the first hour, so most of the stablecoin stash was frozen before it could be converted.

Why Frozen Is Not The Same As Recovered

A freeze stops the attacker, but it does not put money back in Binance's accounts. Frozen tokens stay locked at the attacker's addresses until the issuer decides what to do with them. In past cases, issuers have destroyed frozen tokens and reissued the same amount to the rightful owner, usually after legal or law-enforcement checks. That process takes time, and each issuer has its own procedure.

Binance has said that its initial investigation points to a compromised DevOps engineer's laptop. According to the company, that laptop let the attacker reach a service container and submit unauthorized withdrawal transactions. Binance said it found no evidence that wallet private keys were leaked, and that it has revoked the affected address authorization and is working with law enforcement and blockchain intelligence firms.

Deposits and withdrawals were suspended while security checks continue. Binance has not yet given a date for reopening them.

What The Split Means For Binance's Bill

Binance has said it will cover all losses using its SAFU fund and its own money. Grouping the stolen basket by freezability gives a rough picture of what that bill could be.

The roughly $3 billion that is frozen should eventually be recovered, depending on how and when each issuer releases or reissues the tokens. The bitcoin, ether and TRX are another matter. Unless exchanges block deposits and investigators recover funds as they are laundered, that part is effectively gone. It accounts for most of the money Binance will have to cover from its own reserves.

The theft also raises a question for the whole industry. The assets that limited the damage were the issuer-controlled tokens that crypto purists often criticize. The assets that caused the real loss were the ones built so that no one can stop them. Exchanges have long decided hot-wallet sizes by liquidity needs. After this theft, they may also consider which assets in those wallets can still be stopped once they leave.

Dowman ban upheld, football’s rules need to change

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Morning.

Let’s start again with Max Dowman whose three match ban was upheld after Arsenal’s appeal against the red card failed. The club had made the bid on the basis of wrongful dismissal and excessive punishment, but the claim was dismissed by an independent regulatory commission who will publish their ‘written reasons’ in due course. Thanks for that.

You will already know I think this is too severe. I thought the sending off was harsh, especially with the video evidence that backed the appeal, and for a player to miss three games for what was such a minor reaction to a heavy challenge is really unfair. And I would say that if this were an Arsenal player or not. It’s another example of how we hear about the concept of player protection all the time, but the reality is there have been countless incidents which could have been used to demonstrate there’s a seriousness about that but it never, ever happens.

How absurd is it that Max Dowman will serve the same ban as Ryan Shawcross got for snapping Aaron Ramsey’s leg in two? Remember when Dan Smith launched himself at Abou Diaby’s ankle, putting him out of the game for 9 months and causing an injury so severe it plagued the rest of the Frenchman’s career? He didn’t even get a red card that day, so the next time you see Dermot Gallagher pontificating on Sky about how a referee got an obviously wrong decision right, apply that context (he was the ref at the Stadium of Light that day).

Football has a problem with actual violent conduct, but chooses to deal with it by trying to make us think performative infractions like Dowman’s are worthy of the same punishment as the acts of real violence. The dangerous tackles, the two-footed leaps (which seem to creeping back into the game a bit, probably because they’re not being punished), pushing an opposition player when they’re in the air, these things are not on the same scale. If what Dowman did is worthy of a three game ban, the next time someone goes studs first over the ball into the shins of their opponent, they should get ten games at least.

But we know that’s not going to happen. Almost as long as this blog has been going (coming up on 25 years now!), I’ve made the case for there to be a sliding scale of punishment for acts that meet the definition of violent conduct. Some things are just more violent than others, but as long as this blanket punishment of three games regardless of the seriousness of the incident is in place, the more those in charge do the game and the players a disservice.

Still, it is what it is now, and Arsenal will have to live without Dowman for the games against Liverpool, Hull City, and the away trip to Newcastle on the far side of the upcoming November Interlull. He could well have been involved at some point this weekend against Liverpool, but now we’re sweating on the fitness of Noni Madueke, with Eberechi Eze the only other option for the left hand side. There are some whispers that Christos Tzolis could be back in contention for Sunday, which would be a very welcome boost, but the forward line looks a bit light for a game of this significance.

Tuesday’s game against Fleetwood will live in the memory because of the giant killing aspect, but for two young Academy players, it’s a night that will hopefully be formative. For Dowman, whether you think what he did was worthy of a red card or not, there’s a lesson to learn about how you react. To be quite honest, I think there’s something to like about the way he was prepared to stick up for himself against a grown man who wanted to ‘leave a bit on him’, as they say. The talented players are the ones who get kicked most though, and it’s something he’s going to have to contend with as his career develops. Just ask Bukayo Saka. The two have plenty of time on their hands right now, and I’m sure it will be part of his footballing education.

The other player is Marli Salmon who probably should have done better on their goal, but for me is less culpable overall than some of the senior players who didn’t perform at the right level. Arsene Wenger once said:

You pay for the education of young players with points. If I play a 20-year old centre back, I know he’ll cost me points during the season and I have to stand up for that. A less talented 28 year old would cost me less points. However, by 23/24 you have a player.

Marli Salmon is 17. He didn’t cost us any points. There’s a long way still go in his development, but I’m sure with the coaching staff they’ll go over what happened against Fleetwood and he will learn from it. It is more difficult for young defenders than young attackers too. A mistake at the top end of the pitch, like Dowman scuffing a shot wide from a position he probably ought to score from, doesn’t feel quite as costly as a forward running off a young defender to score the opening goal of the game. But again, it’s all part of the process, every young player makes mistakes, it’s how you then use them to improve that counts, and I’m sure he’ll do that.

Josh Shapiro aims for a strong finish in a campaign with 2028 implications

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

POCONO RACEWAY, Pennsylvania — High up in the starting stand of a famed race track, Josh Shapiro waves a black-and-white checkered flag with an unmistakeable meaning: It’s over.

The metaphor isn’t entirely accurate.

“We may have a lead, but you're coming down this home stretch. People are nipping at your heels,” the Pennsylvania governor told POLITICO. “You’ve got to run hard.”

Shapiro, the potential 2028 contender and 2024 veepstakes short-lister, does have reasons to be optimistic — and careful.

He’s threatening a double-digit blowout over Stacy Garrity, a candidate backed by President Donald Trump, in the most important swing state in the nation.

He’s also putting his coattails to the test.

None of the other potential 2028 Democratic candidates has more at stake in their own backyard on Tuesday than Shapiro. He doesn’t have California Gov. Gavin Newsom’s map-breaking Proposition 50, designed to beat back the GOP redistricting, nor his platinum email list. He doesn’t have Illinois Gov. JB Pritzker’s billions or Arizona Sen. Mark Kelly’s deep bench of donors or former Transportation Secretary Pete Buttigieg’s large map or the red-state credentials of Kentucky Gov. Andy Beshear.

Shapiro is on the brink of flipping four hotly contested congressional seats — and guaranteeing Democrats the House. Pennsylvania Democrats could also take control of the state Senate, including some heavily gerrymandered seats, giving the party a governing trifecta for the first time since 1992.

“I’m not thinking about this as a victory for me,” he told POLITICO during a swing through the state. “I'm thinking about this as a victory for our country.”

All of which paves the way for him to pass a $15 minimum wage and a raft of other measures that could buttress a 2028 presidential bid.

Shapiro is very much following the Ron DeSantis theory of a presidential primary campaign: demonstrate dominance at home before branching out nationally — a strategy, it should be noted, that didn’t pay off for the Florida governor.

“I think what really hurt DeSantis was that he decided he wasn't going to announce until after his legislative session, and that was May,” political strategist David Axelrod said. “Time is at a premium. If that’s his plan, that would be a miscalculation.”

Will we see him venture beyond Pennsylvania’s boundaries before the end of the year?

“I don’t know what my plans are,” Shapiro said.

The day had the feeling of both an ending of one campaign and the beginning of another. Even as he urged a midday audience of about 100 people to vote for Paige Cognetti, the Scranton mayor running against Rep. Rob Bresnahan, and Brian Wrightson, the state Senate candidate challenging Sen. Rosemary Brown, he is nationalizing his rhetoric.

“Don’t let Trump take away your hopefulness,” he told people gathered at East Stroudsburg University. “Don't let him make you feel pessimistic. Don't let him feel like change can't come or that we have to look in the rearview mirror for answers. The answers are always straight ahead.”

Then there’s his set piece about the “American dream slipping away.”

He’s broadening out his message beyond the commonwealth.

“I think the work I do here in Pennsylvania, the confidence I give them about their government here can hopefully restore some of their faith in democracy, and I want in our democracy, and I want them to feel like they've got power to be able to shape the future of that American dream,” Shapiro told us.

A few hours later, Shapiro materializes inside Salerno’s, a tavern in Old Forge known for its American cheese-topped pizza. He sets a room in which patrons already have had a few too many Rolling Rocks and pumpkin martinis abuzz.

On the televisions, his own ad for Wrightson is playing on TV. He grips and grins and backslaps his way through the room.

At the end of the bar, he picks up five pizzas for the road, ducks out the door and boards his bus for another Cognetti rally.

Shapiro’s gubernatorial reelect isn’t drawing the buzz of some of the more razor’s-edge, online Senate races in the waning days of election season. But his project is no less remarkable.

“If we can win enough seats here in Pennsylvania to change the balance of power in Washington and be a check on Donald Trump, then we can both hold the president accountable and get stuff done in Washington,” Shapiro says. “Likewise, here in Pennsylvania, if we can win a trifecta for the first time since Bill Clinton's election, I can raise wages almost immediately for tens of thousands of Pennsylvanians who are at or below minimum wage and raise wages for others. That's a concrete thing that we can accomplish.”

At the raceway, he has just finished cutting a video racing around the Pocono Raceway’s Tricky Triangle when he flashes a grin.

“I’ve got a surprise for you,” he says, inviting POLITICO to the starting stand above the storied three-turn NASCAR track in the Pocono foothills.

Up the stairs, the burnt-orange fall foliage dances on the Pennsylvania horizon. It’s a vantage point Shapiro, as a NASCAR fan, has taken in many times.

Despite the apt setting, he deflects when asked if he’s ready to announce the launch of his campaign.

“If this is a metaphor,” he says, “it's for finishing the race strong.”

There is just one wrinkle: Before he brandished the black-and-white flag, he picked up a green one, waving it vigorously.

“I think you mis-saw that,” Shapiro said with a smile. “I was waving a checkered flag to finish the governor’s race.”
