Court Blocks W. Va. AG's Subpoena to Environmentalist Nonprofits

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

From yesterday's decision by Judge Joseph Goodwin (S.D. W. Va.) in W. Va. Rivers Coalition, Inc. v. McCuskey:

The Attorney General of West Virginia has used the State's power to compel two citizen organizations to open ten years of their files: their drafts and internal arguments, the groups they have funded, the people who serve on their boards, the coalitions they have joined, and what they have told the agencies they petition. Neither organization markets goods nor services relevant to the subpoenas. The Attorney General identifies no consumer who was cheated, no sale, and no purchase made in reliance on anything either group said.

What these organizations do is argue in public about what coal, oil, and gas do to West Virginia's waters and climate. The State's chief legal officer, who has publicly condemned that advocacy, now demands to know how they reached their views and who stood with them.

The First Amendment forbids a State from using its investigative power that way, and I will not allow it while this case is pending.

The country was founded on the premise that a citizen owes the government no account of his opinions or of the people he holds them with. The Attorney General has demanded that two citizen organizations hand the State their working lives.

The State wants a decade of what they published, down to a note sent to a single person outside the organization, along with the unreleased drafts and the internal arguments over what to say. He demanded the names of the organizations that fund them and what each gave, and he has now withdrawn that demand. He still wants the groups they paid, the board members they share, the coalitions they joined, and the comments they filed with the agencies they were petitioning. Because the demand period runs to the date of compliance, the subpoenas reach new communications written while they remain outstanding. No demand identifies a consumer, a sale, or a statement anyone relied on. The subpoenas go into how these citizens arrived at their positions on public questions and who helped them get there.

The Plaintiffs in this case, West Virginia Rivers Coalition, Inc. ("WVRC") and 350.org, promote clean waters, reduced carbon emissions, and healthier lives for the people of West Virginia and beyond. They do not sell any service. They do not advertise a certain product of their own. Instead, they promote ideas. Some of that advocacy and some of those ideas are based, in part, on one model. For that, they have been subpoenaed and asked to turn over ten years' worth of information far beyond any use of that one model.

AG McCuskey's actions violate the protections afforded by the United States Constitution. The Plaintiffs, on this record, have only engaged in protected advocacy speech, not commercial speech. The subpoenas issued on the Plaintiffs trespass on their freedom of association, chill their exercise of the First Amendment, and deter others from engaging in similar conduct.

Because Plaintiffs are likely to succeed on the merits of their claim that the subpoenas violate their First Amendment freedom of association, and because the remaining injunction factors also favor Plaintiffs, their Motion for a Temporary Restraining Order, is GRANTED.

Attorney General McCuskey is enjoined from enforcing these investigative subpoenas for the next fourteen days as set out in the Conclusion of this Memorandum Opinion and Order….

A bit more on the plaintiffs:

Plaintiff WVRC is a river advocacy nonprofit corporation organized under the laws of West Virginia, and its "mission is to promote clean and healthy West Virginia waters for all." The nonprofit is committed to "clean water and healthy rivers through research, public education, policy advocacy, citizen water-quality monitoring, and engagement with state and federal agencies." It has a modest staff of 15 and an annual budget of approximately $2 million primarily funded by foundations and other organizations. WVRC regularly submits comments on proposed legislation, permits, and rules concerning water quality. And as this court is familiar, WVRC is a party litigant in multiple cases across the Fourth Circuit that challenge permits and approvals for infrastructure projects, pipeline projects, injection wells, and toxic discharges. Because of its litigation efforts, WVRC coordinates "with other organizations through shared counsel" about litigation strategy.

WVRC also engages in public education and information campaigns, including "statements about the effects of climate change in West Virginia's waters and communities, and about the environmental effects of fossil-fuel extraction, transport, and combustion." It is a member of multiple different coalitions committed to similar goals. To fund ts work, WVRC "solicits charitable contributions from the public through its website, mailings, emails, and events," but it does not publish the identities of its funders.

Plaintiff 350.org is a nonprofit corporation, and its "mission is to build a grassroots movement to end the use of fossil fuels and to accelerate the transition to renewable energy." The organization publishes information about climate change, organizes public demonstrations, and coordinates with other organizations with similar goals. 350.org also "solicits charitable contributions from the public through grant applications and through its website, mailings, emails, and events." Those grants and contributions are subject to opt-in confidentiality in some cases and agreed upon confidentiality in other cases.

And an excerpt from the court's First Amendment analysis (for more on RCP8.5, see this N.Y. Times article):

[T]he Court applies "exacting scrutiny" to compelled disclosures of protected First Amendment speech and association. To be a permissible intrusion on the First Amendment, "there must be 'a substantial relation between the disclosure requirement and a sufficiently important governmental interest.'" …

The subpoenas … "… concern[ ] an investigation into alleged unfair or deceptive acts or practices and other possible violations of the West Virginia Consumer Credit and Protection Act ('WVCCPA')." State code authorizes the Attorney General, if he "has probable cause," to "subpoena witnesses, compel their attendance, adduce evidence, and require the production of any matter which is relevant to the investigation."

Under West Virginia law, "[u]nfair methods of competition and unfair or deceptive acts or practices in the conduct of any trade or commerce are … unlawful." Trade or commerce "means the advertising, offering for sale, sale or distribution of any goods or services and shall include any trade or commerce, directly or indirectly, affecting the people of this state." "Unfair methods of competition and unfair or deceptive acts or practices" includes 16 different definitions including "[e]ngaging in any other conduct which similarly creates a likelihood of confusion or of misunderstanding."

Deploying these broad definitions, AG McCuskey's theory is that the Plaintiffs, environmental advocacy groups, published information, made comments, and advocated based on a study, the RCP8.5, that has been determined "implausible." According to AG McCuskey, this reliance, and subsequent advocacy, is a deceptive act or practice that affects the energy marketplace of West Virginia.

Begin with common sense—in what way could WVRC and 350.org violate the WVCCPA? They are not financial institutions, lenders, or creditors. They are not businesses seeking to make a profit by engaging with consumers. In fact, both are 501(c)(3) nonprofit organizations. They do not sell services of any kind. As explained, Plaintiffs are advocacy organizations committed to climate issues. Their missions are public education, dissemination of information, advocacy in support of their ideals, and public support of those who align with their beliefs. What little items they sell are merchandise bearing their names and logos. Surely these minimal sales from advocacy organizations are not the transactions the West Virginia legislature sought to protect consumers from….

Plaintiffs do not engage in commercial speech. By virtue of their missions as nonprofit environmental advocacy groups, their interests are not economic. Their interests include public education, funding environmental projects, modeling and projecting the effects of climate change, and contributing to a society that values clean air, clean water, and healthier lives. The fact that their advocacy touches on the economy is insufficient to be commercial speech. Therefore, Plaintiffs' protected speech and advocacy alleged in this case cannot be regulated under the WVCCPA.

Accordingly, I find that the WVCCPA cannot supply a "sufficiently important governmental interest" under the "exacting scrutiny" applied to freedom of association claims. I next turn to the severity of the "disclosure requirement" under the "exacting scrutiny" analysis….

As discussed, the subpoenas broadly assert that a violation of the WVCCPA might have occurred. And in his response, AG McCuskey admits that there may not be any WVCCPA violation. He does, however, have authority to issue subpoenas to investigate "whether a violation … has, in fact, occurred."

That violation, AG McCuskey argues, arises from only a few instances of Plaintiffs using a certain climate projection model, RCP8.5. Any publication born from that model, he asserts, is potentially unfair or deceptive. So to assess the breadth of the subpoenas and the compelled disclosure Plaintiffs are subjected to, the court turns to the subpoenas' demands. It is reasonable to assume that the demands would roughly track the RCP8.5-related violations and the allegations of unfair or deceptive trade practices.

Not so. The subpoenas ask for much more. The 16 categories of demands include every statement Plaintiffs have ever published about a West Virginia energy business with a "Climate Claim." But that demand is not limited to climate-related statements supported by the RCP8.5 model. It includes every climate-related statement that names a West Virginia energy business which is published and supported or informed, at least in part, by any model, study, project, plan, projection, or paper. Demand No. 4 is similarly broad: the subpoena demands every published projection concerning West Virginia and all documents related to those projections, without narrowing it down to those supported by the RCP8.5.

The rest of the demands do not even try to mask their broad and sweeping intentions. Donation requests, payments to third parties, and licensing and funding documents of all projects concerning West Virginia are demanded. Every document projecting costs and damages is demanded, as well as every report, study, model, or paper that the Plaintiffs directly or indirectly supported. Documents pertaining to internal considerations are demanded wholesale. So, too, are all documents pertaining to third-party communication and coordination in any fashion. And even with AG McCuskey's last-minute withdrawal of certain demands for donor information, Plaintiffs will still be obligated to disclose "board affiliation, coalition partners, grantees, agency submissions, internal deliberations and drafts, solicitation totals and targeting."

Plaintiffs call this a fishing expedition. In part, I agree. The subpoenas demand a large share of documents in Plaintiffs' control for the last ten years. But even on a fishing expedition, hoping to just get one bite in the vast and quiet water, a person brings a fishing pole. And in other cases, perhaps, a net. Here, AG McCuskey has abandoned both the fishing pole and the net. Instead, he has brought dynamite to the lake. Tossing a couple in, he hopes to catch something.

As stated, compelled disclosure that invades First Amendment protections can only stand if there is a substantial relation between what must be disclosed and a "sufficiently important governmental interest." Here, I find neither a sufficiently important government interest nor do I find a substantial relationship between the compelled disclosure and the government interest….

And the court also briefly notes plaintiffs' retaliation claim, without resolving it:

Plaintiffs have alleged substantial evidence to support their [retaliation] claim. AG McCuskey has publicly disparaged pro-environmental groups and has called for the DOJ to investigate similar groups. He has joined contentious litigation against environmental advocacy organizations. And the chasm between the subpoenas' demands and the purported basis for investigation suggests a retaliatory motive.

This phenomenon of state and federal investigation into the affairs of advocacy non-profits has begun to permeate our culture. The courts have enjoined civil investigative demands on comparable records. [Citations omitted. -EV]

However, because retaliation concerns the Attorney General's motive, I will reserve judgment on Count One until he can present evidence at a hearing for a preliminary injunction. Having already determined that an injunction is warranted on the basis of Count Two (freedom of association), the court declines to address the remaining counts….

Derek Teaney and Amanda Demmerle (Appalachian Mountain Advocates, Inc.) represent plaintiffs.

‘In Over Their Heads’: Red Flags Before the Sudden Collapse of ‘Perpetual Cruise’ That Left Residents Stranded

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Kit Cassingham credits her yoga practice and her experience as a life coach with keeping her sane these days. 

And Cassingham has a lot of reasons to feel crazy: Earlier this week, she and her husband Randy were told to permanently disembark from the Villa Vie Residences Odyssey cruise ship they’d been living on for the last two years. 

The Cassinghams, along with around 200 or so fellow passengers, were told that the Villa Vie Odyssey had run out money and could no longer afford fuel, so passengers would need to collect their belongings and find another place to live. 

Their odyssey with the Odyssey started more than two years ago, when Cassingham and her husband decided to sell the dream home they had built in Colorado. 

“Randy realized he was allergic to Colorado,” Cassingham told Realtor.com. “He was allergic to four-legged animals, mold, dust, grasses, weeds, pinyon and juniper trees. And we lived in a pinyon-juniper forest. His allergies got worse and worse, and even his allergists couldn't stay on top of it.”

So, the couple decided a never-ending cruise was the one place where Randy might be safe from the scourge of grass and tree allergies.

The Cassinghams initially put a deposit down with Victoria Cruises before realizing that the company didn’t actually own a ship (and still doesn’t, though it continues to sell cabins).

By then, they had already sold their home and taken off on a pre-cruise road trip to say farewell to friends. So they then began looking for other options. 

Enter Villa Vie Residence’s Odyssey, where passengers were promised the opportunity to cruise for the rest of their lives, if they wanted. 

The Cassinghams purchased a cabin with a balcony on the Odyssey’s 7th floor deck, for $369,000. Monthly charges, which covered housekeeping, laundry, fuel, and three meals a day, were $8,000, though the couple purchased stock in the company, which gave them “founder” status and knocked their monthly fees down to $6,000. 

From the start, things were a bit rocky. The Odyssey was supposed to leave in May 2024, but mechanical problems kept the ship docked in Belfast until October 2024. 

That's when she says she began noticing that the Villa Vie’s owners were in over their heads. 

“We were we're gonna go up to Amazon, and it turns out our boat was too big,” she says. “And they just weren't paying attention to some details.”

Cassingham believes delays leaving Belfast put the company on the backfoot. 

“Because we were in Belfast so long, they spent a lot more money on the problems. Every time they would fix one thing, two other problems would come up. So they went into deep debt in Belfast.”

And then when the Iran War broke out in February and the Strait of Hormuz closed, “our fuel bills doubled and then tripled. Yeah. And so if you already are spending more than you're making, having it double and triple was insane. They did ask several times for us to pay fuel charges.”

Through it all, Cassingham says, “the mood on the ship stayed strong because we were all doing what we loved and wanted to do.”

The ship got to the Philippines days after a deadly typhoon hit the country, and the passengers held a fundraiser and contributed to care packages for victims. 

“We're doing this kind of community stuff, and have no problem raising money for that. We just got tired of raising money for stuff we thought we were already paying for.”

Things took a turn, though, when the ship hit Thailand, and Petterson told passengers that he wouldn’t leave the country until he had secured an investor. 

“Next thing we know, he's pulling up the stakes, and off we go to Cambodia where there's no Starlink, the cell service is bad, and the fuel is expensive.”

According to Villa Vie’s estimates, per-cabin fuel expenses rose from $1,800 to between $6,000 and $8,000 per month, putting “its current operating model under unsustainable financial pressure.”

“Since the conflict [in Iran] began in late February, we have taken significant steps to reduce fuel consumption, including slowing the itinerary and spending more time in port,” the company’s statement continued. “Those measures have not been enough to offset the extraordinary increase in fuel costs, and we have been unable to continue carrying the resulting operating losses.”

In an interview with Business Insider on Wednesday, Villa Vie Residences founder Mikael Petterson called himself “the villain in the story.”

“Nobody that’s tried residential cruising has succeeded, and we have gone further than anyone else,” he said. “I think we would have been absolutely fine and we would have seen this through, if there was no Iran war.”

Red flags before the shutdown

In retrospect, Cassingham says there were a raft of red flags.

“I personally had red flags because when we would talk to Mikael, he could never look into the camera. His eyes were always shifting back and forth and mysterious—off into space.”

“Another red flag for me was the revolving door on their employees. Other red flags were invoices... Our monthly invoices were never correct. We always triple checked them if we could get them. Because they were using, it turns out, a spreadsheet to manage the company. Now I'm no bookkeeper, but I know a spreadsheet's not what you use to manage a large business.” 

Cassingham says she and her husband have no plans to come back to the U.S. at this point, especially since Randy is in the middle of treatment for prostate cancer. They want to see what happens with the ship, and Randy has further cancer tests in Thailand in December. 

A group of passengers has formed an LLC and is looking into how they can possibly get the ship back into operating order. But for now, Cassingham thinks things won’t be resolved for at least another 60 to 90 days.

Still, Cassingham seems sanguine about her current predicament. The yoga and meditation helps, as does her work as a transformation life coach. 

“When the pioneers headed West from the East Coast, they didn't know what was ahead of them,” she says. “You make it or you don't.”

The Hidden Housing Cost Pushing Millions of Homeowners Over Budget

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

A new roof can be just as essential to homeownership as a monthly mortgage payment. Yet only one of those expenses is factored in when determining whether an owner is housing cost-burdened.

Now, preliminary findings from Harvard University's Joint Center for Housing Studies (JCHS), presented on Friday, suggest that the omission has been hiding a much larger affordability problem in plain sight.

Under conventional measures, 22.7 million homeowners, or 26%, spent more than 30% of their incomes on housing in 2023—the threshold for being considered cost-burdened.

But when researchers included spending on routine maintenance and major replacement projects, that figure jumped to 28.4 million—nearly 1 in 3 homeowners.

"It would be a surprise to many that nearly every measure of cost burdens that you see out in the wild, including those that we most often published, do not account for these expenditures," says Alexander Hermann, a senior research associate at JCHS and co-author of the analysis.

For some homeowners, even that expanded measure may underestimate the financial strain.

The housing costs that don't show up in affordability statistics

Much of the housing affordability debate has focused on home prices and recurring expenses such as property taxes and insurance—and for good reason.

Between 2019 and 2025, homeowners' property tax expenses rose 31%, while average monthly homeowners insurance premiums jumped 72%, according to ICE Mortgage Technology data cited by Harvard.

But spending on home maintenance has also surged, with considerably less attention.

Between 2019 and 2023, homeowners' collective spending on routine maintenance climbed 61%, from $65 billion to $105 billion, according to Harvard's Improving America's Housing 2025 report. Even after adjusting for inflation, spending increased 35%.

And in the American Housing Survey data underlying the new analysis, maintenance and replacement spending grew faster than other housing expenses, including taxes and insurance, Hermann says.

So why has it been missing from affordability calculations?

Part of the problem is the data itself. Most major housing surveys don't ask homeowners what they spend on repairs. And unlike mortgages or property taxes, maintenance costs are irregular. A homeowner might spend thousands replacing a roof one year and nothing the next, making it difficult to translate into a consistent measure of affordability.

Then there's an even larger complication: What homeowners spend doesn’t necessarily reflect what their properties need.

Unlike a mortgage or tax bill, a major repair can be postponed when money is tight. That means a homeowner might report little or no spending even as their house deteriorates.

"These expenses can be deferrable to a point, but you can't put them off indefinitely," Hermann explains.

To capture costs that actual spending might conceal, the researchers developed a second estimate based on what homeowners could be expected to spend on upkeep, using property characteristics and spending patterns among middle-income households.

Under that approach, 30.8 million homeowners, or 35.5%, would be considered cost-burdened—roughly 8 million more than conventional estimates suggest.

America's aging homes are making affordability worse

As the U.S. housing stock gets older, understanding the nuance between what owners are spending and what their homes actually need is poised to become more important than ever.

The median owner-occupied home was 42 years old in 2023, compared with just 27 years old three decades earlier, according to JCHS.

And that age is already catching up. More than 22% of owner-occupied homes built before 1960 had a structural deficiency in 2023—more than twice the rate for homes built since 2000.

But the households facing the greatest upkeep needs are often those least equipped to pay for them.

"Lower-income homeowners, for example, are more likely to live in homes that require greater upkeep in the first place, and these households are at the same time least likely to have the resources they need to address those repair needs,” Hermann explains.

That disparity is already evident in what homeowners spend.

Among owners of homes built before 1960, households in the highest income quintile spent an average of $12,700 on improvements and repairs in 2023, compared with just $3,400 among those in the lowest quintile.

Of course, that difference doesn't necessarily mean lower-income homes need less work. Instead, it may mean their owners simply cannot afford to do it.

And that distinction helps explain why Harvard's estimates of expected upkeep spending reveal a much wider affordability gap than conventional measures suggest.

Three-quarters of homeowners earning less than $30,000 annually already exceeded the traditional housing-cost threshold. When researchers accounted for modeled upkeep spending, that share reached a staggering 93%.

For those earning $30,000 to $45,000, the rate climbed from 43% to 64%.

The cost of putting off repairs can come due at sale

Older homeowners also face a particularly difficult version of this affordability squeeze.

Among homeowners aged 65 and older, 32% were cost-burdened under conventional measures, compared with 45% when modeled upkeep costs were included.

Part of that can be attributed to the fact that retirement often brings lower incomes, even as aging homes require more expensive repairs. And while some homeowners can draw on savings or investments, others face a choice between preserving cash today and maintaining the value of their largest asset.

There’s already data that the trade-off can eventually work its way into the sale price of these homes.

An 80-year-old homeowner realizes an annualized return that's roughly half a percentage point lower than a 45-year-old homeowner, equivalent to a sale price that's more than 5% lower over the average 11-year holding period, according to research from the National Bureau of Economic Research.

Differences in renovations and property upkeep explained about a quarter of that age-related gap, while selling practices, including off-market transactions and sales to investors, also contributed.

The dilemma is gaining attention in Washington.

Earlier this month, a bipartisan group of lawmakers urged the Department of Housing and Urban Development to implement a new home-repair program, warning that some seniors cannot afford the updates needed to sell their homes without accepting steep discounts.

The Whole-Home Repairs pilot was authorized under the 21st Century Road to Housing Act, enacted in July, but federal funding has not yet been appropriated and the program has not been implemented.

Rachel Drew, director of Harvard's Remodeling Futures Program, argues that assistance should prioritize households with the greatest repair needs, ideally through grants rather than loans or tax benefits requiring upfront spending.

For homeowners who cannot afford to preserve their properties, repair assistance could protect both their housing and the wealth they have built.

As Hermann puts it, "All repair programs are affordability programs."
