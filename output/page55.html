Heads up: The clocks are about to change. The evening of Halloween gets an extra hour as the clocks fall back an hour, meaning you can sleep in on Sunday, November 1. 

Complaints about daylight savings are as old as daylight savings itself, and pretty much everyone has an opinion about the practice. As with any such topic, there are all kinds of myths and misunderstandings out there. 

Farmers didn’t ask for daylight savings

This one comes up a lot: the idea that farmers are the reason we have daylight savings. In this version of the story, farmers, who work outside and need to align their workdays with the daylight, were the ones who made the argument for the practice. 

In truth, farmers were the main group opposed to daylight savings during World War I. According to the Library of Congress: “The agriculture industry fervently opposed the measure because farming schedules are based on sunrise and sunset not the clock.” 

After the war there was a bill to repeal daylight savings, which passed through both houses of Congress. President Woodrow Wilson vetoed that bill, and his statement about the veto acknowledged this wouldn’t make farmers happy. 

“I realize the very considerable and in some respects very serious inconveniences to which the daylight-saving law subjects the farmers of the country,” he wrote. Wilson sided with the industrial sector. The veto was overridden, leading to a long period where daylight savings was practiced only in certain areas. For example: New York City observed daylight savings, but rural areas in New York State did not (not what you’d expect if farmers broadly supported daylight savings). 

None of this is to say that some farmers don’t support daylight savings time today—it’s just that farmers aren’t the reason the practice exists in America. If anything, they tried to stop it. 

Benjamin Franklin didn’t invent daylight savings

There are some “facts” repeated so often that hardly anyone questions them, and “Ben Franklin invented daylight savings time” remains one of them. The problem is that it’s not true. 

I’d understand if you’re skeptical—I’m a Canadian journalist trying to steal an achievement from one of America’s founding fathers. But you don’t have to take my word on this one—The Franklin Institute, a museum dedicated to Franklin’s legacy, states that “Daylight saving time is one thing that Franklin did not invent.” The United States didn’t even implement daylight savings until 1918, well over a century after Franklin passed away. 

So where did the idea that he invented it come from? Probably from a satirical Journal de Paris article Franklin penned back in 1784. 

In that piece he jokingly encouraged the residents of Paris to wake up earlier in the morning, in order to spend less money on candles. He also proposed a tax on shutters, rationing of candles, a prohibition of carriages on the streets after sunset, and church bells ringing at the crack of dawn every day. He, it goes without saying, wasn’t being entirely serious. 

So where did the idea for daylight savings come from? New Zealand entomologist George Hudson first proposed intentionally shifting the clock in an 1895 talk to the Wellington Philosophical Society, then followed up with a paper in 1898. 

The US already tried year-round daylight savings

This isn’t a myth, exactly, but it is something that rarely comes up in contemporary discussions about daylight savings. Multiple US states have proposed doing away with standard time entirely. Nationally, the Sunshine Protection Act, which passed the House over the summer, would institute the change from sea to shining sea. 

Here’s the thing, though: this isn’t the first time. The US tried making daylight savings permanent during the height of the 1970s energy crisis. The problem was that people hated it. What was supposed to be a two-year experiment lasted less than a year because of a massive change in public opinion. 

Support for the change started high, as an October 1974 New York Times article lays out: 79 percent of the public favored the idea in December of 1973, but by February of 1974, that dropped to 42 percent—a 37-point swing. A bill to restore standard time in winter passed the House by a 362 to 16 vote. 

What caused the backlash? Dark winter mornings. Particularly prominent were complaints from parents, who didn’t like their children walking to school in the dark. 

A 1975 study by the US Department of Transportation concluded that a year-round switch possibly isn’t a great idea, proposing instead a schedule change. “We conclude that modest overall benefits might be realized by a shift from the historic 6-month DST system (May through October) to an 8-month DST system (March through October),” the paper concludes. This, notably, is pretty much what the US has been doing since 2007. 

The past isn’t a prediction of the future—it’s possible switching to year-round daylight savings would go differently today. Walking to school is less common, for one thing, and streets are better lit. All the same, it’s worth realizing this has been tried before.

Intel lost almost a third of its value on Friday. By the close, the Nasdaq Composite had sealed its worst month since March 2025, down 5.3%.

Tom Lee of Fundstrat, a Wall Street research firm, has another suspect. He thinks one big investor may have been forced to dump tech shares, leaving tech holders with the losses.

Why Tom Lee Thinks One Investor Was Forced to Sell

Speaking on CNBC as the Nasdaq slid on Friday, Lee said the market was fighting an “internal battle” and that the selling looked forced.

“Today looks like someone’s getting liquidated. I don’t know if it’s an institution,” he said.

By “liquidated,” Lee means forced to sell. This happens when an investor buys shares with borrowed money and prices then fall. The lender wants its money back, so the investor has to sell at any price.

Selling that big drags prices even lower. Lee thinks that is what hit tech on Friday, not investors abandoning stocks.

As evidence, he pointed to a version of the S&P 500 that counts every company equally, so a small firm matters as much as a giant like Nvidia. That index was rising while he spoke, a sign to Lee that money was still in stocks.

By the close, it had slipped 0.79%. The regular S&P 500, where the biggest companies count most, fell 1.82%.

Bad Earnings Hit Tech the Same Day

Intel fell 28.6% after a weak fourth-quarter forecast, its worst day since 1974. Amazon reported $42.4 billion in negative free cash flow, driven by heavy AI spending.

A week earlier, Lee said yields back below 5% meant the AI selloff was ending. On Friday, the 10-year Treasury yield closed at 4.93%. The Nasdaq still had its worst day since June 5.

Steve Sosnick, chief strategist at Interactive Brokers, said his retail clients had not fled. On Thursday, though, they piled into Alphabet.

Why Lee Says Wall Street Is Waiting for November 3

Lee says big investors lack the conviction to buy falling stocks before Tuesday’s midterm elections. Until then, he warned, a small push could send prices sharply lower.

“I think people are marking their calendar until November 3rd,” he added.

CNBC’s anchor said markets expect Democrats to edge ahead in both chambers of Congress. Sosnick called that the prevailing theory.

CME FedWatch also puts the odds of a December rate hike at 72%.

Lee says the buyers will come back. Tuesday’s vote will show whether they do.

Volkswagen Literally Can't Afford to Develop the Electric Golf Right Now

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Crisis Mode

Volkswagen went through very choppy waters in 2025, but 2026 has been anything but kind to Germany’s biggest automaker. In the third quarter of this year, it posted massive losses amounting to $6.6 billion. It posted profits of nearly $4 billion in the second quarter and $2.8 billion in the first quarter, but the most recent financial report reveals everything it had gained had practically been wiped out.

It’s been one problem after another for VW, as it needs to cut costs amidst plunging sales, the beating it’s getting in China, and resistance from workers’ unions due to mass layoffs. With the way things are going, projects are bound to be suspended, or worse, canceled altogether.

Getty

Electric Golf at Risk

With all of that going on, one project that has been put on ice is the electric Golf. Reuters noted VW Passenger Cars CEO Thomas Schäfer during the earnings call, saying, “I know what the Golf means for Volkswagen and for Wolfsburg. That’s why this decision was really not an easy one for us.”

Simply put, there simply isn’t enough money to continue the car’s development. Beyond the losses, another reason it’s been put on ice is that VW couldn’t hit its cost-cutting target, falling short by $336 million. VW could’ve carried on, or at least slowed, the electric Golf’s development if it had achieved that number, but that’s not the case right now.

This doesn’t mean the project is canceled outright. However, the electric Golf is at greater risk of becoming a stillborn model if VW can’t pull itself out of the rut it’s stuck in right now. On the flip side, if things do look up, perhaps a little work goes into it, but it likely won’t be of the highest priority.

Volkswagen

Just When Things Were Looking Up (Sort Of)

It’s a major blow to the automaker’s EV push, especially given that its subcompact EVs are actually making headway. The ID. Polo has already raked in a whole lot of orders, and the reviews so far are actually positive. That’s something that couldn’t be said about VW’s initial attempts at mass-market EVs. With the way things are going, the ID. Cross is poised to be a hit for the brand in Europe.

The electric Golf, likely to be called the ID. Golf, could have carried the momentum that the ID. Polo and ID. Cross have been gathering, but alas, it won’t be the case right away. That’s a bit of a shame given that it was being hyped up just a few months ago. Based on the previous report, it will follow the form of the MK. 4 Golf, and it will have a button-rich cabin like the subcompact EVs.

The ID. Golf does, indeed, sound promising, but we won’t know what it would be like for the next couple of years, and that’s if development for it pushes through. Right now, though, there are more urgent matters that VW has to attend to before moving forward.
