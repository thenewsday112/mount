Arsenal could expose Liverpool’s biggest weakness at Anfield

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Arsenal have spent years struggling to impose themselves at Anfield. However, Mikel Arteta could have an opportunity to change that record when his side face Liverpool on Sunday.

The Gunners have often struggled against the physicality of Liverpool on Merseyside. Since Jurgen Klopp took charge, Liverpool have built a reputation for being high pressing, fast, and making their opponents make mistakes.

However, looking at the latest stats, it seems that Liverpool are no longer as physical, meaning that Arsenal could target their weaknesses.

The disparity is stark between the two teams, with Liverpool having run the least distance of any Premier League side this season. Meanwhile, Arsenal are running the most.

If Arteta’s side can capitalise on this and create fast attacks from possession, then they can utilise this to their advantage.

Liverpool’s pressing has lost its physical edge

Liverpool have shifted away from their high-octane style associated with much of Klopp’s reign. Arne Slot has slowly moved towards a more possession-based approach, focusing on keeping the ball.

Andoni Iraola looks keen to add more intensity to their pressing. The Reds have had some of the best pressing stats in the Premier League this season (Opta Analyst).

However, they are not getting the desired intensity from the system.

In addition to chasing the opposition when they have the ball, players must quickly recover, cut off passing lanes and return to their defensive positions.

This is where Arsenal might have an opportunity. If they can get past Liverpool’s first line of pressure, they may have space to push forward before the home side can get back into position.

Rice and Lewis-Skelly could cause problems centrally

Another area that the Gunners could target is Liverpool’s midfield.

Alexis MacAllister and Ryan Gravenberch are technically gifted and very composed when they have the ball. But neither are a true defensive midfielder, which raises questions over their ability to stop teams playing through the middle.

Arsenal have the players to exploit this. Declan Rice can put in an energetic, physical performance and drive the play forward. Myles Lewis-Skelly provides another option who can run with the ball into dangerous areas.

This can cause problems for the Liverpool midfield. Arsenal can try to disrupt Mac Allister and Gravenberch, not letting them set the tempo of the game.

If they win these individual battles, it can stop Liverpool from getting the ball control they want.

Arsenal must put their Anfield record behind them

The task is certainly a tall one. Arsenal have lost their last 16 games at Anfield, including seven in Arteta’s reign.

Whilst that may be the case, the current Liverpool team are very different to the one Arsenal have faced on a regular basis during Klopp’s time in charge.

They certainly can’t expect to be outplayed, but they must take advantage of Liverpool’s failings.

Arsenal are currently second in the Premier League with 21 points from eight games. They will want to win here but will need to do more than just run them into the ground.

If Rice and Lewis-Skelly can push forward and pressurise them, forcing them to make mistakes, then Arteta could finally have his winning recipe at Anfield.

Greensboro has already surpassed 40% of its “Road to 10,000” housing goal, with 4,071 homes completed or under construction roughly 18 months after the initiative launched. City leaders say the program is ahead of schedule toward its goal of adding 10,000 housing units by 2030, an effort that has taken on greater importance as major employers expand across the region.

The housing need extends well beyond the initiative. Greensboro officials estimate the city will require at least 33,000 additional housing units to serve current and future residents across a range of incomes and housing types.

That demand outlook is being reinforced by large employment investments, including Toyota Battery Manufacturing NC, JetZero, and Boom Supersonic. The near-term sales backdrop offers another encouraging signal. As of August, the broader Greensboro market recorded 1,344 new-home sales over the prior 12 months, up 24.9% year over year. Attached sales were especially strong, rising 67.8% to 515, compared with a 7.8% increase in detached sales to 829.

The stronger sales picture comes despite a slower labor market. Greensboro’s non-farm employment increased just 0.6% year over year in July, representing an addition of 1,000 jobs. While still positive, that pace has moderated from earlier periods. The metro’s unemployment rate stood at 3.8%, and Zonda forecasts annual employment growth of 0.6% for 2026 before improving to 0.9% in both 2027 and 2028. 

Greensboro is adding housing fast, attached product is contributing meaningfully to sales growth, and major economic development projects could support future household formation. Yet slower near-term job growth and increasing lot and finished inventory reinforce the need for disciplined product selection, pricing, and submarket strategy.

Editor's Picks: The Best Ski Boots of 2027

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Ski boots are hands-down the most important part of your kit. Without a proper boot, your fancy skis, fresh bindings, and any sort of pre-season fitness you’ve gained are going out the window. As always, the right boot for you depends on your foot shape, ski ability, skiing style, and what sort of features you might be looking for. This year, we tested loads of boots, and here our five top picks, spanning the range from ultra-narrow race-style boots to roomier freestyle-focused boots. We’ve highlighted the stiffest, highest end version of all of these boots, but every boot on this list also comes in various softer flexes.

Types of Alpine Ski Boots

Four-buckle/Overlap: The most common type of ski boot, made of a cuff and a lower “clog” or “scafo.” Both pieces are connected by ankle pivots and bolted together on the spine. These boots close with four buckles (or BOA dials) located on the cuff and lower. The boot’s stiffness largely comes from the rigidity of the spine.

Cabrio/Three-piece: A somewhat newer style of ski boot that uses three pieces of plastic: a lower, cuff, and a hard plastic tongue. Instead of a rigid, bolted, spine behind the foot, the boot’s stiffness comes from the plastic tongue. These are typically easier to get on and off, offer more room over the instep, and will have a different flex pattern than an overlap-style boot.

Race/Plug: A subcategory of overlap boots, true race boots (sometimes called “plug boots”) use extremely thick and heavy plastic to offer super-stiff flexes and very precise fits. The thick plastic is designed to be heavily customized by a professional bootfitter. These are great choices for expert and pro-level skiers, but are not appropriate for most everyday skiers. They are also usually not compatible with standard alpine bindings without some modification. The Ferrari of ski boots.

Rear-entry: These type of boots are making a comeback for entry-level skiers looking for easy-to-use and comfortable boots. The back half of the boot folds backwards, allowing you to more easily slide your foot into the boot. While the comfort and convenience factor is certainly high, these tend to lack high-end performance. The Toyota Camry of ski boots.

Hybrid/Crossover: Essentially alpine boots with a walk mode and tech fittings, hybrid boots are meant mostly for resort skiing, with occasional tours or bootpacks in mind. These offer the good ski performance with the ability to transition into a touring boot, and make the best option as a one-boot quiver. Recently, some offer tech fittings in the toe, but skip the walk mode entirely. Mostly ski the resort, but want to tour out the gates every once in awhile? These are for you. The Jeep Wrangler of ski boots.

Boot Terminology

Flex: While all manufacturers state flex numbers on their boots, there is no official “standard” to what they mean. In other words, a 120-flex boot will not necessarily feel the same across brands. A boot’s flex is determined by both it’s fit around your specific foot (think of your leg as lever, and the way it hits the boot cuff will give you more or less leverage), and the stiffness of the actual plastic used. That stiffness is derived from the type of plastic and its “durometer” or hardness. A good way to decipher the indicated numbers is: <100 flex is great for entry-level skiers, 110-120 is for intermediate to advanced skiers, and a 130+ is for experts. Subtract 10 from those numbers for women’s boots.

Last: How wide the boot is on the inside of the shell. Indicated last measurements are typically taken from the from the sample or prototype size (usually a 26.5 or a 27.5 shell). These range from 96mm (super narrow) to upwards of 104mm (very wide). Most bootfitters will agree that you can get away with a slightly narrower last than you might think, especially if you properly support your foot with a footbed, and modify the shell where necessary.

Walk Modes: Some alpine boots offer a touring-boot-style walk mode. While these come in many shapes and sizes, the basic principle is the same: unlock the rigid connection between the shells lower and upper cuff. Some boots use external hardware, some use complicated internal systems, while others integrate it all into the buckles that close the boot.

Shell Materials: Most alpine boots are built with Polyurethane (PU) plastic, a tried and true material that offers excellent stiffness and damping and easy modification by a bootfitter. Pebax is often used to create lighter crossover boots, but doesn’t offer the same flex and damping characteristics.

Stance: The position your foot and lower leg are in in when inside a ski boot. This is determined by the boot’s forward lean (usually in the 12-16 degree range) and the boot board’s ramp angle (anywhere from 3-5 degrees). A more aggressive forward lean brings your knees further over the front of the boot and can make a boot feel more “powerful,” at the cost of comfort when standing around. Ramp angle determines how high your heel is compared to your toes, with a more aggressive number typically making it easier to initiate the boots flex.

No matter what boot you buy, go see a bootfitter!

Salomon has long had an aggressive low-volume freeride boot in their lineup, but the 2027 S/Pro Alpha gets a ground-up redesign to offer a very unique set of features. From a distance, Salomon’s new S/Pro Alpha C BOA looks like any other traditional overlap-style ski boot, but the devil is in the details. First off, there is a single BOA dial, but it’s on the cuff, not the lower. Secondly, there’s no pivot on the inside ankle–instead, Salomon uses a bolted link plate (called PowerLink) that rigidly connects the cuff to the lower. That’s part of the magic of how this boot’s flex feels different than any other boot out there.

This new, lower leverage point allows the boot to flex more easily in the first part of the its “travel,” eating up small bumps in the snow and allowing a skier to engage their edges a bit more precisely. Once you flex deeper into the boot’s travel (eg. while pushing into higher-angle carves, skiing moguls, or landing bigger airs), the stiffness ramps up considerably. The flex is incredibly progressive, and offers a more bouncy, lively feel than the vibration-damping character of a super-stiff race boot.

For anyone looking to replace their classic, everyday high-performance resort boot, Salomon’s new S/Pro Alpha C BOA brings a new kind of ski boot feel to the table. Do you typically ski fast and drive big skis, but are tired of dealing with heavy, uncomfortable race-style boots to achieve the performance you want? The S/Pro Alpha can match that on-snow performance with lighter plastic and a more progressive flex. It’s not a race boot, or a piste boot, it’s a freeride boot that can do it all.

There are no BOAs on this boot. The shell retains the super-narrow 96mm last, but there’s a bit of extra room in toe box and instep, a soft rubber internal bootboard, updated plastic that is less sensitive to temperature, and Atomic adds GripWalk soles to the bottom.

There are three words I’d use to describe Atomic’s new Remedy 130 boot: precise, responsive, and ridiculously damp. Ok, that’s four words, but whatever. I’m not really sure which descriptor is more important here, as they really all go hand in hand. An aggressive forward lean, a flatter internal ramp angle, and best-in-class damping make this a tank ready to demolish any conditions in sight. And after a full season skiing it as my everyday resort (and sometimes touring boot), it’s my personal favorite ski boot for hard-charging skiing.

That being said, like most high-performance ski boots, the Atomic Remedy 130 is a bit of a niche product. I would call it a no-limits freeride boot, but there are a few caveats. First of all, it’s got one of the narrowest lasts in a commercially-available boot today–which means it’s just not going to be comfortable for anyone with a wide foot, sensitive toes, or a high instep. Given the thickness of the plastic and all the built-in fitting adjustments, that’s not really a bad thing, it just requires extra attention to get realistic all-day comfort out of these.

Louvre Signs Deal with Unions, Ending Threat of Continued Strikes

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

As nationwide protests continue to loom across France, with the next action planned for Saturday, October 31, at least one social grievance has been addressed at the country’s top museum. On Thursday, the Louvre in Paris signed an agreement with unions who had threatened to strike for months, reports Le Figaro and AFP.

“The signature of the agreement amounts to a positive step for the Louvre,” a museum representative told AFP.

A few months after last year’s daytime robbery at the Louvre emptied the museum of over $100 million worth of its crown jewels, a broad coalition of French unions went on strike and, until this week’s deal, had pledged to continue protesting over poor working conditions, insufficient personnel, and the handling of museum renovations. The strikes began in December and forced the museum to close entirely for a total of four days and partially for another four days. Meanwhile, unions maintained an official “notice” to continue protests as needed, signaling they were not backing down and could block the museum at any time. All told, the dispute with unions is one of the longest face-offs of its kind for the Louvre.

As part of the new deal, the Louvre pledged to hire 50 new security personnel in 2027, or about 100 new hires in total by 2028, according to Le Quotidien de l’Art and other French reports. Additionally, the museum will improve pay for certain categories of workers, including a €50 ($56) monthly raise for security staff.

“The more of us there are carrying out our surveillance duties, the more we will deter criminals from targeting our institution,” Christian Galani, a representative of the French union CGT, told AFP.

The museum is in the throes of raising funds for an over one-billion-dollar renovation that will include security upgrades, the preservation of its aging infrastructure that has fallen into disrepair, and a separate, new entry and gallery dedicated to the Mona Lisa. However, there is disagreement about the cost and scope of the major renovation, and union representatives want a say in how it is handled. “We will quickly ask for a meeting on the management and prioritization of the construction works. We still have very little visibility on the safety and security plan announced in November 2025,” Alexis Fritche, leader of the CFDT-Culture union, told Le Quotidien de l’Art.

Following the crown jewel theft a year ago, Louvre president Laurence des Cars stepped down amid staff criticism that she used a top-down management style and ignored their concerns about the building’s disrepair and lack of personnel, while spending on projects they considered lavish. The arrival of the museum’s newly appointed president, Christophe Leribault, seems to have calmed some of those tensions.

One of the most common arguments we hear in favor of choosing larger cartridges for hunting big game is that a magnum cartridge will offer margin for error. When considering cartridges like 6.5 Creedmoor vs. .300 Win. Mag., the .300 has more energy, drives a bigger bullet faster, and, we assume, will do more damage than the 6.5. That means that if we don’t hit exactly where we are aiming, we are more likely to still kill the animal cleanly by choosing the magnum. At face value, the logic is sound. But how does it hold up when we start examining what the bullets actually do downrange? You might be surprised.

Smaller Cartridges Vs. Magnums

Magnums like the .300 Win. Mag. are popular, but do they really give you margin for error? Tyler Freel

There are two schools of thought that commonly divide hunters when it comes to choosing cartridges. One opts for milder cartridges that have lower recoil and are generally easier and more pleasant to shoot well. The other wants the extra power to cover their bases. Everyone, of course, falls somewhere on a spectrum, and the categories aren’t mutually exclusive. 

Small Cartridge Theory

I’ve written a lot about why I think smaller cartridges are often the right choice for big game, and it generally boils down to two observations. First, with thoughtful bullet selection, I haven’t noticed a dramatic difference between the wound characteristics of smaller and larger cartridges — and the smaller rounds have produced consistently satisfactory results. Second, milder cartridges are more pleasant to shoot and, despite having no real limitations on the cartridges I have access to, that’s what I reach for consistently. I have the rifles and ammo to torch a hundred rounds of .300 PRC in a range session practicing positional shooting but, realistically, I’m going to pick a 6.5 Creedmoor, 6 Creed, or 22 ARC to do that. Practice with smaller cartridges can also benefit how you’ll shoot magnums, but there’s a lot of value in hunting with the cartridge that you train with.

Proponents of smaller cartridges will point out that they deliver enough terminal performance to get the job done but are, generally, much easier to shoot well. Tyler Freel

Large Cartridge Theory

You’ll commonly hear hunters argue that you should choose the largest cartridge that you can handle without flinching — especially for larger game. Lots of hunters consider this basic conventional wisdom, and it’s rooted in the belief that a larger cartridge, bullet, or more kinetic energy will result in a larger wound and will kill the animal more quickly. This makes up for some degree of shooting error, or will allow more forgiveness on less-than-ideal shot angles. Essentially, choosing a more powerful cartridge is viewed as cheap insurance. 

The .300 Win. Mag. works very well on big game like moose, even beyond 300 or 400 yards, so it’s easy to understand why people like it. Tyler Freel

Real-World Results

There are many ways that bigger cartridges like the .300 Win. Mag. outperform smaller ones like the 6.5 Creedmoor. When choosing something for yourself, you need to know what the real benefits are, and decide whether they actually apply to you and your hunting. What does that look like in terms of margin for error? That’s exactly what we took a look at in the video above. 

I took a 6.5 Creedmoor and .300 Win. Mag. to the range and fired the same line of factory ammo — Federal Fusion Tipped — from each into identical blocks of Clear Ballistics gel. This gel isn’t a one-to-one representation of flesh, but it is a homogeneous, consistent medium in which we can get a side-by-side comparison of two bullets. The blocks were placed at 200 yards with a Garmin chronograph positioned about 15 yards in front of the target to record impact velocity. The Fusion Tipped bullets were 140 grains and 180 grains for the 6.5 CM and .300 Win. Mag., respectively. The rifles were the same except for different barrel lengths — 18 inches for the 6.5 CM and 22 inches for the .300. Impact velocities were recorded as 2,233 fps and 2,526 fps, with the advantage going to the .300.

As expected, the .300 Win. Mag. load delivered higher velocity and more energy at the target than the 6.5 Creedmoor. The block was knocked off the table and was clearly hit harder with the 180-grain slug than the 140. However, the actual wound cavities were not much different. The expanded diameters of the recovered bullets were within .015 inch when measuring both the extreme diameter of the jacket (which would typically be smashed down flat in an animal) and the diameter of mushroomed bullet bodies. The .30-caliber bullet left a slightly wider overall wound cavity across its traverse, disrupting slightly more tissue, but the wound channels aren’t perfectly cylindrical either. The overall diameter of the wound channels and penetration were nearly identical. 

While the .300 Win. Mag. (bottom) delivered significantly more energy to the target, that didn’t result in a dramatic difference in results. Tyler Freel

Takeaways

This demonstration shouldn’t be taken to mean that the 6.5 CM and .300 Win. Mag. are completely equal. They each have their advantages and disadvantages. The results, however, do track pretty well with other gel testing I’ve done, and what I’ve observed on animals. Those are that, with the same bullet construction, results don’t vary too much across most common hunting cartridges. In fact, things like penetration and wound cavity size can be more greatly influenced by bullet selection than simply picking a bigger or smaller cartridge. 

Some hunters place a lot of weight in having a cartridge that “hits harder.” While we can see some of the effects in the movement of the gel blocks during this demonstration, and that delta in impact force could possibly offer increased effect to the central nervous system, it’s not a reliable predictor of how fast an animal will die, or how much room for error you have. What we can rely on is the damage the bullet does.

One of the real advantages of a magnum cartridge, with a thoughtfully chosen bullet, is that it can deliver effective velocities over longer distances. A disadvantage of a 6.5 Creedmoor, in common shorter barrel configurations, is that it will produce lower muzzle velocities and result in a shorter effective velocity window. Ensuring that your impact speeds are high enough to make the bullet work properly is critical. 

Our field shooting exercise did demonstrate that you might be able to shoot a magnum just as well as the 6.5. If you can, then you are better poised to take advantage of a magnum’s real benefits. Over time, most people will find that they shoot milder cartridges more consistently. It is most important to choose a cartridge that you can shoot well and will practice with.

Final Thoughts

Along with better equipment and more refined shooting technique, we are constantly learning more about terminal ballistics. Many of our long-held beliefs are simply a product of lore and oversimplified logic. If you’re a successful hunter and your favorite cartridge works well for you, we’re not asking you to change. But if you’re feeling the pressure to choose a cartridge that you don’t think you’ll shoot as well, because you feel that it’s powerful enough to make up the difference, you’re barking up the wrong tree. The smaller cartridges will work just as well on the good shots, and the big ones will do just as poorly on the bad shots.
