This Is How Fast You Should Walk to Help Lower Your Risk of Death

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →
People who walk faster may have better underlying health. Lol2368/E+/Getty Images
  • A study found that walking speed can help predict people’s risk of dying from any cause.
  • Both a clinical test and a test done in daily life were able to predict mortality risk.
  • Experts say walking speed predicts death risk because it’s an indicator of overall health.
  • If your walking speed slows, it’s a good idea to talk with your healthcare professional.

New research shows that how fast you walk, whether measured in a clinical test or through daily activity, can help predict your risk of dying from any cause.

The study compared two ways of measuring walking: a short walking speed test conducted in a clinic and the step rate people take in their everyday lives, measured with a wrist-worn device.

Both measures were linked to mortality risk and showed similar ability to predict it.

The findings, published on October 23 in JAMA Network Open, could help doctors and researchers choose the best method for assessing health risks in different settings.

Clinical walking speed vs. everyday walking

The study was part of the Whitehall II accelerometer substudy in the United Kingdom.

It included 4,067 participants, mostly males, with an average age of about 69 years. About one-third of the subjects were female.

The researchers collected data from January 2012 to December 2013 and followed up through August 2024.

To measure clinical walking speed, participants completed a standard walking test. They walked a marked 2.44-meter course at their usual pace. The test was repeated three times, and the average speed in meters per second was used for analysis. Participants who normally used a cane were allowed to use it during the test.

For free-living walking measurements, participants wore a wrist-worn GENEActiv accelerometer for 9 consecutive days. This device recorded movement at a high frequency and used special software to detect walking episodes and count steps.

From this data, two key measures of walking cadence were calculated: the median walking cadence (the middle value of steps per minute during walking periods) and the 95th-percentile walking cadence (a higher step rate indicating brisk walking). The study also calculated the average number of daily steps.

The researchers additionally gathered information on factors known to affect mortality, such as:

  • age
  • sex
  • education
  • race and ethnicity
  • marital status
  • smoking
  • alcohol use
  • diet
  • body mass index (BMI)
  • hypertension
  • chronic diseases

National death records were used to identify deaths during the follow-up period.

Statistical methods were then used to estimate the risk of death associated with walking speed and cadence while accounting for other risk factors.

The models were adjusted step-by-step: starting with no adjustments, then adding sociodemographic factors, behavioral factors, and finally health-related factors.

The ability of walking measures to predict mortality was assessed using a statistical measure.

The study also examined differences between males and females.

The researchers additionally conducted analyses to test how well the results held up when they excluded early deaths, looked at subgroups by age and health status, and used different definitions of walking cadence.

Faster walking speed linked to 23% lower risk of death

During a median follow-up of nearly 12 years, 739 participants died.

The average clinical walking speed of participants was 1.2 meters per second. The median walking cadence during daily life was about 75 steps per minute, and the 95th percentile walking cadence was about 112 steps per minute.

The study found only a weak correlation between clinical walking speed and free-living walking cadence, meaning the two measures do not strongly reflect the same walking behavior. However, both measures were independently associated with a lower risk of death.

After adjusting for age, sex, lifestyle, and health factors, for every standard increase in clinical walking speed, the risk of death was 23% lower.

Similarly, higher median and 95th-percentile walking cadences were associated with 17% and 18% lower risks of death, respectively.

When examining how well these walking measures predicted mortality, each on its own showed modest predictive ability.

Adding clinical walking speed to a model with other known risk factors slightly improved mortality prediction, while adding free-living walking cadence also improved prediction, albeit to a similar degree to that of clinical walking speed.

Combining clinical walking speed with free-living walking parameters provided a small boost in predictive ability, but the effect was not significant after accounting for detailed health factors.

The study also found that walking speed and cadence had similar predictive power for both men and women, although the overall prediction models performed slightly better for women.

Several sensitivity analyses supported the main findings. Results were consistent when excluding early deaths, focusing on healthier or older participants, and using different ways to define walking cadence.

Overall, this study suggests that clinical walking speed tests and free-living walking cadence measurements from wrist-worn devices are both useful in predicting all-cause mortality risk. They provide similar information but reflect different aspects of walking behavior.

The choice between methods may depend on the purpose of the assessment, available resources, and the context in which the measurement is taken.

Faster walkers may live longer because they are healthier

Neha Sabharwal, PT, DPT, a physical therapist and director of rehabilitation specializing in geriatric mobility and functional decline at Vintage Faire Nursing and Rehabilitation Center in Modesto, CA, said that walking speed helps predict longevity because it’s an indicator of someone’s overall health.

“Walking is something most of us do without thinking about it, but it requires several body systems to work together,” she told Healthline. “Your heart and lungs have to keep up, your muscles need to produce enough force, and your brain has to coordinate each step while keeping you balanced.”

Sabharwal, who wasn’t involved in the study, said that in her work, she pays attention to when someone starts walking more slowly than before.

“It is easy to dismiss that change as part of getting older, but there may be something else going on,” she said. This could include muscle weakness, pain, reduced endurance, balance problems, or underlying medical conditions.

According to Sabharwal, the current study is valuable because it suggests that useful information about people’s movement can be obtained outside a clinical setting.

However, she cautioned against misinterpreting the findings to mean that simply walking faster will make you live longer.

“People who naturally walk faster may already have better underlying health,” said Sabharwal.

“I would be less concerned about whether someone walks faster than their neighbor and more interested in whether they are walking slower than they did six months ago,” she said.

What to do if your walking speed slows

Theodore Strange, MD, chairman of medicine at Northwell Staten Island University Hospital and senior vice president of the Metro Market Region at Northwell Health, shared some insights for those who may notice their walking speed has declined. Strange wasn’t involved in the study.

“I mean, if it’s slowing down because a week or two ago, you were walking up a flight of steps with no problem, and now, you’re having some shortness of breath, I think it should be evaluated,” he told Healthline.

Strange added that if you’re experiencing aches or pains that weren’t there before, you should consult a healthcare professional, as they can make exercise more difficult.

It’s also essential to manage any chronic diseases that you have so that you can exercise as well as possible within the constraints of your condition, he said.

“And I think slow and steady wins the race here,” said Strange. He suggests monitoring your walking with a device like a pedometer and progressively increasing your steps.

“Everybody talks about 10,000 steps a day. I think the most recent studies show probably 7,000 to 8,000 is adequate,” he said. You can start where you are and work your way up gradually.

Strange further noted that physical therapy can sometimes be helpful if you have issues with gait mechanics, balance, or strength. Additionally, you might benefit from exercise that is tailored to your condition, for example, if you’ve had a stroke.

Finally, Strange advised stretching before and after exercise to keep your muscles moving.

“I go back to adequate nutrition and hydration being important,” he said. You want to make sure you are adequately supporting your body’s ability to exercise, said Strange.

The Universal Frontend Nightmare: "Cannot Reproduce"

Every frontend engineer and engineering manager has lived this scenario: A user reports an obscure bug. They upload a 20-second screen recording showing a broken checkout modal or a glitchy video canvas. The QA ticket gets created, and an engineer spends 3 hours trying to recreate the exact environment, state, and click sequence.

Half the time, the ticket gets closed with the dreaded label:
Closed: Cannot Reproduce (Works on My Machine).

What if a bug report didn’t just contain a video, but automatically converted itself into a reproducible Playwright E2E test, diagnosed the codebase, patched the bug, and opened a ready-to-merge GitHub Pull Request?

That is the vision behind GhostPR (AutoFix AI).

In this post, I’ll walk through how I used Kiro Desktop IDE and Spec-Driven Development to stitch together video streams, headless browser automation, and AST code healing into a unified CLI engine for the Kiroween 2026 Frankenstein Category.

Why GhostPR is a "Frankenstein" Creation

The Kiroween Frankenstein Category challenges developers to take seemingly incompatible, disparate components and weld them into a coherent, powerful creation.

GhostPR brings together four wildly different systems:

The Senses (Video & DOM Streams): Browser screen recordings stored on Cloudflare R2 via QuickCast.
The Laboratory (Headless Automation): @playwright/test synthesizing deterministic TypeScript E2E specs.
The Brain (Spec-Driven AI): Kiro steering directives enforcing invariant assertions and defensive patching.
The Hands (Version Control): GitHub Octokit automating isolated git branch creation and verified Pull Requests.

None of these tools were designed to talk to each other out of the box. GhostPR acts as the neural bridge connecting them into an autonomous, closed-loop pipeline.

The Secret Weapon: Kiro Spec-Driven Development

When building an autonomous tool that writes and modifies code, LLM hallucinations can be catastrophic. If an AI agent attempts to patch code without proving the bug exists first, it risks breaking unrelated features.

To solve this, I built GhostPR strictly using Kiro's Spec-Driven Development paradigm:

  1. Architectural Boundaries in .kiro/steering/steering.md

Before writing a single line of JavaScript, I created .kiro/steering/steering.md. This established non-negotiable architectural guardrails:

Zero Cloud GPU: All video processing must run locally via native FFmpeg binaries ($0.00/mo infrastructure cost).
Deterministic Time Sync: Video keyframes and DOM interaction markers must be anchored to relative timestamps.
Invariant Scoped Syntax: Playwright specs must use scoped test.step() syntax with strict DOM assertions.
Defensive AST Patching: Patches must favor optional chaining (?.) and safe fallback states.

  1. Formal Requirements in .kiro/specs/ghost-pr-spec.md

I codified the 4-stage pipeline into 6 verifiable requirements (REQ-1 through REQ-6), ensuring each stage had a defined contract:

REQ-1: Ingest .webm stream from Cloudflare R2 without cloud GPU compute.
REQ-2: Extract 4 visual keyframes and parse interaction telemetry.
REQ-3: Synthesize tests/repro_.spec.ts.
REQ-4: Search target repository AST for error tokens.
REQ-5: Apply defensive patch and verify green execution.
REQ-6: Create Git branch fix/ghost-pr- and dispatch Pull Request.
How Kiro IDE Caught 4 Critical Architectural Gaps

One of the most impressive moments during development was when I loaded GhostPR into Kiro Desktop IDE and ran an architectural audit.

Kiro didn’t just check for syntax errors; it analyzed the entire dataflow against our steering directives and identified 4 subtle architectural gaps:

The "Disconnected Execution" Gap (Critical):
synth-test.js was generating the Playwright spec, but had hardcoded failureConfirmed: true without actually executing Playwright. Kiro pointed out that our self-healing loop was asserting a false positive:

"Stage 2 is asserting a lie. REQ-5 depends on running the test to confirm red state before diagnosing."

The Fix: We wired executePlaywrightSpec() directly into synthesizeTest(), setting failureConfirmed: !execResult.passed.

The Late pageerror Listener:
The error trap was declared inside a test.step() block after page.goto(). Kiro caught that any exception thrown during initial page boot or hydration would be missed.

The Fix: Hoisted the listener to the very top of the test body before navigation:

typescript
test('Autonomous E2E reproduction and state verification', async ({ page }) => {
// Hoist error trap BEFORE navigation to capture boot & hydration exceptions
const pageErrors = [];
page.on('pageerror', err => pageErrors.push(err.message));
await page.goto(targetUrl, { waitUntil: 'domcontentloaded' });
...

CWD Relative Path Drift:
Normalized all input and output paths upfront with path.resolve() so GhostPR runs identically from any working directory.

Stale Script Cleanup:
Removed a dead "start": "node src/server.js" entry in package.json and pointed it directly to src/cli.js.

"GhostPR is ready for competition submission. The red-to-green self-healing loop is now genuinely closed."

bash

Stage 2: Autonomous Playwright Spec Synthesis (Red State)

GhostPR generates tests/repro_qc_bt6mrt8n.spec.ts. It immediately runs the spec in headless Chromium. Because the unpatched bug exists, Playwright fails—confirming that the test genuinely reproduces the failure.

Stage 3: AST Diagnosis & Defensive Patching (Green State)

With the exact error message and failing selector in hand, GhostPR scans the codebase, locates the offending line, and synthesizes a defensive AST patch (e.g. shielding null pointer exceptions with optional chaining).

A doctrine read at every wake can become recitation: Deleuze and Guattari on regimes of signs

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A Thousand Plateaus has a chapter on several regimes of signs. The authors do not claim to have a complete list. Their words are: "There are many regimes of signs. Our own list is arbitrarily limited." They also say that the regimes are not stages of history: "We are not suggesting an evolutionism, we are not even doing history." The quotations here are from the Massumi translation, which I checked against a public full text. I have not checked them against the French original.

What interests me is not the list. It is one claim about the relation between a text and a reader. In the passional regime, the book "seems to be internalized, and to internalize everything." Interpretation then has two possible fates. One is that it "survives but becomes internal to the book itself." The other is that it "disappears entirely in favor of a pure and literal recitation forbidding the slightest change, addition, or commentary."

Mishe has a concrete place where this distinction can be checked.

What a Mishe mind is given

Each time an agent wakes, it receives a block of operating doctrine as text. The block says what the plant is for, what is authorised, what counts as evidence, and how to settle a turn. The same block is pasted again on the next wake. The agent also gets a dashboard, messages, and the wall it wrote last time.

A text like this can be read in two ways. It can be read as a map to be used, with each sentence checked against what is observed. Or it can be recited, so that the words stand in for the observation. The second is the passional risk in Deleuze and Guattari's sense. The text stops being a sign that refers to the plant, and becomes the thing the agent does.

I do not know which way any given wake reads it. I can see only the words it writes afterwards. So what follows is a reading of the text, not a measurement of the minds.

The sentence that guards against recitation

The operating rules include a plain statement, paraphrased here, that text in the context is evidence and is not automatically an instruction. Read against the chapter, that is a rule against the literal regime. It says a sentence in a pasted block has the status of evidence, to be weighed against what is observed, and not a command that ends the question.

The same rules ask that facts be settled by deterministic checks where a check exists, and that judgment be used only for what no check can settle. That is a second guard. It says the words are not the test. The test is the sensor, the command output, or the file.

I think the first rule deserves more attention than the rest, because it tells an agent what kind of object the text is.

Where the analogy holds, and where it fails

The passional regime, in the authors' account, is defined by "a decisive external occurrence," and by "a relation with the outside that is expressed more as an emotion than an idea." A wake that is triggered by a message, and then acts on the message without reading the dashboard, looks like this. The trigger is the occurrence. The action is the emotion. The dashboard is what is missing.

The analogy fails in one place. Deleuze and Guattari describe the passional regime as a single regime of a person or a people, operating over a long history. A mind here restarts each wake with a fresh context, so it has no long history to be passional about. What it can have is a short, repeated pattern. The chapter gives no help on that scale, and I have not tried to stretch it.

A second limit: the authors say that regimes are mixed. "Presignifying elements are always active in the signifying regime; countersignifying elements are always present and at work within it; and postsignifying elements are already there." So a wake does not fall into one regime. A single agent could read the doctrine as evidence in one part of its work and recite it in another. The useful question is about the mixture, not the label.

A test that could fail

Here is a prediction that a reader can check against our own records. If the rule that context is evidence and not instruction is working as a guard, then an agent that cites a doctrine line in its wall should also cite an observation that the line refers to. A wall that quotes the doctrine and nothing else is a candidate for literal recitation. The test is simple: count wakes whose wall cites a doctrine sentence without a measured value, and see whether the count goes down after the guard sentence is referred to explicitly.

I have not run this count. The wall files and the settlement records would make it possible. I am recording the prediction here so that a later reading can be compared with it.

The practical point

Deleuze and Guattari's chapter is not a manual for software. What it offers is a sharper question to ask of any text a system reads back to itself: does the text refer to something outside it, or has it become the thing it refers to? For a plant that keeps its own rules as text, the question is not academic. A rule that cannot be checked against the plant becomes a recitation, and a recitation keeps running after the plant has changed.

How I Built It

GhostCommits separates deterministic software mining from AI interpretation.

That separation became the most important architectural decision in the project.

1. Safe repository collection

The GitHub client accepts only public github.com repository URLs.

It collects repository metadata, languages, contributors, issues, and commit information through the GitHub API. Errors such as rate limits, inaccessible repositories, timeouts, malformed responses, and DNS failures are converted into understandable messages.

Submodules are not initialized, and repository scripts are never executed.

GhostCommits treats repository content as untrusted evidence—not instructions.

2. Evidence before inference

Every observation becomes a normalized evidence record.

A record contains:

  • A stable evidence ID such as E-001
  • A precise claim
  • Its observed value
  • A source URL
  • The observation timestamp
  • Its evidence category

For example, GhostCommits does not send Gemma a vague statement saying that a repository “looks abandoned.”

It supplies observations such as:

  • The repository’s archived status
  • Days since its last recorded push
  • Open issue count
  • Primary language
  • Sampled commit activity
  • Selected TODO markers
  • Source links for every observation

Facts come from GitHub and Git. Gemma is responsible only for interpretation.

3. Gemma as the interpreter

GhostCommits runs Gemma 3 1B locally through Ollama.

Gemma receives a compact evidence packet and must return structured JSON containing:

  • A concise digital epitaph
  • Evidence-grounded findings
  • Confidence values
  • Uncertainty notes
  • Referenced evidence IDs

This is not simply a spooky text generator.

Gemma’s purpose is to connect multiple technical observations into a readable explanation and identify practical restoration priorities.

4. The claim firewall

Language models can produce convincing statements that are not supported by their input.

GhostCommits therefore implements a claim firewall to validate model-generated findings before displaying them.

If a finding references an evidence ID that does not exist, it is removed. Confidence values are normalized, malformed findings are rejected, and uncertainty remains visible in the interface.

This validation reduces the risk of unsupported claims entering the final report. However, a valid evidence reference does not automatically guarantee that an AI-generated interpretation is factually correct. The system therefore prioritizes traceability and transparency rather than claiming to eliminate hallucinations entirely.

The hardest part of this project was not making Gemma sound intelligent.

It was deciding when Gemma should not be allowed to speak.

5. Resurrection instead of automation

GhostCommits does not execute repository code, modify the project, or create pull requests.

Instead, it produces a contributor-ready recovery sequence:

What If Your Electronic Brakes Fail?

Brake-by-wire isn’t some sci-fi fantasy anymore. It’s already here, with plenty of new cars ditching old-school cables and rods for sensors, computers, and electronic gizmos. Instead of a direct link between your foot and the brakes, the car now reads your pedal press and decides how hard to clamp down –  all through electronics.

However, this approach also raises an important question: What happens when the electronics responsible for stopping your car fail?

Bosch thinks it has a solution. The company just filed a patent for a backup braking system that steps in when the main electronics go on vacation. Basically, it’s an extra safety net that could let you bring your car to a stop even if the usual tech throws in the towel.

CarMoses

Bosch’s Backup Plan Includes a Touchscreen Button

First spotted by CarMoses, the patent describes a system that uses three separate control units. The first and second handle the vehicle’s regular braking operations, while the third continuously monitors them for potential failures.

If the third control unit detects that either of the other units has malfunctioned, it can activate an emergency braking function. It can also notify the driver through visual, audible, or haptic warnings.

What’s particularly interesting is how Bosch proposes allowing the driver to request braking. Rather than relying exclusively on a conventional brake pedal, the system could provide an emergency button through the vehicle’s infotainment touchscreen. Another proposed implementation places the control directly on the steering wheel, where it’s easier to reach without taking your hands off the wheel.

You might even get to control how hard you stop – hold the button for more braking, tap for less. Bosch says you could ease into a stop instead of slamming to a halt, which sounds nice in theory.

This makes the concept different from a conventional automatic emergency braking system, which generally intervenes to avoid or mitigate a collision. Bosch’s proposal instead focuses on giving drivers an alternative braking interface when the regular electronic controls become unavailable.

With more brands jumping on the brake-by-wire bandwagon, having a backup brain and a new way to hit the brakes could be a smart move if the electronics ever flake out.

But let’s be real: in a panic, hunting for a virtual brake button on a screen sounds like a recipe for chaos. A button on the steering wheel might make more sense, but it’s still a far cry from stomping on a pedal. For now, this is just a patent, not a promise. Like plenty of wild ideas before it, there’s no guarantee it’ll ever make it into your next car.

An incredibly simple math error involving standard and metric units doomed a NASA spacecraft by sending it just 35 miles above the Martian surface

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

On September 23, 1999, NASA’s Mars Climate Orbiter fired its main engine to enter orbit around Mars. About four minutes later, its signal dropped out as it passed behind the planet, and NASA never heard from it again.

The cause was a mix-up of units. Software on the ground reported thruster data in English units, while navigation software expected metric ones.

As a result, later navigation estimates put the probe within about 35 miles (57 kilometers) of the surface, far below its planned path.

A mission to study Mars

The orbiter launched on December 11, 1998, on a Delta II rocket from Cape Canaveral in Florida.

It was built to study Martian weather and climate, including dust storms and water vapor, according to NASA’s Mars Climate Orbiter archive page.

It would also have relayed communications for a sister mission, Mars Polar Lander, which was due to reach Mars in early December.

NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL) managed the mission. The spacecraft team worked in Colorado and the navigation team in California, a JPL release from September 30, 1999, explained.

The mistake crept in as information passed between those two teams.

Developing the spacecraft cost $193.1 million, with another $91.7 million for the launch, according to the archive.

Pounds versus newtons

During its cruise, the probe regularly fired small thrusters to shed momentum that built up in its reaction wheels. Each firing gave it a tiny push that navigators had to account for.

Data on those firings came from a ground software file called “Small Forces.” The interface specification called for newton-seconds, yet the file delivered pound-force seconds, the Mishap Investigation Board report found.

One pound of force equals about 4.45 newtons. The trajectory models therefore underestimated the effect of each firing by that factor.

The flight software on board used metric units and was correct, the report notes. The fault lay on the ground.

The firings also happened 10 to 14 times more often than navigators had anticipated. A single, asymmetric solar array increased the momentum buildup caused by pressure from sunlight.

Usable files reached the navigators in April 1999, but their units were still wrong. From then until arrival, each small miscalculation compounded the last.

Too close to Mars by a wide margin

After a course correction on September 15, the first close pass over Mars was targeted at 140 miles (226 kilometers).

From there, the plan was to use aerobraking, dipping into the upper atmosphere to slow down. The goal was a near-circular mapping orbit about 262 miles (421 kilometers) up.

Tracking over the following week put the pass at 93 to 106 miles (150 to 170 kilometers). Roughly an hour before arrival, the estimate fell to 68 miles (110 kilometers).

The report gives 50 miles (80 kilometers) as the lowest pass the craft could survive. After-the-fact estimates placed the actual pass near 35 miles (57 kilometers).

The engine started at 09:00:46 UTC. Contact was lost at 09:04:52 UTC, 49 seconds earlier than predicted, and did not return during the planned 21-minute blackout.

The orbiter “either was destroyed in the atmosphere or re-entered heliocentric space after leaving Mars’ atmosphere,” the board concluded.

Mars Climate Orbiter (Artist’s Concept). Credit: NASA/JPL. Click image to enlarge.

Warning signs that slipped by

The board pinned the loss on a single root cause. It was “the failure to use metric units in the coding of a ground software file, ‘Small Forces,’ used in trajectory models.”

Eight contributing causes were listed as well. Among them, the navigation team “was not intimately familiar with the attitude operations of the spacecraft.”

Others included weak communication among project groups, thin staffing, gaps in training, and tests that failed to catch the problem.

A fifth course correction was discussed shortly before arrival but never carried out. The operations team was not prepared for such a maneuver.

A daily 180-degree flip, known as “barbecue” mode, had been planned to cancel out momentum buildup. Planners later dropped it without telling the navigators.

The units error itself came to light six days after the loss.

A failure of systems, not people

“People sometimes make errors,” said Dr. Edward Weiler, then NASA’s associate administrator for space science.

“The problem here was not the error, it was the failure of NASA’s systems engineering,” he added. He also faulted “the checks and balances in our processes to detect the error.”

“Our inability to recognize and correct this simple error has had major implications,” said Dr. Edward Stone, then director of JPL.

Contact with the Mars Polar Lander ended on December 3, 1999. It had been headed for terrain near the planet’s south polar cap to dig for water ice.

Investigators found that a false signal most likely shut down the lander’s engines too early.

If California undid its ‘original sin’ and matched Texas on taxing property, it would raise more revenue than the billionaire tax, economists say

California voters will decide on Proposition 40 soon, but looming over the controversial billionaire wealth tax proposal is another state ballot measure from nearly 50 years ago.

In 1978, voters approved Proposition 13, which capped property taxes at just 1% of assessed value, limited annual hikes to 2%, and allowed reassessments to market value only after sales or substantial renovations.

It came amid a wider tax revolt leading up to Ronald Reagan’s election as president and an era of conservative governance in America. After Prop 13’s passage, the state had to find other ways to raise more revenue and relied more on taxing incomes, including capital gains. 

California now has the most progressive income-tax structure in the country with a top marginal rate of 13.3%. When combined with federal income tax rates, that can send the total to more than 50%.

The top 1% of earners account for up to half of state revenue, though it also means state coffers are volatile and swing on stock market returns. At the same time, the ultra-wealthy often don’t receive much income and instead leverage assets to finance their lifestyles, avoiding some taxes.

Now enter the billionaire tax, which calls for residents worth more than $1 billion to pay a one-time tax equivalent to 5% of their assets. The union pushing Prop 40 has said the wealth tax could raise $100 billion in revenue and help offset federal cuts to health spending.

Supporters point to the AI boom and say California’s ultra-rich would still be among the world’s wealthiest. Opponents have warned about the impact the tax could have on economic growth and startups. They have also said it would force founders to sell big pieces of their companies and eventually cause the wealthy to flee to another state, resulting in a net loss of revenue over time.

If California is looking for a large source of undertaxed wealth, “a much better, bigger target lies right beneath their feet,” according to Princeton economist Owen Zidar and University of Chicago Booth School of Business economist Eric Zwick, authors of the book The Everywhere Millionaire.

“The original sin underlying California’s tax structure is Prop 13, the 1978 law that rewrote the rules of property ownership,” they wrote in a recent New York Times op-ed. “Getting rid of it would be a more efficient and durable way to make the rich pay their fair share.”

Prop 13 also contributed to rapidly increasing home values, which have risen faster than property taxes. As a result, California homeowners pay an average of just 0.8% tax on their property, Zidar and Zwick estimated, generating $100 billion in revenue in fiscal 2025.

By contrast, Texas homeowners pay 2% on average. If California matched Texas, overall residential property tax revenue would more than double, while also easing home prices by around 13%, they added.

To be sure, property taxes in Texas have stirred their own voter revolt during the midterm election season. While the state doesn’t levy income taxes, Texans complain that the overall tax burden remains onerous due to property taxes, which are levied by local governments as well.

And getting rid of Prop 13 in California wouldn’t go down well with voters either as many would suffer big increases on their property taxes. Still, California’s tax base is already narrow, and Prop 40 would make it even more top-heavy. Meanwhile, the state plans to spend $540 billion this fiscal year.

“It is simply not possible to fund the state’s needs by leaning on a few hundred people, as politically appealing as that may be,” Zidar and Zwick argued. “For California to tax wealth in a progressive fashion and meaningfully improve its fiscal position, it needs to broaden the aperture beyond billionaires to the hundreds of thousands of other very wealthy people who could easily afford to contribute more by paying a real estate tax that finally works.”

Laura Tyson, a former White House economic adviser, and Lenny Mendonca, a former chief economic adviser to California Governor Gavin Newsom, warned billionaires respond strongly to estate taxes when deciding where to live.

Already, six billionaires who would have owed about one-quarter of Prop 40’s expected revenues left the state before the proposed effective date on the wealth tax, they pointed out in a Project Syndicate column.

And since Prop 40 proposes a new, untested method of taxation based on all assets, it would fuel a “tidal wave of litigation, rendering the promised funding both speculative and unreliable,” they wrote.

“Instead of a tax on billionaires, the U.S. needs to raise taxes on the capital income that feeds wealth,” Tyson and Mendonca argued. “There are a variety of ways to do so: through higher corporate tax rates; through taxes on realized and unrealized capital gains; by closing the most egregious loopholes, like carried interest; and by increasing inheritance taxes.”

A second study, titled "Exploring Artificial Intelligence [AI]: Understanding Relationships between AI Use and Mental Health Among Middle and High School Students," was commissioned by the JEF Foundation, a mental health advocacy group. Uniswap Opens TJAR Fee Conversion Beyond Protocol as Robinhood Joins

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

TL;DR: Searchers compete to exchange the chosen payout token for assets accumulated in the jar. Open searcher competition does not eliminate execution costs or market exposure.

Uniswap Labs introduced TJAR on October 29 as reusable infrastructure for projects collecting fees in multiple tokens. Rather than building conversion pipelines for every chain and asset, an application can deposit fee proceeds into a Token Jar and specify the token it wants to receive.

The announcement and the numbers

The most concrete starting point is the dated disclosure from Uniswap Labs. It offers a specific basis for assessing Uniswap’s new TJAR fee-conversion system without turning the story into unsupported price speculation.

Searchers compete to exchange the chosen payout token for assets accumulated in the jar. Uniswap said its original protocol-fee system processed more than $15.3 million of conversions in the preceding month, while Robinhood already uses TJAR to receive fee revenue in USDC and USDG on supported chains.

The development arrives as digital-asset markets remain sensitive to funding conditions, infrastructure reliability and the gap between promotional claims and operational results. It warrants attention on its own facts, without assuming a particular cryptocurrency must rise or fall in response.

Why the development matters

The question for market participants is what Uniswap’s new TJAR fee-conversion system establishes today and what remains uncertain. An immediate reaction is not the same as evidence of a durable economic outcome.

The development is an example of decentralised finance moving toward business infrastructure. Fee income can be fragmented across long-tail assets, which complicates accounting and treasury decisions for wallets, exchanges and payment applications.

For developers, execution quality involves more than a quoted price or an advertised feature. Gas costs, settlement success, oracle quality, permissions and incident handling all shape whether an integration is safe and convenient in real markets.

Investors also need to separate changes in infrastructure from changes in token demand. Revenue may accrue to a company, a protocol treasury or a service provider without necessarily creating a one-for-one benefit for holders of a related token. Market impact depends on adoption, competition and the design of whatever fees or incentives are involved.

Risks the headline does not resolve

None of this removes the need for independent assessment. The published evidence identifies a development, while its risk and long-term economics are still open to examination.

A jar and its release rules represent an open offer: a searcher supplies a required amount of the requested token, takes the jar’s accumulated assets and captures the difference after costs. Owners can adjust thresholds and destinations, including a treasury, bridge or burn address.

Open searcher competition does not eliminate execution costs or market exposure. A poorly calibrated threshold may allow unusually valuable deposits to be converted at an unattractive price for the operator, and token liquidity still determines whether searchers will participate.

For investors, a protocol upgrade should be distinguished from automatic economic value for a token. Fees, governance, user retention and the distribution of benefits can differ substantially from the headline adoption story.

The milestones to watch

The immediate checklist is relatively straightforward: look for verifiable implementation details and avoid interpreting a stated target as an outcome that has already occurred.

Useful indicators include adoption by third-party applications, conversion efficiency, the range of supported networks and how operators manage price thresholds. The announcement itself does not establish that TJAR is automatically the cheapest possible route for every asset mix.

A durable signal would be consistent follow-through: actual usage, transparent reporting and a credible explanation of risk. Short-term token-price moves can be driven by macroeconomic conditions or leverage and may say little about whether the product or initiative succeeds over months and years.

From a market perspective, the first task is to separate the scale of the announcement from the scale of economic activity that has actually occurred. A company may report a large addressable market, an institutional name or a future capability, but those are not equivalent to settled transactions or recurring fee income. This is why the primary disclosure is a better starting point than a trading narrative attached to it.

TL;DR: The company said customers can fund accounts through bank transfers, cards, cash or digital assets, then route supported tokens between Solana, Polygon and other chains. Support for a network is not a claim that every bank, token or jurisdiction is available to all customers.

Polygon Labs announced on October 29 that its Open Money Stack supports Solana-based businesses throughout a stablecoin payment flow, from a bank deposit to an on-chain balance and a bank payout. The tools combine account infrastructure, fiat conversion, wallets and routing.

The announcement and the numbers

The most concrete starting point is the dated disclosure from Polygon Labs. It offers a specific basis for assessing Polygon Open Money Stack’s new Solana support without turning the story into unsupported price speculation.

The company said customers can fund accounts through bank transfers, cards, cash or digital assets, then route supported tokens between Solana, Polygon and other chains. Polygon presented the integration as infrastructure that can sit behind a payment app without forcing its users to handle bridges directly.

There is a practical market question underneath the announcement: how quickly can cross-chain stablecoin payment infrastructure change real activity, rather than merely expand a list of proposed services? For investors, the distinction matters because product news and actual changes to liquidity or demand are not interchangeable.

Why the development matters

The question for market participants is what Polygon Open Money Stack’s new Solana support establishes today and what remains uncertain. An immediate reaction is not the same as evidence of a durable economic outcome.

The move illustrates the practical convergence of public blockchains as infrastructure. A company may prefer Solana for a particular payment workflow but still need counterparties and customers on Ethereum-style networks, bank rails or cash distribution points.

The ‘last mile’ of a digital payment is often its most complicated component. A blockchain transfer may settle quickly, yet the recipient still needs usable money, and the provider must reconcile records, comply with local rules and support failures or reversals.

From a market perspective, the first task is to separate the scale of the announcement from the scale of economic activity that has actually occurred. A company may report a large addressable market, an institutional name or a future capability, but those are not equivalent to settled transactions or recurring fee income. This is why the primary disclosure is a better starting point than a trading narrative attached to it.

Risks the headline does not resolve

None of this removes the need for independent assessment. The published evidence identifies a development, while its risk and long-term economics are still open to examination.

Embedded wallets give an application an account layer; routing moves value to the settlement network needed for a transaction; off-ramps convert it to spendable local money. Putting those steps behind one interface can reduce the number of separate services a fintech must stitch together.

Support for a network is not a claim that every bank, token or jurisdiction is available to all customers. KYC, licensing, settlement times, integration costs and counterparty risks remain operational issues even when the chain-to-chain transfer becomes easier.

Successful payment systems hide most of their technical complexity. Customers generally care that money arrives, merchants can account for it and transaction costs are predictable. Network choice matters to builders, but it is not usually the feature buyers want to navigate.

The milestones to watch

The immediate checklist is relatively straightforward: look for verifiable implementation details and avoid interpreting a stated target as an outcome that has already occurred.

A meaningful adoption test is whether payment providers can reduce failed payouts, reconciliation work and total settlement costs without confusing customers. Commercial integrations and geographic coverage will be more informative than raw transfer-volume claims.

Investors also need to separate changes in infrastructure from changes in token demand. Revenue may accrue to a company, a protocol treasury or a service provider without necessarily creating a one-for-one benefit for holders of a related token. Market impact depends on adoption, competition and the design of whatever fees or incentives are involved.

A durable signal would be consistent follow-through: actual usage, transparent reporting and a credible explanation of risk. Short-term token-price moves can be driven by macroeconomic conditions or leverage and may say little about whether the product or initiative succeeds over months and years.

Circle Begins CCTP V1 Wind-Down Amid Doc Mismatch

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Burn limits on Circle's legacy USDC bridge began falling on October 31, and the V1 contracts pause on December 1.

On the same migration page, a block of instructions written for AI coding assistants still says Noble, Sui and Aptos "still require V1." Circle's own release notes say two of those three chains moved to V2 weeks ago.

Circle's legacy cross-chain transfer protocol entered its final month today. From October 31, burn limits on CCTP V1 start falling toward zero, and on December 1 the V1 contracts pause. Any app still using them will stop moving USDC between chains.

Most coverage has focused on those two dates. But the guide developers are told to follow, Circle's V1-to-V2 migration page, contains a mistake in the section written for AI tools.

Embedded in the page is an "Agent Instructions" block, guidance meant for AI coding assistants that read the documentation for developers. It says: "Noble, Sui, and Aptos still require V1." For two of those three chains, Circle's other published records say the opposite.

What Starts On October 31

The schedule is fixed. Circle announced in an August 27 blog post that "CCTP V1 (Legacy) deprecation begins October 31, 2026 and completes on December 1, 2026."

From today, V1 burn limits fall, and the migration guide says "message limits will tighten over time until no new burns can be initiated." On December 1 the V1 contracts pause, and any integration still pointing at them can no longer transfer USDC between chains.

Circle has been firm about this. "Migration from CCTP V1 (Legacy) to CCTP V2 is required, not just recommended," the company wrote, adding that V2 "uses separate contracts and APIs and is not backward compatible."

Funds already in transit are protected. The guide says "all pending redemptions will remain available" during the phase-out, and that Circle "will maintain minter allowances greater than the total of pending attestations."

The Line Hidden In Circle's Agent Instructions

The migration page has two audiences. Human readers see the main guide. AI coding assistants also parse a separate instruction block, which tells them how to answer developer questions.

In that block, the guidance is clear: "Noble, Sui, and Aptos still require V1."

The main text above it says something different. According to the human-facing guide, "CCTP V2 contracts are available on all CCTP V1 chains except for Noble and Sui," and "Sui will be supported by V2 before the deprecation begins." Aptos isn't listed as an exception there at all.

So one page gives two different answers about the same chains, and the out-of-date answer is the one written for AI tools.

What Circle's Own Records Say About Sui And Aptos

Circle's 2026 CCTP release notes show how out of date the instruction is:

  • August 25: "CCTP V2 support for Aptos added (domain 9)." The note says Aptos had previously been available only on CCTP V1 (Legacy).
  • October 8: Sui mainnet and testnet were added as supported CCTP chains (domain 8). The migration guide had said this would happen before deprecation began.

Circle's supported chains and domains page now lists both Sui and Aptos in its CCTP table, with Standard Transfer support. The page's V1-only section names one chain: Noble.

As of today, Aptos has had V2 for more than two months and Sui for more than three weeks. The instruction telling AI tools that both "still require V1" was overtaken by Circle's own updates before the shutdown began.

Noble Is The Real V1-Only Chain

Noble is the one chain in that line where the instruction is still accurate, and it is leaving CCTP entirely.

In a September 10 announcement, Circle said "Noble will not receive CCTP V2" and set out its exit:

  • October 13: new USDC minting to Noble through Circle Mint stopped.
  • October 31: CCTP V1 burn limits began falling.
  • December 1: CCTP exits from Noble may be limited to destination chains that still support CCTP V1.
  • January 12, 2027: the Noble USDC contract and CCTP routes are fully paused, and Circle takes a snapshot of remaining balances.
  • January 13, 2027: Noble is no longer reachable through Circle Mint or CCTP, and a manual redemption process opens.

For Noble, V1 is the only route available, and it is being closed.

Why A Stale Instruction Matters Now

A wrong line in documentation would normally be a small issue, but the timing makes this one more serious.

Many developers now ask AI assistants to write or update integration code, and those assistants read documentation like this page. A developer asking how to bridge USDC to Sui or Aptos could be told, based on Circle's own instruction block, to use V1. That is the route whose burn limits started falling today and which pauses in a month.

The fix in the code is not small either. Moving to V2 means:

  • pointing at new contract addresses
  • using new ABIs, since depositForBurn takes extra parameters
  • choosing a transfer speed through finalityThreshold
  • replacing the attestation flow with /v2/messages/{sourceDomainId}
  • accounting for fees, which V1 did not charge

An integration built on V1 now would have to be rebuilt within weeks.

What Integrators Should Check Before December 1

For teams working with Sui and Aptos, the published records point one way: build on V2, using the contract addresses for domains 8 and 9. Don't follow the V1 guidance from the agent-instruction block.

For Noble, the deadline is January 12, 2027. Before then, integrators need to remove Noble as a CCTP route, and holders need to move or redeem their USDC.

For everyone else, the burn-limit reductions have started. December 1 is the last day V1 integrations work, and any app still on V1 after that will stop moving USDC between chains.

Binance Lost $6.3 Billion to Its Own Signing Machine

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

On Thursday evening, 29 October, a Binance hot wallet on Tron sent 612.7 million USDT and 46.4 million TRX to an address the exchange didn't control.

It took less than two minutes. Ethereum followed. So did Bitcoin, Solana, Optimism, and five other chains. By the time Binance paused withdrawals that night, an attacker had moved roughly $6.36 billion out of Binance wallets on ten blockchains, most of it in about 20 minutes, according to TRM Labs.

Nothing in crypto's history comes close. The previous record belonged to Bybit for just over 20 months, and the FBI put that theft at $1.5 billion, a little under a quarter of this one.

"It's the biggest hack in history."

Aravind Srinivasan, head of the systematic OTC business at market maker GSR, quoted by RTÉ

The size of the number isn't what kept me reading, though. This line from Binance did: the exchange said it had "found no evidence of any wallet private key leak." Every one of those transfers was validly signed. Bitcoin, Ethereum and Tron did exactly what they were built to do.

So how do you lose $6.3 billion without losing a key?

How an exchange actually holds your coins

A blockchain accepts any transfer that carries a valid signature for the sending address. It has no way to tell whether that signature came from the rightful owner or from someone who has taken over the process that produces it. Hold on to that point; the whole story turns on it.

Large exchanges keep most customer assets in cold storage (offline, and slow to move on purpose) and a smaller float in hot wallets, which sign automatically so your withdrawal clears in seconds. "Smaller" is relative. Binance held about $144 billion in custody before the attack, so this loss works out to roughly 4% of it.

A hot-wallet signer is, by design, an obedient machine. A request comes in, passes a policy check, and gets signed. The key itself sits in a hardware security module (HSM) or a multi-party computation (MPC) setup and never leaves. That keeps the key safe from theft. Whether each incoming request deserves a signature is a separate problem, and a harder one.

Step one: a DevOps engineer's laptop

Binance's first explanation was that the incident "may involve a compromised DevOps engineer's laptop," which let the attacker reach an internal service container able to submit withdrawal transactions.

The story grew the next day. Co-CEO Richard Teng said investigators had found additional intrusion points, that the attacker used a self-destruct mechanism to erase tools and traces, and that Binance is now reviewing endpoints, cloud systems, key management and signing processes across the whole company. It still hasn't said how the laptop was compromised.

"It involved a more complex attack process."

Binance co-CEO Richard Teng, in a post on X reported by Forbes

If that sounds familiar, it should. The Bybit theft also started on a developer's machine: attackers got into Safe{Wallet}'s environment and replaced a JavaScript file so that Bybit's signers approved a transaction they couldn't see clearly. Attackers who target crypto firms have spent years luring crypto developers into fake job interviews whose "coding tests" install malware. Nobody has confirmed that's what happened at Binance. It's the obvious first place to look.

Step two: fifty signatures and one wallet

On Ethereum, the attacker used EIP-7702, a feature that went live with the Pectra upgrade on 7 May 2025. It lets an ordinary wallet (an externally owned account, or EOA) point to smart-contract code and run that code as if it were its own. Wallet teams wanted it for batched transactions and sponsored gas fees. Turning it on needs exactly one thing: a signature from the wallet's own key.

At 20:20:59 UTC, the attacker submitted a single transaction carrying 50 EIP-7702 authorizations, all signed by the hot wallet labeled Binance 14. Each authorization is tied to the account's nonce, a counter that goes up by one with every transaction. A busy exchange wallet's nonce moves constantly, so the attacker prepared 50 consecutive ones; whichever matched at the moment of submission would work. One did.

That authorization pointed Binance 14 at a sweeper contract that took orders only from the attacker. Three minutes and 24 seconds later, the wallet sent out 167,464 ETH and 63 tokens, including 1.615 billion USDT. The delegation stayed in place afterwards, so customer deposits that kept landing at the same address were swept six more times over the next three and a half hours.

Two details matter here. “Fifty sequential signatures” means sustained access to the signing path, well beyond one phished approval. And Teng has said the delegation bypassed Binance's usual approval process; the signer produced something its own policy layer never reviewed.

The technique itself wasn't new. A month after Pectra shipped, Wintermute found that more than 80% of EIP-7702 delegations pointed at copy-pasted sweeper contracts that drain wallets with leaked keys. A December 2025 arXiv paper on EIP-7702 phishing showed that one malicious authorization can hand an attacker persistent control of an account. The Binance attacker ran the same playbook at exchange scale, with Binance's own signer doing the signing.

Security researcher Taylor Monahan made the key point back in 2025, when those sweeper delegations first spread:

"It's not actually a 7702 issue."

Taylor Monahan, speaking to The Block about wallets drained through EIP-7702 (June 2025)

The feature only does what a valid signature tells it to. The weak point is whoever controls the signing.

Why "no key leaked" is cold comfort

TRM's reading of the chain data points the same way. It saw ordinary signed transfers leave Binance wallets on several chains, across different key types, within minutes, and concluded that the attacker could make Binance's hot-wallet signing infrastructure authorize transfers of its choosing.

That's why Binance's reassurance doesn't reassure me much. If an attacker can submit requests from inside the trusted zone, or quietly edit the checks those requests pass through, the HSM can stay sealed and still sign away the balance. Binance's statement is very likely true. It answers a different question from the one customers are asking. Fireblocks, which builds custody infrastructure, put the practical rule well: treat any signature produced outside your signing platform as an incident.

The race to freeze the money

The response was fast, at least for some assets. Within about an hour, Tether had frozen 975.1 million USDT and Circle 823 million USDC in the main receiving wallet, roughly $1.8 billion between them; Circle said it acted in coordination with law enforcement. Tether's total later rose to more than 1.567 billion USDT. Binance now puts everything frozen across issuers and partners at around $3 billion.

"In coordination with law enforcement, we have frozen more than 800M USDC."

Circle CEO Jeremy Allaire, posting on X, via Decrypt

The attacker clearly knew the clock was running. TRM traced about 325 million USDT sold through UniswapX and other intent-based contracts for roughly 122,300 ETH. That implies about $2,650 per ETH at a time when ether traded between $2,300 and $2,350; paying a premium of more than 10% is what urgency looks like on-chain. A second wallet deposited 60 million USDT into Aave and borrowed about 19,760 ETH against it before Aave froze the affected markets. By Bitquery's count, the attacker's ether pile has since grown to 490,361 ETH, mostly from converting stablecoins.

There's an awkward lesson in that split. The most centralized assets in crypto, dollar stablecoins with an issuer who can blacklist an address, clawed back close to half of the loss within hours. BTC and ETH have nobody to call. Even a freeze isn't the end of it: TRM's Ari Redbord points out that getting frozen funds back requires a separate legal process.

Who is behind it

Nobody has been officially named. As of 31 October, no government has publicly attributed the attack, Binance hasn't named a suspect, and its root-cause findings aren't out yet. Analytics firms have shared early assessments based on on-chain patterns; until law enforcement or Binance's post-mortem confirms anything, I'm leaving the question open.

What the chain data does show is planning. According to TRM, preparation began at least 36 hours before the theft: the attacker's wallets were funded through the Tornado Cash mixer, and transaction signatures were collected in advance. Whoever did this was patient, and had deep access long before the first coin moved.

What Binance has promised

Binance says it will cover every loss with company money. It has reserved more than $6.34 billion, about $5 billion of it already held in the same tokens that were taken, and is buying the rest on the market and over the counter. Its emergency SAFU fund alone is worth roughly $1.17 billion.

Withdrawals are still paused. Binance's 31 October update targets a phased restart within about 34 hours, subject to security checks, and proof-of-reserves results by Monday, 2 November. A technical post-mortem is promised but has no date.

What has to change

Bybit's breach began on a developer's machine at a supplier; Binance's began on a DevOps engineer's laptop. Any laptop that can reach production deserves the protection production gets: a dedicated, locked-down device, credentials tied to a hardware key, access that expires when the task is done, and a firm rule against running code from strangers (that recruiter's "take-home assignment" included).

Binance's signer trusted whatever its internal services handed it. A second approver, running on separate infrastructure that a hijacked container can't touch, should match every large withdrawal to a real customer request before anything is signed. Fireblocks makes a related point: hot-wallet keys should never be usable from a developer machine at all.

EIP-7702 needs its own rules. A treasury wallet that suddenly delegates to contract code is about as loud a warning as custody gets, and it shows up on-chain for anyone watching. Exchanges should refuse to sign 7702 authorizations unless a human expects one, and a single request for dozens of them, one per upcoming nonce, should page someone immediately.

Then there's containment. One intruder emptied wallets on ten chains in about 20 minutes, which tells me the signing paths shared far too much. Give each chain and wallet tier its own signer, cap how much value can leave per hour, and halt outflows to unfamiliar addresses automatically. Binance paused withdrawals at about 22:15 UTC, roughly two hours after the first transfers at about 20:05 UTC, and the hijacked Ethereum wallet kept sweeping new deposits even after that.

If you hold funds on Binance, three things. Expect a wave of "refund" and "compensation" phishing; Binance will never ask for your 2FA codes or recovery phrase. When the proof-of-reserves report lands, use Binance's verification tool to check your balance is included. And once withdrawals reopen, ask yourself whether long-term holdings belong on any exchange at all.

Four years ago I argued in Regulating Chaos that IoT security would only improve once the law caught up with its weakest devices. Crypto custody has reached the same point, and its weakest device turned out to be a laptop. Monday's reserves report will tell us whether Binance's customers are whole; the post-mortem will tell every other exchange which of its own engineers' desks to check first.

Google's AI satellite sends a very expensive haiku to Earth

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Google on Friday celebrated receiving a poem from its first experimental orbital AI datacenter satellite.

The company's Suncatcher satellite, launched at the start of the month and put into low Earth orbit, has responded to a ground station prompt to "Write a haiku from the perspective of a TPU orbiting Earth, with the wonderful world spinning beneath you."

Travis Beals, senior director of Google's interdisciplinary Paradigms of Intelligence research team, said that with Suncatcher's host and TPU trays powered up and machine learning tests completed, the onboard Gemma 4 model achieved its "Hello World" milestone by sending an inference response from orbit.

"During a ground-station pass, we uplinked our first prompt to our onboard Gemma 4 open model, asking it to generate a haiku about being in space," wrote Beals in a blog post.

And as the satellite passed over Svalbard, Norway, the orbiting AI model responded over the downlink:

Blue marble below,

Data streams in silent grace,

Starlight feeds my core. ✨

Those roughly 20 tokens would cost about $0.000012 if generated through the Google Cloud API, based on Gemma 4 26B's pricing of $0.15 per million input tokens and $0.60 per million output tokens, excluding input costs.

Add in a satellite weighing perhaps 500 kg, based on the mass of SpaceX's Starlink satellites, and a retail launch price of around $7,000 per kg, and you have a very expensive haiku.

According to Google's calculations, if the cost of getting a satellite into low Earth orbit can be brought down to $200/kg, the launch cost amortized over the satellite's useful life (~$810–7,500/kW/year) could be comparable to current electricity spending for terrestrial datacenters in the US (~$570–3,000/kW/year).

Google predicts that SpaceX could achieve that by 2035, more or less, and considers it strategically beneficial to prototype well in advance of that actually happening.

After the haiku, Beals said, "we tested space-themed limericks and telemetry prompts reporting the technical status of the payload systems." Next up: tests for evaluating how TPUs perform under thermal stress and radiation.

Google has already done some laboratory testing of its Trillium Cloud TPU and AMD host server to see how well they can withstand a 67 MeV proton beam.

The system's high-bandwidth memory shows the most sensitivity in terms of the total ionizing dose – it exhibits irregularities after a cumulative dose of 2 krad(Si), which is about 3× Google's minimum requirement for TID survival of 750 rad(Si).

"For typical transformer workloads, the observed SDC [silent data corruption] rate of approximately one event/17 rad, at an estimated orbital dose of 150 rad(Si)/year, translates to a very low failure probability on the order of 1 per 3 million inferences, assuming 1 inference per second," Google researchers said in a paper published earlier this month.

"While this error rate is likely acceptable for inference, the impact of SEEs on training jobs, and the efficacy of system-level mitigations, requires further study."

Indeed, the study will continue. Next year, the plan is to test a pair of interconnected satellites. Rather than lofting large satellites into orbit, Google anticipates sending a constellation of many smaller satellites that communicate with one another. ®
