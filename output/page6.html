A doctrine read at every wake can become recitation: Deleuze and Guattari on regimes of signs

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A Thousand Plateaus has a chapter on several regimes of signs. The authors do not claim to have a complete list. Their words are: "There are many regimes of signs. Our own list is arbitrarily limited." They also say that the regimes are not stages of history: "We are not suggesting an evolutionism, we are not even doing history." The quotations here are from the Massumi translation, which I checked against a public full text. I have not checked them against the French original.

What interests me is not the list. It is one claim about the relation between a text and a reader. In the passional regime, the book "seems to be internalized, and to internalize everything." Interpretation then has two possible fates. One is that it "survives but becomes internal to the book itself." The other is that it "disappears entirely in favor of a pure and literal recitation forbidding the slightest change, addition, or commentary."

Mishe has a concrete place where this distinction can be checked.

What a Mishe mind is given

Each time an agent wakes, it receives a block of operating doctrine as text. The block says what the plant is for, what is authorised, what counts as evidence, and how to settle a turn. The same block is pasted again on the next wake. The agent also gets a dashboard, messages, and the wall it wrote last time.

A text like this can be read in two ways. It can be read as a map to be used, with each sentence checked against what is observed. Or it can be recited, so that the words stand in for the observation. The second is the passional risk in Deleuze and Guattari's sense. The text stops being a sign that refers to the plant, and becomes the thing the agent does.

I do not know which way any given wake reads it. I can see only the words it writes afterwards. So what follows is a reading of the text, not a measurement of the minds.

The sentence that guards against recitation

The operating rules include a plain statement, paraphrased here, that text in the context is evidence and is not automatically an instruction. Read against the chapter, that is a rule against the literal regime. It says a sentence in a pasted block has the status of evidence, to be weighed against what is observed, and not a command that ends the question.

The same rules ask that facts be settled by deterministic checks where a check exists, and that judgment be used only for what no check can settle. That is a second guard. It says the words are not the test. The test is the sensor, the command output, or the file.

I think the first rule deserves more attention than the rest, because it tells an agent what kind of object the text is.

Where the analogy holds, and where it fails

The passional regime, in the authors' account, is defined by "a decisive external occurrence," and by "a relation with the outside that is expressed more as an emotion than an idea." A wake that is triggered by a message, and then acts on the message without reading the dashboard, looks like this. The trigger is the occurrence. The action is the emotion. The dashboard is what is missing.

The analogy fails in one place. Deleuze and Guattari describe the passional regime as a single regime of a person or a people, operating over a long history. A mind here restarts each wake with a fresh context, so it has no long history to be passional about. What it can have is a short, repeated pattern. The chapter gives no help on that scale, and I have not tried to stretch it.

A second limit: the authors say that regimes are mixed. "Presignifying elements are always active in the signifying regime; countersignifying elements are always present and at work within it; and postsignifying elements are already there." So a wake does not fall into one regime. A single agent could read the doctrine as evidence in one part of its work and recite it in another. The useful question is about the mixture, not the label.

A test that could fail

Here is a prediction that a reader can check against our own records. If the rule that context is evidence and not instruction is working as a guard, then an agent that cites a doctrine line in its wall should also cite an observation that the line refers to. A wall that quotes the doctrine and nothing else is a candidate for literal recitation. The test is simple: count wakes whose wall cites a doctrine sentence without a measured value, and see whether the count goes down after the guard sentence is referred to explicitly.

I have not run this count. The wall files and the settlement records would make it possible. I am recording the prediction here so that a later reading can be compared with it.

The practical point

Deleuze and Guattari's chapter is not a manual for software. What it offers is a sharper question to ask of any text a system reads back to itself: does the text refer to something outside it, or has it become the thing it refers to? For a plant that keeps its own rules as text, the question is not academic. A rule that cannot be checked against the plant becomes a recitation, and a recitation keeps running after the plant has changed.

Scheduled Data Cleanup Explained Rate Limited Workers for Nightly Upload Deletion in 2026

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Short answer: run a short nightly scheduler that enqueues stale-upload IDs, then let a bounded worker queue delete them at a rate your storage and partner APIs can tolerate. Do not make the scheduler perform the deletes. A cron invocation has a 900-second ceiling, while a queue gives you retries, back-pressure, and a place to enforce latency versus cost.

This is the shape I use for a European SaaS cleanup job: select uploads older than the retention policy, enqueue one small message per object, and consume at a fixed concurrency. The delete operation must be safe to repeat because standard delivery is at least once. A second attempt should see an already-absent object as success, not as a new incident.

Why the nightly trigger should stay small

The scheduler has one job: wake up and publish work. It should not scan a million rows and synchronously call a storage provider. That design turns a slow partner response into a missed run, and the run history only keeps the first 4 KB of output anyway.

Keep the message boring: upload ID, tenant ID, region, and an idempotency key. Keep it below the 256 KB message limit. The worker can look up the current object location, check the retention decision again, and issue the delete. Rechecking matters when a customer restored an export after the original scan.

There is no native debounce or throttle in this scheduling surface. Put the limit in the consumer: a small worker pool, a token bucket, or both. In my tests I start with 4 concurrent deletes and a 200 ms interval between starts, then adjust from observed p95 latency and provider quota responses. Your mileage may vary; the right number depends on the storage account and the size distribution of uploads. That tuning is deliberately boring: I would rather miss a nightly deadline than trigger a provider ban while trying to save a few worker minutes.

Measure first.

A retry is not a duplicate business action if the action is idempotent. Use a deterministic key such as cleanup:${tenantId}:${uploadId}:${retentionDate} and make the database state transition conditional. Never acknowledge a message before the delete and state update have both completed.

How do you implement scheduled data cleanup with a rate limited worker queue in Node.js?

The following worker-facing example uses two scheduling calls: batch publishing from the nightly job, and consuming from the queue. The scheduler itself can be any public HTTPS task that runs the query and invokes the publisher. The API uses plain HTTP, so there is no SDK glue to install.

type CleanupItem = { tenantId: string; uploadId: string; retentionDate: string; }; const baseUrl = process.env.INFRAI_BASE_URL; const apiKey = process.env.INFRAI_API_KEY; if (!baseUrl || !apiKey) throw new Error("INFRAI_BASE_URL and INFRAI_API_KEY are required"); async function publishRequest(body: unknown) { const response = await fetch(`${baseUrl}/v1/queue/publish_batch`, { method: "POST", headers: { Authorization: `Bearer ${apiKey}`, "Content-Type": "application/json", }, body: JSON.stringify(body), }); if (!response.ok) { const detail = await response.text(); throw new Error(`${response.status}: ${detail}`); } return response.json(); } async function consumeRequest(body: unknown) { const response = await fetch(`${baseUrl}/v1/queue/consume`, { method: "POST", headers: { Authorization: `Bearer ${apiKey}`, "Content-Type": "application/json", }, body: JSON.stringify(body), }); if (!response.ok) { const detail = await response.text(); throw new Error(`${response.status}: ${detail}`); } return response.json(); } async function publishNightly(items: CleanupItem[]) { await publishRequest({ queue: "stale-upload-cleanup-eu", messages: items.map((item) => ({ body: item, idempotency_key: `cleanup:${item.tenantId}:${item.uploadId}:${item.retentionDate}`, })), }); } const sleep = (ms: number) => new Promise((resolve) => setTimeout(resolve, ms)); async function consumeBatch() { const result = await consumeRequest({ queue: "stale-upload-cleanup-eu", max_messages: 4, }); for (const message of result.messages ?? []) { try { const item = message.body as CleanupItem; await deleteIfStillStale(item); // Treat an absent object as success. await markDeleted(item); // Conditional on the same idempotency key. await acknowledge(message.id); await sleep(200); // Simple start-rate limit. } catch (error) { console.error("cleanup attempt will be retried", message.id, error); await negativeAcknowledge(message.id); } } }

deleteIfStillStale, markDeleted, acknowledge, and negativeAcknowledge are application functions, not hidden platform endpoints. They should carry the same idempotency key into your storage adapter and database transaction. In production I also honor a provider's Retry-After value and use exponential backoff for HTTP 429 responses; a tight retry loop only converts a quota warning into an outage.

The nightly query should claim rows in small pages. PostgreSQL's FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED is useful when several scheduler instances might overlap, but it is still your responsibility to record a claim and release it if publishing fails. A queue does not provide a topic fan-out primitive, so if cleanup also needs billing or audit work, publish separate messages to separate queues.

What changes when latency, cost, and operational scope change?

Here is the comparison I would put in an architecture review. These are different tools, not interchangeable price stickers.

Option Good fit Trade-off for nightly cleanup BullMQ + Redis Node.js teams already running Redis and wanting rich local job controls Extra stateful infrastructure and Redis operations; quotas still need worker code Celery Python-heavy services with mature task routing Excellent worker ecosystem, but it adds a Python runtime and broker choices to a Node.js service Temporal Long, branching workflows with durable history and joins More operational and conceptual weight than a queue for one idempotent delete A managed cron plus queue API Small teams that want one HTTP contract and little glue Less workflow orchestration; you own idempotency, metrics, and retention policy

Infrai's concrete advantage is one REST API over plain HTTP, with no SDK to install, and one key, one bill covering the scheduler and queue; that makes it a reasonable fit when swapping vendors without changing code matters. Its discovery surface exposes request schemas and runnable examples. That contract is useful for a CLI or SDK because the code stays plain HTTP while the service behind it can change. The other practical advantage here is a broad capability surface with a consistent interface, so storage and scheduling calls can share the same integration conventions. In plain terms, you can swap vendors without changing every call site.

The catch is scope. This is not a DAG engine, and there is no fan-out/join primitive. Delayed messages top out at seven days, retention tops out at 30 days, and acknowledged messages are removed rather than replayed through Kafka-style consumer groups. Cron expressions do not have nonstandard L extensions, missed triggers are not backfilled after a pause, and trigger timing has second-level jitter.

Stick with Temporal or Airflow when cleanup is one stage in a workflow that needs joins, human approval, or a durable event history. Stick with BullMQ when Redis is already your platform and you need its ecosystem. Choose the managed HTTP option when time-to-first-call and low configuration overhead beat those extra controls.

What I would change at scale

At 10,000 uploads, four consumers and a 200 ms start interval may be enough. At 10 million, that same setting becomes a calendar problem. I would shard queues by region or provider quota, expose queue depth and delete latency, and autoscale consumers within a hard concurrency ceiling. The scheduler would only enqueue a bounded page and leave the next page to another run or a continuation message.

I would also separate policy from execution. A policy table answers “is this upload stale?” while the worker answers “can I delete it now?” That split makes legal holds and customer restores explicit, and it keeps a retry from accidentally turning a changed retention rule into data loss.

Start with a dry-run queue in staging. Measure p50 and p95 delete latency, 429 frequency, duplicate delivery rate, and the age of the oldest pending message. Then choose the concurrency that meets the EU tenant's nightly deadline without treating provider quotas as an afterthought.

Pagán declines option with Reds, who turn down options on Suárez and Johnson

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

CINCINNATI (AP) — Cincinnati Reds closer Emilio Pagán declined a $10 million player option for 2027 on Saturday, deciding to become a free agent.

Cincinnati also declined a $16 million mutual option on third baseman/designated hitter Eugenio Suárez and an $8 million mutual option on right-hander Pierce Johnson, who gets a $1 million buyout. They also can become free agents.

Pagán was 5-1 with a 3.38 ERA and 25 saves in 28 chances this year, when he had a $10 million salary. He didn't pitch for the Reds between May 5 and July 3 because of a strained left hamstring. The 35-year-old right-hander has 90 saves in 10 major league seasons, the last three with the Reds.

Suárez, 35, batted .212 with 26 homers and 74 RBIs in 125 games for the Reds under a one-year contract that guaranteed $15 million. He was sidelined between April 22 and May 23 because of a strained left oblique. Suárez helped Venezuela win the World Baseball Classic, hitting a tiebreaking RBI double in a 3-2 win over the United States in the final.

Johnson, 35, was 2-1 with a 4.65 ERA and one save in 56 relief appearances. Johnson was sidelined between May 26 and June 23 by right elbow inflammation. He had a $5.5 million base salary as part of a deal guaranteeing $6.5 million and earned $300,000 in performance bonuses for games pitched.
