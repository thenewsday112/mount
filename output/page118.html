THISS STUDIO PUTS RADICAL REUSE BEFORE THE FINISHED DRAWING

 

An old barn, a pontoon boat, and a dismantled exhibition might seem like unlikely starting points for an architectural brief. For THISS Studio, they offer materials with which to begin designing. Founded by architects Tamsin Hanke and Sash Scott, the London practice works across cultural spaces, fashion, retail, and public installations, developing projects around the people and resources available to make them. Hanke’s research into low-impact design meets Scott’s interest in material experimentation and structural invention, an approach informed in part by his experience designing and flying high-performance kites. In an exclusive interview with designboom, Hanke explains how working with reclaimed components changes the architect’s role, the construction process, and the expectations attached to a finished space.

 

‘Primarily, reuse changes the order in which we design,’ Hanke tells designboom. A project can no longer be fully resolved on paper before its materials have been found. Instead, the studio establishes its intentions, gathers available stock, and revisits the design as that stock arrives, continuing to make adjustments during construction. Suppliers, craftspeople, and the dimensions and condition of individual components all influence the outcome. ‘With reuse, necessity means we can no longer be the sole authors of the outcome,’ he explains. This requires a strong concept and details capable of accommodating differences between materials. As Hanke puts it, ‘The work becomes a conversation rather than a monologue.’

Tamsin Hanke and Sash Scott, founders of THISS Studio | image by Jasper Fry

 

 

SALVAGED TIMBER AND AN UPTURNED BOAT SHAPE NEW SPACES

 

That willingness to let existing objects redirect a project is visible in Barn Again, developed with architect Tom Svilans for the 2025 Copenhagen Architecture Biennial. Beams recovered from disused Norwegian barns are stacked into a tapering pavilion, retaining weathered surfaces and traces of previous connections. Designed for disassembly and further use, the structure gives those irregularities a place in its architectural expression. At Kiko Kostadinov’s London flagship, a pontoon boat installation by artist Ryan Trecartin provides a very different starting point: inverted railings become a ceiling-mounted support system for the shop’s shelving. ‘Often the material will suggest a move we would never have drawn from scratch,’ Hanke says. Both projects make that possibility tangible, finding a new spatial role for something with an established identity.

 

The same attention to available resources extends to Pergola, a public outdoor theater in Cairo developed with CLUSTER and Orient Productions. Its red structure incorporates materials from local reuse initiatives, including textiles made from recovered river plastic by VeryNile and recycled-plastic bricks from Rebloxx. Elsewhere, the studio’s work on PoliNations demonstrates how a temporary installation can continue beyond its original setting. First presented in Birmingham in 2022, its monumental tree structures returned at Glastonbury’s Shangri-La in 2025. These projects approach material life at different scales, from individual components to substantial structures that can be used again. ‘Reuse should never dilute the conceptual ambition of a project,’ Hanke argues. ‘We are happily now in a position in the industry where reuse is not even enough to drive the design, it is just part of the process.’

monumental tree structures frame a temporary garden in Birmingham

 

 

EXHIBITION DESIGN STARTS WITH WHAT HAPPENS AFTERWARDS

 

For THISS Studio, exhibition design brings these questions into particularly sharp focus. A display may occupy a gallery briefly, yet its construction can determine whether its materials remain useful long after visitors leave. ‘Exhibition design is never neutral,’ Hanke says. ‘It participates in the conversation the exhibition is having with the work, and that conversation is ideological, even political.’ Discussing work with the V&A, he describes an all-timber assembly without metal fixings, conceived to preserve the components for subsequent use. The design considers how pieces will come apart and what condition they will be in when they do. ‘As we see it, an exhibition should be an interruption in a material’s life not the end of it.’

 

Working with the Design Museum has prompted another shift in method: taking stock before drawing. The studio catalogued and assessed existing exhibition infrastructure, then tracked design decisions against that inventory, scrutinizing each proposed addition. ‘We worked more like archaeologists or archivists than conventional architects,’ Hanke explains. Here, the challenge becomes how to alter the experience of a space through careful adjustments to what is already available. ‘The creative challenge is restraint: making something feel new while touching as little as possible.’ The approach gives the less visible work of sorting, documenting, and evaluating materials a direct influence on the architecture visitors eventually encounter.

repurposed boat railings support shelving inside Kiko Kostadinov’s store

 

 

MAKING REUSE WORK MEANS CHANGING THE COMMISSION

 

Turning this approach into routine practice requires institutions to reconsider how they commission and fund design. Hanke identifies a tension between the certainty clients expect at the outset and the adjustments that reclaimed materials can demand later. Design programs need to accommodate those revisions, while funding must arrive early enough to secure stock before rapid exhibition construction schedules take over. He points to organizations such as Yes Make, which can source and hold materials while a design develops. ‘Money and materials need to move earlier,’ he says. A willingness to reuse therefore has practical consequences for budgets, procurement, and decision-making, well before the first component reaches the gallery.

 

Long-term galleries introduce further pressures, particularly around maintenance and durability. Hanke argues that these requirements need not exclude previously used materials, citing the studio’s research into the recovery of hardwoods and cross-laminated timber after extended service. What matters is designing both the assembly and its eventual separation with sufficient care. ‘Dismantling should be designed, not improvised. The materials should leave as a resource not as waste.’ For THISS Studio, the architect’s responsibility consequently extends beyond the appearance and performance of an installation during its public life. It includes organizing the collaboration that produces it and anticipating what its parts might become once the exhibition is over.

Male black bear ‘Nugget’ wins Introvert Award for his adorable hiding technique

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Like the patient Paul, a male black bear (Ursus americanus) named Nugget would rather hang back alone than jump right into the salmon fest. For his solo tendencies, the team at Tongass National Forest in southeastern Alaska have awarded Nugget the 2026 Introvert Award.

“For much of the season, Nugget could be seen wedged between rocks by lower falls,” the U.S. Forest Service team writes. “If you saw a furry, black head with glassy eyes poking out of a crack—that’s Nugget! You could also find him perched on a rock waiting for his turn to fish in the lower falls hierarchy; afraid to assert his dominance in the crowd.”

Nugget was first spotted in the area only two years ago, and is beloved for his small size. He has a reddish coat that shifts to black later in the season. Nugget is often described as “scrappy.” He mostly scavenged during his first season at the creek, but slowly learned to catch salmon.

“The pristine ecosystem around Anan Creek allows even small, scrappy bears like Nugget to thrive,” the Rangers write.

Meet Bears of Anan Creek

The U.S. Forest Service team at Tongass National Forest are currently doling out the Anan Bear Awards to the 39 bears spotted at the Anan Wildlife Observatory over the summer. 

The nation’s largest national forest, Tongass covers most of Southeast Alaska, including the capital city of Juneau. The area includes Mendanhall Glacier (a popular destination for Alaskan cruises), numerous hiking trails, eagles, and plenty of bears. One of the hotspots is Anan Creek, home to one of the largest runs of pink salmon in Southeast Alaska. 

According to Friends of Anan, 39 known bears feast on the salmon at Anan Creek. The population includes 31 black bears and eight brown bears (Ursus arctos). Like their chunky counterparts further north in Katmai National Park, the bears return annually each summer to eat salmon and teach their young how to fish and survive. And also like the fat bears of Katmai, every bear here has their own habits and quirks. 

The U.S. Forest Service tracks and documents every bear and explore.org’s cameras also broadcast live streams of their antics.

Olaver unveils multi-purpose Laneway Studio with "lantern-like" glass-block facade

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

In the garden of a Melbourne family home, a facade of glass blocks set within recycled white brick and a concrete band beam tops this two-storey studio by Australian architecture practice Olaver.

Olaver designed Laneway Studio to appear as a "beautiful object" reflecting light into the garden in the day and glowing like a lantern at night. The multi-purpose building was designed in response to the growing needs of the family of five, offering a practical alternative to renovating the existing home.

It houses a study-cum-guest-room with an en-suite and terrace on the first floor and an open-plan living area below, fitted with concealed laundry facilities and a bar.

The new studio block was constructed behind the main house and its newly configured garden, designed by landscape architect Penny Starr to include a pool and areas of planted foliage.

Large glass-panelled sliding doors leading into the garden from both the house and studio create a seamless flow between the interior and exterior living spaces.

Another sliding door on the opposing wall of the studio, made from oak with a steel-clad exterior, connects the studio's living space with the titular laneway behind the property.

"With large sliding doors on opposing walls, the ground floor can open right through, so it works as an extension of the garden and pool terrace to the laneway rather than a closed room", Olaver director Emlyn Olaver told Dezeen.

"Open, the space feels far bigger than its footprint, and the family can spread across the house, the pool and the studio as one connected space when entertaining", she continued.

Inside, the ground floor was designed to be minimal and open-plan to ensure its multi-functionality. It features neutral terrazzo flooring throughout. Oak cabinetry conceals storage, laundry facilities and a TV nook and frames a central bar area with a stainless-steel sink and surround.

"We wanted the studio to be calm, with joinery doing the heavy lifting so each space can change roles without feeling cluttered", Olaver explained.

"Every space had to do more than one job; a laundry, a living room or a bar depending on the day. That flexibility also gives the building a longer life, because the family's needs will keep changing as the kids grow up, move out and come back."

On the first floor's east-facing wall, overlooking the garden, a facade of glass blocks was installed above a concrete band beam to bypass planning restrictions on the use of conventional windows.

The glass-block facade brings in soft morning light while offering privacy to the first-floor interior and, viewed from the garden and house, transforms the studio into a "glowing lantern" at night, Olaver said.

The first-floor interiors were also designed to be multi-functional, comprising a combined study and guest bedroom with an en-suite and small roof terrace. Red wraparound tiling in the bathroom is echoed by red panelling in the nook of the foldable bed, itself concealed behind more oak joinery when not in use.

"The new studio's interiors echo the existing home's rich but calm textures," Olaver said. "Natural materials and tactile elements exist in both spaces, but the studio is slightly more playful in its approach."

The project tasked Olaver with extending the home of a family rapidly outgrowing their existing space.

"In Melbourne, the usual answer is yet another extension that eats into the backyard," she said. "Laneway Studio goes the other way, keeping the garden at the centre of the site and showing how a well-designed studio can add flexibility and density to an existing lot."

World Anti-Doping Agency’s Latest Banned List Sends a Warning to Athletes Looking for ‘Loopholes’

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

There is a certain kind of athlete who treats the World Anti-Doping Agency’s prohibited list like a treasure map. He reads it, finds something that isn’t printed there, and figures he’s discovered a loophole. The molecule has a name that sounds like a Wi-Fi password, the vial says “research use only,” and some guy with a podcast swears it repairs tendons, melts fat, or turns you into a gazelle. Perfect. What could possibly go wrong?

Well, WADA just made that little game harder. Its 2027 Prohibited List, which takes effect Jan. 1, now explicitly mentions peptides in the category for non-approved substances. The U.S. Anti-Doping Agency put out an advisory explaining the change and warning athletes not to assume an experimental peptide is legal simply because its particular alphabet soup isn’t spelled out on the list. That’s a useful distinction, because the peptide business has become a sprawling mess of legitimate medicine, promising research, questionable claims, and little bottles of who-the-hell-knows-what.

What Changes Are Included in WADA’s 2027 Prohibited List?

Before anybody starts screaming that WADA banned every peptide on earth, it didn’t. Peptides are chains of amino acids, and some are established medicines. Insulin is a peptide. The relevant category, called S0, covers pharmacological substances without approval for human therapeutic use by a government health authority anywhere in the world, provided they aren’t already covered by another prohibited class. Some peptides fall into other categories. The word “peptides” appearing in S0 is a clarification, not a declaration that every peptide drug is forbidden.

The important point is that experimental substances weren’t necessarily fair game yesterday. BPC-157, for example, was already prohibited under the rules for non-approved substances. Others, including certain growth-hormone-related compounds, are covered elsewhere. What changed is the language, making it even harder to pretend that an unlisted research chemical gets a free pass. And if you compete in a tested sport, “I couldn’t find it on Google” ranks right up there with road rage. No one is gonna buy it.

There are some other interesting additions as well. WADA names two experimental metabolic modulators, SLU-PP-332 and SLU-PP-915, as examples in its hormone and metabolic modulators category. The list also adds examples of diuretics and masking agents, including eplerenone and finerenone, drugs that have perfectly legitimate medical uses. That’s another reality athletes forget: the prohibited list isn’t just a catalog of things somebody invented to cheat. It can include ordinary prescription medications, depending on the drug and circumstances.

Meanwhile, semaglutide and tirzepatide, the active ingredients in two widely used families of weight-loss and diabetes treatments, remain on WADA’s monitoring program for 2027. Monitoring is not banning. WADA is collecting information about their use in sport; it has not put them on the prohibited list. The same distinction matters with caffeine, which is monitored in competition but not prohibited. Which makes me feel good that the people making the rules recognize a difference between watching something and convicting it.

While they were at it, they also tuned up the cannabis language. WADA clarified that psychoactive THC-related cannabinoids, including certain synthetic and semi-synthetic versions, are prohibited in competition. CBD itself isn’t prohibited, but a CBD product containing undeclared THC can still create a problem. Meaning, you run the risk of failing a drug test.

It doesn’t matter if you’re an Olympian, a fighter, a weightlifter, or some local competitor. Under anti-doping rules, athletes are responsible for what’s in their bodies. What’s missing off a label is not an alibi. Neither is a coach or a doctor. You’re on your own. So play the game wisely.

I am 100% for researching new performance drugs. That’s how some useful things eventually get discovered. But research is not approval, and a promising animal study is not proof in humans. None of that really matters to many athletes because they usually rely on anecdotal evidence. They will do it anyway—if they think it works and can find a way of not getting caught. Which, for the most part, in my view of things, they do. But if your competitive career matters to you, you need to be very careful, as peptides create a very broad gray area. Make sure you check the actual status of the substance before you put it in your body—and remember, just because WADA has banned it doesn’t mean that there’s a test for it. January isn’t far away, and WADA is making one thing abundantly clear: They’re always a step behind. I guess it never occurs to them to switch hats and discover the new stuff before we do.
