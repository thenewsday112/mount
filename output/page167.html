What 500 doorsteps taught me about getting kids back to school

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

I know the feeling when a letter from school shows up at the house. Nobody opens one of those smiling. Most of the time, the letter tells a family something they already know and nothing about what they actually need.

To be fair, letters, emails, and automated calls have their place in returning absent students to school. For plenty of families, a reminder is all it takes. A parent sees the message, realizes how many days have added up, and gets their child back on the bus the next morning. Districts should keep using those tools. The trouble starts with the families who never respond, because silence gives a school almost nothing to work with.

Not long ago, my team completed more than 500 home visits in one of the country’s largest urban school districts. Many of those families hadn’t answered the letters or returned the calls. What we found on their front porches is something I believe every district leader should hear.

The first lesson was about who knocks. Our advocates come from the communities they serve. Many grew up a few blocks from the families they visit, and some speak the same home language. When one of them knocks, the parent on the other side of the door often sees a neighbor instead of an official.

More than once, a mom or a grandmother opened the door already braced for bad news. She expected to hear about a court date, fine, or some other penalty. Instead, our advocates asked how the family was doing, asked about the children, and listened carefully to the answers. You could see the relief on her face.

That first interaction shapes the whole conversation. People tell you the truth when they feel safe, and no recorded phone message or form letter will ever get you there.

Once families felt safe, they told us a lot. Every morning I read through the case notes from the day before, and over hundreds of visits the same stories kept coming up. Some kids missed school because a parent’s work shift changed and nobody was home to get them on the bus. Others stayed home to watch younger siblings while their parents worked. Some were bullied because of their ethnicity or sexuality, or just because other kids saw them as “different.” We met families navigating complicated legal or family situations, and many students were dealing with several of these issues at once.

None of those reasons shows up in an attendance report. A student with 15 absences looks the same in the data whether the cause is a bus schedule or a bully.

The visits also exposed problems with the outreach itself, and I say this with real respect for the people who manage student data in districts nationwide. Phone numbers on file were out of date. Letters went to old addresses. Messages went out in English to households where English wasn’t the first language. Some of these families never ignored the district at all. The message simply never reached them. None of this came from a lack of caring. These are the gaps you only find when someone goes out and looks.

This is where home visits pay off for district leaders. When I sit down with superintendents and attendance teams to walk through the results of our home visits, they see patterns a spreadsheet could never surface. One neighborhood needs transportation help. A particular grade level in one specific school is slipping. Communication breaks down before it ever reaches certain families. Leaders stop wondering why families aren’t responding and start working on the reasons behind it, and that information becomes the foundation of a real plan to bring students back.

How a district shows up matters as much as whether it shows up. I understand why so many attendance systems lean on compliance. Districts have rules to follow and reports to file. But warning letters and truancy notices often push families away. A parent who feels judged stops picking up the phone, and the families who most need support drift even further out of reach.

A home visit starts somewhere else entirely, with a simple question. How are you doing, and what would help your child get back to learning? When families feel respected, they open up. When they open up, we find answers built around their real lives. Sometimes the answer is a ride to school. Sometimes it’s a conversation with a teacher or a referral to a clinic.

The trust lasts well beyond the visit. Families who feel heard reach out sooner. They call the school after two missed days instead of 20. I’ve watched this happen again and again.

People sometimes ask me if chronic absenteeism feels too big to solve. I tell them no. After 500 doorsteps, I’m more hopeful than when we started.

The best advice I ever got was “Be the change you want to see.” Every advocate on my team lives those words. They knock on doors in the rain and the heat. They stand on front stoops and listen. They come back the next day.

Families want their kids in school. Schools want their students back. For the families a letter or a phone call can’t reach, the missing piece is usually someone willing to show up and ask the right question.

The 10 hottest kids’ Halloween costumes of 2026, according to Google

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Every October, parents become amateur anthropologists, scouring the internet for a Halloween costume that will satisfy one small, extremely opinionated client. (Or, more likely, researching a request for a character we’ve never heard of.) 

Now, Google has put all that frantic searching into one place. Its new site, Frightgeist, tracks which costumes are top ranked in search, and the kids’ top ten for 2026 is a gloriously incoherent collection. We’ve got a country legend we’re still mourning, a Thriller-era King of Pop, a web-cartoon jester, and, with no explanation offered, a dumpling.

There’s no tidy theme to sum it up, and I’ve stopped trying. Here’s what you need to know about this year’s top costumes, along with advice for putting a look together.

Top trending kids costumes according to Google

Dolly Parton

It just makes sense that our dearly departed Saint of Tennessee comes in at No 1. And since Dolly sported so many iconic looks across a long parade of eras, you have the opportunity to seem like you’re presenting your child with options even when there’s a solid chance this was your idea, not theirs. The only must is a blonde wig that defies gravity. A stuffed bra is optional but encouraged–Dolly would have wanted it that way.

Ella Langley

Makes sense she’s spiking, since she’s leading this year’s CMA nominations with nine nods and “Choosin’ Texas” made her the only woman to top the Hot 100, Hot Country Songs, and Country Airplay charts at once. That said, I’ll be honest. Unless said trick-or-treater is belting out “Choosin’ Texas,” this costume will be lost on me. But any excuse to pile on some rhinestones is a good one. (See above.)

Dumpling

The lone non-celebrity in the lineup and, I’d wager, the only costume on this list your kid actually chose for themselves. Chalk it up to the squishy dumpling toys, the brightly colored, bamboo-steamer-packaged squishies that have been dominating TikTok and every kid’s pocket this year. On the plus side, the costume is cozy, warm, and fully nap-compatible. No notes.

Shakira

Between her two World Cup performances last summer and the early-2000s revival in full swing, she was overdue. I recently combed the attic for my old low-rise jeans, the ones with the half-inch zipper, because my teenage daughter asked for them in earnest. The horror.

Ash Ketchum (from Pokémon)

Pokémon turns 30 this year, which makes it officially old enough to have trained a generation of parents before becoming their kids’ obsession. Give them a backwards red cap, tuck a Pikachu they already own under one arm, and you’ve got the cheapest, most comfortable costume on the list. Additionally, they’ll be recognizable to every other kid on the block which, for better or worse, is more than I can say for the country singers above.

Hannah Montana

The show hit its 20th anniversary this year, which means the early-aughts kids who wore out the DVDs are now the ones doing the costume shopping. And if you need another reason to love the pick, Miley is Dolly’s goddaughter, which makes number one and number six a true family affair.

Spider-Gwen

Worth noting that the lone superhero to crack this entire top ten is a woman who also drums in a punk rock band. That’s the kind of role model I can get behind. Spider-Man? Old news. And there’s no new movie to credit here, which means the kids are picking her on pure merit.

Michael Jackson (Thriller Era)

Specifically the Thriller era, so we’re talking red leather jacket and at least a loose attempt at the choreography. If your kid can pull off the zombie shoulder roll, they’ll absolutely crush this one. Credit the Antoine Fuqua biopic, “Michael,” that hit theaters last spring and put the moonwalk back in heavy rotation. 

Pomni (from The Amazing Digital Circus)

Admittedly, I had to look this one up. Turns out, she’s a jester trapped in a digital circus having a slow-motion existential crisis, which tells you this is for the tween and up set–not the toddlers. If your kid is asking for it, they’re too old for the cute stuff and whether you knew it or not, you’re officially in a new stage of parenting. The hat is non-negotiable, though.

Grogu

Baby Yoda by any other name, and still the single most efficient way to make a small child look even smaller and more adorable. The ears make it truly irresistible and though your stroller may not float, it can still be part of the costume. 

And there you have it. Ten costumes, roughly four decades, and one dumpling. Whoever your child opts into being this year, may the wig defy gravity and the candy be plentiful.

The Louisiana Gaming Control Board has unanimously granted DraftKings a conditional four-month license extension while it examines the company’s use of artificial intelligence in customer practices and delays a decision on full renewal. DraftKings can continue operating in the state under the interim arrangement.

The company’s existing Louisiana license expires November 10, 2026. The board has scheduled a renewal hearing for February 18, 2027, with a final decision expected afterward; the conditional extension is set to keep DraftKings authorized through February 19.

AI allegations and a shareholder review

The Louisiana Gaming Control Board’s review follows a New York Times report alleging that DraftKings used AI to direct promotions and bonus inducements toward customers considered more likely to bet more. The reported practice was referred to internally as “elasticity.” The allegations are part of a broader regulatory examination of AI in sportsbook marketing.

There is also a separate ownership issue. The Louisiana State Police Gaming Enforcement Division found information indicating that some DraftKings shareholders with stakes of 5% or more had not completed customary suitability reviews. Assistant Attorney General Houston told the board the investigation was incomplete and that regulators needed additional time to examine the allegations and shareholder question.

The board has not found evidence that DraftKings violated a licensing or moral obligation. Its decision leaves both matters under review while the company operates under conditional authorization.

DraftKings disputes the reported AI use

DraftKings President of Operations and cofounder Paul Liberman appeared before the board to address the concerns. He denied that the company used AI in the manner described in the reporting or used the technology to harm consumers. Liberman also said DraftKings keeps records showing whether customers are winning or losing.

Legal Sports Report reported that the conditional renewal includes deadlines for DraftKings to provide shareholder suitability materials and written responses about AI use, prediction-market activity, digital offerings to Louisiana residents, and other regulatory questions. The company must also review and certify its marketing materials, commercials and digital ads for responsible-gambling compliance.

The conditions require a senior company representative to handle communications with the board within seven days. DraftKings must submit several materials within 30 days and provide monthly compliance reports covering areas such as new digital products, promotions, responsible-gambling systems, customer complaints and unresolved compliance issues.

Board Chairman Chris Hebert described the conditional approval as a way for regulators to complete their oversight and give DraftKings an opportunity to respond. The board’s action is not a finding that the company engaged in wrongdoing.

February hearing will set the next decision point

DraftKings will remain authorized to operate while state investigators continue their work and the company responds to the board’s conditions. The February 18 hearing is the next scheduled step toward a full license decision, with the extension running through the following day.

Ten TV Series That Highlight the Best in Humanity: 2026

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Every year, we invite staff and regular contributors to nominate TV series for Greater Goodies, the awards we give to stories that exemplify or illustrate kindness, community, empathy, and more keys to well-being. Here’s what we came up with this year: The Boroughs, Death by Lightning, Envidiosa, The Hawk, Heated Rivalry, A Knight of the Seven Kingdoms, Lucky, Murderbot, Pluribus, and Shrinking.

The Aging-as-a-Virtue Award: The Boroughs (Netflix)

Name the last show you’ve seen that features a hero over the age of 60. Drawing a blank? It’s not your fault—the media’s modus operandi is to equate heroism with youthfulness. Netflix’s The Boroughs flips this stereotype on its head.

With a star-studded lead cast, all 70 or older, we follow Sam Cooper’s (Alfred Molina) experience as he reluctantly moves into a senior living community in New Mexico after the death of his wife. But a series of strange events suggests that this is no ordinary retirement community.

As Sam navigates grief, new friendships, and a new identity, he joins forces with his neighbors to solve and resolve the community mystery. Overcoming obstacles like age discrimination and self-doubts, the group peels back the layers of the strange occurrences. At the root of the mystery is a societal fear of aging.

The show highlights what researcher Margie Lachman calls “crystallized intelligence”: the culmination of life experience, memory, and know-how. In The Boroughs, that turns out to be just as heroic as super human strength or the ability to fly. — Shanna B. Tiayon

The Purpose Award: Death by Lightning (Netflix)

History has already told us how Death by Lightning ends: Charles Guiteau will assassinate President James Garfield in 1881. But why? That’s one of the questions explored by the series, and it finds motivation in a sense of purpose–how purpose forms and how people come to cross-purposes.

Garfield (Michael Shannon) is principled and drawn reluctantly into a presidential contest he entered to nominate someone else. Guiteau (Matthew Macfadyen), meanwhile, is disturbingly sympathetic: charming, articulate, and desperate to do something meaningful with his life. Despite failed ventures, including a stint at the Oneida free-love commune, he remains convinced greatness awaits him. Believing his campaigning secured Garfield’s victory, he expects a government position to confirm his worth.

As their stories converge, Garfield finds purpose in his presidency: fighting corruption while supporting education and equal opportunity. Guiteau pursues a reward he feels he deserves, an entitlement that leads to tragedy.

In these ways, Garfield and Guiteau offer sharply contrasting answers to what makes a life meaningful, and watching their stories unfold side by side, we see how differently the desire to matter can play out.

Who you really ought to be watching closely is Garfield’s Vice President, Chester A. Arthur (Nick Offerman). A comfortable beneficiary of the political machine, he becomes an unlikely source of hope as the story goes on. Offerman makes him funny, flawed, and increasingly affecting as the demands of office begin to collide with old loyalties.

In a political landscape that can leave us cynical–in both the 19th century and the 21st–the series makes room for something more hopeful: the possibility that even an unlikely person can rise to the occasion, if they know why they need to rise. – Aurelia Santos

The Empathy Award: Envidiosa (Netflix)

When almost-40-year-old Vicky (Griselda Siciliani) breaks up with her long-term boyfriend, she is driven to embark upon a journey of self-discovery.

At first, Vicky is just looking for a replacement for her boyfriend—but the problem is that her compulsive comparisons with other women sabotage attempts to connect with other people. (Indeed, the name of this Argentinian show, Envidiosa, means “envious.”) Vicky is self-absorbed and yet at the same time she doesn’t have a strong sense of her own self.

We see this vividly in her hilarious sessions with her therapist. When, for example, she goes on an envious rant about a friend’s milestone, she is forced to confront the quiet, uncomfortable realization that she never once asked how her friend actually felt. In short, Vicky is not a polished role model—and over the course of four seasons, we see her empathy grow through the painful process of unlearning self-centeredness.

Personally, I see Envidiosa as an opportunity to question how we show up for others’ joys and struggles, and I take it as an invitation to reflect: How can I empathize with the ups and downs of those around me? Envidiosa is a funny and at times embarrassingly relatable story about trying to become a more empathic human being and friend. — Criss Cuervo

The Zest-for-Life Award: The Hawk (Netflix)

In The Hawk, washed-up professional golfer Lonnie “The Hawk” Hawkins (Will Ferrell) has made a miracle comeback into the PGA tour—this time around, facing a tough competitor in his son, Lance (Jimmy Tatro).

It’s a screwball comedy, full of dirty jokes and a rapid-fire rapport between Lonnie and his not-quite-ex-wife, Stacy (Molly Shannon). A makeshift-caddy he “picked up at Walmart” (while chatting up the parking lot, from his camper van) adds to the fun, serving up an unlikely duo that seem happy just to be alive.

Even when he’s pretty down and out, Lonnie is looking on the bright side of things. Everything is done with gusto—he chats up the golf ball before each swing, he devours plates of Buffalo Wild Wings, he plays mini golf with his son and makes it a full-on competition.

“Son, never play golf angry,” he tells Lance. For Lonnie, life is all about golf—and golf is all about fun. Why spend all your time on something if you aren’t doing it for the joy of it?

I especially love Lonnie’s adoration for his estranged wife, a firecracker in her own right. In episode four, when the Hawk crashes his son’s engagement party–yes, you read that right–and then attempts a getaway with his caddie in the van, Stacy hurls the cake at them, along with a string of expletives.

It doesn’t even faze him. “You gotta love the passion!” Lonnie gushes. “That is one hot tamale!” — Hope Reese

The Bridging Differences Award: Heated Rivalry

Heated Rivalry follows stick-crossed lovers Shane and Ilya as they figure out how to be intimate within the highly competitive, homophobic environment of professional hockey.

At first, Shane and Ilya swap fluids more readily than emotions. But over the course of six episodes, the two learn to love across gaps in culture, language, and experience. In one pivotal scene, Shane invites Ilya to speak vulnerably about family dynamics in Ilya’s native Russian—despite not understanding a word of it.

That works because Shane does not focus attention on himself. Instead, he stays present on the phone, serving as a witness to Ilya’s visceral emotions. Their connection lies in felt care, in hearing the texture of another’s heartbreak. 

In similar fashion, Ilya grounds Shane in stressful encounters. He supports Shane through panic without wallowing in his lover’s fear and entices Shane to relish pleasures outside of straight standards. As a couple, they help each other explore complex parts of themselves. We see a similar process unfold in another couple in the show, as they learn to respect each other’s different needs.

People might tune into Heated Rivalry for locker room towel drops and HBO’s glossy cinematography. They should stay to see a couple learn to love each other’s differences. The show exemplifies how bridging differences, at its best, means supporting others to more fully encounter themselves, and how in the process our relationships can grow.

With plenty of additional homoerotic stick-and-ball games to pull on from the novels of Rachel Reid, the next season of Heated Rivalry is expected to air on HBO Max and Canada’s Crave in Spring 2027. — Luke Brown

The Integrity Award: A Knight of the Seven Kingdoms (HBO)

The monstrously successful HBO series Game of Thrones famously tossed out a lot of good-and-evil fantasy tropes to tell a morally complicated story of how power corrupts even the best people. A prequel called House of the Dragon shares that worldview.

That’s why a new prequel called A Knight of the Seven Kingdoms came as something of a surprise to fans. Unlike the other two series, this is not a story about a struggle for power. Instead, it focuses on a rather naive, not-too-bright “hedge knight” named Duncan (Peter Claffey) seeking to be hired by whatever lord will take him. Though Duncan is taller and stronger than most, he’s touchingly childlike as he tries to find his place in the merry old kingdoms of Westeros.

The first two episodes are funny and charming, as Duncan acquires a squire in the form of a little bald boy going by the name Egg (Dexter Sol Ansell), and fumblingly tries to enter a jousting tournament. And then Duncan finds himself on a collision course with the great and powerful of Westeros. When violence finally comes, there’s nothing casual about it. We feel the force of every blow, the tragedy of every death, and the erosion of Duncan’s innocence.

I’m giving A Knight of the Seven Kingdoms the award for integrity, but I can’t tell you why without spoiling the ending. Let’s just say that the series turns out to be the story of how one simple young man discovers what is most important to him—and finds the courage to stick to that path even in the face of isolation, temptations, and threats. — Jeremy Adam Smith

The Redemption Award: Lucky (Apple TV) 

How do you exit a life you were born into?  

That’s the question behind Lucky, starring Anya Taylor-Joy. Lucky was raised by her con-artist father and taught the “family business” from a young age—but when we meet her, she navigating internal conflict with the life she’s been told to live and the life she wants for herself.

Her desire to quit is complicated by her marriage to Cary, the son of a ruthless mob leader, who was also socialized into a life of crime. The couple plan one final con as their exit strategy, but it goes terribly wrong. Lucky sets out to resolve the botched plan, reconcile her marriage, and protect both her life and her father’s.

Her intelligence is put to the test as she puts the pieces together, connects the dots, and outsmarts her rivals. In the process she discovers her own virtue—and that the intelligence which made her a successful con artist can also set her free.

Through the course of the series, she realizes that she does not have to pay for the sins of her father. Having never been given the opportunity for self-determination, she makes peace in the best way she knows how and chooses a new path. — Shanna B. Tiayon

The Belonging Award: Murderbot (Apple TV)

The Murderbot Diaries are a series of science-fiction novellas by Martha Wells, about a genderless, robotic soldier who manages to achieve free will. Since discovery will result in deactivation and since it doesn’t like humans very much anyway, Murderbot conceals its freedom of mind and consumes a lot of human-made entertainment inside its own head, even as it externally follows every order given to it by the evil corporation that owns it. Murderbot even has a favorite TV show: the Star-Trek-like Rise and Fall of Sanctuary Moon.

As a result of this isolation, the novellas are built almost entirely around its memorable inner monologues, with a voice that is by turns snarky, vulnerable, cold-blooded, disgusted, and puzzled. One of the things that makes these books distinctive is that Murderbot is not Pinocchio—it has no wish to become a “real boy.” It doesn’t want a gender, or the same feelings and drives as people. It just wants to be itself—and to have that difference respected.

While the Apple TV show based on the books is not quite that original or interesting, Murderbot retains its focus on finding the balance between belonging and difference. When we meet Murderbot (Alexander Skarsgård), he’s been hired to protect a group of polyamorous hippie scientists who hail from a utopian colony that exists independently of the corporations that rule the rest of the galaxy.

They are Murderbot’s first encounter with humans not shaped by dehumanizing hyper-capitalistic values, and they observe Murderbot in a way the corporate humans cannot. Over the course of the first season, they begin to realize that there is more to Murderbot than meets the eye, and they learn to see and appreciate Murderbot on its own terms. But what’s crucial is not that they perceive the humanity in Murderbot—it’s that Murderbot sees the humanity (and the goodness) in them.

In time, it becomes an accepted part of their society—without becoming more like them. Even so, the first season concludes on a tough-minded, melancholy note, as Murderbot itself can’t quite feel like it belongs. Will it someday find a place among these crunchy idealists? Stay tuned: The second season is coming in 2027. — Jeremy Adam Smith

The Difficult-Human-Appreciation Award: Pluribus (Apple TV)

Have you ever been among people but you didn’t feel part of the crowd? Like they were in on a joke that you just couldn’t get?

Welcome to Carol Sturka’s world. The science-fiction premise of Pluribus is that an alien virus transforms humanity into one big happy hive mind—everyone except Carol and 13 other people who seem to have immunity.

Even among those remaining individuals, though, Carol stands out as a malcontent. We sense that she’s been a fish out of water for her whole life.

Indeed, not every viewer will find Carol relatable or likable. When we first meet her, she’s at a bookstore event promoting her highly successful romantasy series. With her fans, she’s charming, funny, and kind. When she gets into a limo after a big sendoff by readers, the drivers asks if she’s famous.

“That depends,” Carol replies. “Are you a big fan of mindless crap?”

In short, Carol hates herself, her work, and her readers. And as we discover, her personality is snarky, uptight, stubborn, and uncompromising. When the virus hits, it’s an apocalyptic event, but Carol doesn’t change one bit—and her traits become both a blessing and a curse. The whole world wants to be kind to her, but Carol can’t accept kindness. That drives her to fight back. And that’s what ultimately makes her the hero of the story.

In the end, Pluribus is a very Greater Good thought experiment. It takes prosociality to its limits and finds that a total togetherness, empathy, and cooperation to the exclusion of selfishness, crankiness, and boundaries is a self-destructive dystopia, not a utopia. The show seems to argue that humanity needs difficult people like Carol to survive, and so invites us to appreciate what the Carols of the world have to offer. — Jeremy Adam Smith

The Deep Listening Award: Shrinking (AppleTV)

In Shrinking, talking and listening are the name of the game. Jimmy (Jason Segel) is a widowed psychotherapist who helps patients navigate anxiety, OCD, PTSD, and other ailments as he himself is floundering.

But he’s not alone. His teenage daughter, overbearing-but-lovable neighbors, and coworker-friends (with Harrison Ford as the curmudgeonly head of the practice) are all there to help him when his life goes off the rails after his wife was killed by a drunk driver.

In its three-season run, the series is heavy-handed with its use of therapy-speak among the whole gang, whether between therapist-to-patient, therapist-to-daughter, or therapist-to-therapist. Characters “feel seen.” More times than I can count, they tell each other “I hear you.” We witness, in detail, exchanges inside the therapy office—and outside of it, in a rogue practice known as “Jimmying,” where therapy takes place at such venues as, for instance, the boxing ring.

In other words, there’s a lot of “deep listening”: the practice of being present, open, and empathic with another person, in a way that goes beyond just hearing and understanding words. In his grief, Jimmy himself is not always a perfect deep listener. He tends to talk more than a typical therapist, and frequently injects himself into the discussion. Shrinking isn’t about perfection—it’s about struggling to connect and growing in the process. – Hope Reese

Across my conversations with leaders in government, critical infrastructure and regulated industries, one priority consistently rises to the top: putting technology to work on their most important systems while maintaining the control those systems require.

This challenge is becoming more important as advanced cloud and AI capabilities move into public services, regulated processes and essential operations. Organizations are considering more than where data resides. They also seek to understand who can access and operate their systems, how sensitive digital assets remain protected, which technology dependencies matter most and whether critical services can continue as regulatory, geopolitical, security or network conditions change. Digital sovereignty should give organizations greater control and security over the technology they depend on, while continuing to innovate.

Last year, we introduced Microsoft Sovereign Cloud to give customers more choice in how they address these requirements. Since then, we have continued to turn our commitments into practical capabilities across public cloud and customer-operated environments, while expanding how we enable partners to address sovereignty requirements in their local markets. Today, we are sharing the next stage of that work.

A continuum of control, built around the workload
Microsoft Sovereign Cloud diagram showing a continuum of sovereignty solutions across three areas: Sovereign Public Cloud for control, data protection and regional requirements; Sovereign Private Cloud for critical workloads across connected and disconnected environments; and the Sovereign Partner Ecosystem for trusted, locally operated and specialized solutions.

Our approach to digital sovereignty starts with the workload. Each workload carries different requirements for data, operations, technology dependencies, continuity and risk. Microsoft Sovereign Cloud is designed to make it easier for organizations to apply the appropriate level of control to each workload without forcing every application into the same deployment model.

Those needs can vary widely across organizations. A public-facing digital service may benefit from the scale and pace of innovation in the public cloud. A system supporting essential operations may need to continue running locally if connectivity is disrupted. An AI workload handling highly sensitive data may require models, data and processing to remain within a customer-controlled environment.

Microsoft Sovereign Cloud is designed around this workload-based approach, providing a continuum of management, control and options across Sovereign Public Cloud, Sovereign Private Cloud and the Sovereign Partner Ecosystem. Organizations can apply the level of control appropriate to each workload while maintaining a consistent approach to governance and operations across their digital estate. Sovereign Public Cloud adds capabilities for workloads that require greater control and transparency while continuing to benefit from the scale and innovation of Azure.

Sovereign Private Cloud extends cloud capabilities into customer-operated environments, including connected and disconnected operations. The Sovereign Partner Ecosystem broadens the continuum through independently operated environments, specialized partners, locally relevant expertise and technologies that support customers’ sovereignty requirements.

These are not isolated destinations. Customers can combine public cloud, private cloud and partner capabilities across their digital estate, applying different levels of control to different workloads as their operating needs evolve.

Making control more visible in Sovereign Public Cloud
For many workloads, the public cloud provides a combination of scale, innovation, integrated security and compliance. Organizations can benefit from these capabilities while applying additional sovereignty controls for residency, confidentiality, operator access, policy and continuity. Our work in Sovereign Public Cloud makes these controls easier to understand, apply and demonstrate.

Sovereign Control Panel, entering public preview at Microsoft Ignite, brings these signals together in a unified Azure portal experience. It helps organizations understand their sovereignty posture, apply and manage controls, identify gaps and risks and take action to address them. It also supports business continuity readiness by helping customers understand workload dependencies and assess portability across operating environments when required.

This visibility complements controls across the operator-access lifecycle. Just-in-time Access and Customer Lockbox give customers additional control over access to their environments. For customers in the European Union (EU) and the European Free Trade Association (EFTA), we are rolling out Data Guardian, which adds European-resident oversight by a trusted EU partner for qualifying Microsoft engineer remote access to in-scope production systems. Production Touch Ledger, also launched with phased rollout in the EU and EFTA, creates tamper-evident records of that access and the actions performed.

Azure Key Vault Managed Hardware Security Module (HSM) already gives customers dedicated, single-tenant protection and control over the encryption keys used by supported Azure services. Last year, we committed to extending that control beyond Microsoft infrastructure. External key management with Azure Managed HSM is now generally available, allowing customers to keep the root encryption key in hardware they own and operate on-premises or through a trusted third party. This gives organizations another option for workloads that require physical control of key hardware outside Microsoft infrastructure.

Azure confidential computing helps protect supported data while it is processed in a hardware-based and attested trusted execution environment. We are also increasing transparency into the technologies that underpin that trust. Azure Confidential Clean Rooms, now in limited preview, applies confidential computing to approved multiparty collaboration, allowing participants to analyze shared sensitive data while limiting broader exposure of the underlying information.

Together, these capabilities provide additional technical and operational controls that can support a broader digital sovereignty strategy aligned to an organization’s legal, policy, security, resilience and business requirements.

Designing for resilience and operational independence
For some workloads, digital sovereignty also means maintaining critical operations as connectivity, geopolitical and operating conditions change. Resilience must be designed from the start so essential workloads can continue operating through disruption.

To help customers prepare for these scenarios, we are publishing new sovereign business continuity guidance and architecture patterns. These resources help organizations translate resilience requirements into architectures they can plan, implement and test before they are needed.

For many workloads, the scale and geographic reach of the public cloud provide a strong foundation for resilience across locations and operating conditions. For workloads that require greater operational independence, Sovereign Private Cloud extends that continuum into customer-operated environments. It supports connected and disconnected operating models. The ability to transition workloads from connected to disconnected operations is entering public preview, giving organizations greater flexibility to sustain critical services as connectivity and operating requirements change.

Bringing more of the application lifecycle under local control
Operational independence requires more than infrastructure. Increasingly, organizations need data, development tools, model capabilities and productivity services their applications depend on to operate within the same customer-controlled environment.

Sovereign Private Cloud, built on Azure Local, extends Microsoft cloud capabilities into environments that customers operate and control. SQL Server on Azure Local is already generally available for connected and disconnected deployments. DocumentDB and Blob Storage on Azure Local are both entering public preview, giving organizations more choice in how they run and manage critical data within customer-controlled environments.

GitHub Enterprise Local will become generally available at Microsoft Ignite as a validated GitHub Enterprise Server appliance on Azure Local, helping organizations keep repositories, code and development artifacts under local control. Developers can also use GitHub Copilot with locally hosted models for inference, bringing AI-assisted development closer to their code and data within the environments they control.

The local application environment is expanding with Foundry Local. At Ignite, Foundry Local will become generally available for connected and disconnected operations on Azure Local, with an expanded model catalog that includes Microsoft, partner, open-weight and open-source models. Local inference and agentic retrieval will also enable users to interact with local enterprise data using natural language and receive responses grounded in that data. Integration with Microsoft 365 Local, available in preview for approved customers, extends this functionality to locally operated Microsoft SharePoint and Exchange servers.

Confidential virtual machines on Azure Local are generally available, extending confidential computing capabilities to customer-controlled infrastructure. Hardware-encrypted memory and hardware-based isolation help protect sensitive data while it is being processed. Workload attestation adds assurance before sensitive data or keys are released. For regulated, sensitive and mission-critical workloads, these safeguards provide another layer of protection while giving organizations greater control over where their data and applications run.

Azure Local will support multitenancy, allowing organizations to separate resources, governance and management across multiple tenants while sharing the underlying infrastructure. This significantly expands what customers with distinct business units or security boundaries can achieve with Sovereign Private Cloud. Multitenancy also creates new opportunities for local operators and service providers to deliver differentiated sovereign cloud services on Azure Local infrastructure.

These additions bring more of the application lifecycle under local control, spanning data, development, productivity, AI and operations. Organizations can determine what runs locally based on the requirements of each workload.

AI is amplifying both the opportunity and the impact of technology choices. As AI systems and agents interact with sensitive data, business processes and essential infrastructure, they also expand the security considerations organizations need to manage. Organizations need clear control over where these systems run, who can operate them, what actions they can take and how critical services continue when conditions change.

Microsoft Sovereign Cloud brings these choices together as a continuum of control across the public cloud, customer-operated environments and our partner ecosystem. We will continue to expand this continuum so organizations can innovate with cloud and AI while maintaining the control, confidentiality, resilience and operational authority their most important workloads require.

Introducing Amazon ECS Gateway: A fully-managed ingress solution for ECS services

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

When platform teams manage containerized applications at scale on Amazon Elastic Container Service (Amazon ECS), configuring ingress networking for each service often becomes a bottleneck. For every new service, teams must provision and connect Application Load Balancers (ALBs), AWS Certificate Manager (ACM) certificates, AWS WAF web ACLs, and security groups, then keep those resources in sync as services scale, update, or operate. This per-service overhead slows down deployment velocity and increases the risk of configuration drift across an organization.

Today, we’re announcing Amazon ECS Gateway, a new account-level resource that simplifies ingress networking for containerized workloads by dynamically provisioning and managing the necessary infrastructure on your behalf, with all resources created directly in your account.

ECS Gateway introduces a clear separation of responsibilities between platform teams and application teams.

  • Platform teams create and configure a gateway, defining organizational networking standards including subnets, security groups, TLS certificates, listener settings, and WAF web ACLs, in a single place. They then associate the gateway with one or more ECS clusters, making it available to services running on those clusters.
  • Application teams opt their services in by adding a gateway connector parameter when creating or updating a service. ECS provisions the required per-service resources automatically: an ACM certificate, a unique DNS hostname, listener rules, target groups, and a service security group that permits inbound traffic only from the ALB. No networking expertise required.

ECS Gateway offers host-based routing using ALBs and enables up to 25 services to share a single ALB, optimizing infrastructure costs. ECS manages the full lifecycle of the underlying infrastructure, provisioning the first ALB when your first service connects, adding more ALBs as capacity is reached, and removing them when no longer needed. Each service receives a unique DNS hostname, giving your teams a ready-to-use endpoint the moment provisioning completes.

A first look at ECS Gateway
In the ECS console, platform team can create a gateway and sets its configuration. The infrastructure role is an IAM role that ECS assumes to provision and manage networking resources such as ALBs, security groups, and certificates in your account. To create a gateway, provide a gateway name and select an infrastructure role. You can optionally associate the gateway with ECS clusters during creation or later.

Choose a load balancing scheme, either public or VPC-internal traffic routing. Under Additional Settings, you can also set up a listener, port, maximum count of load balancers the gateway can scale to, and a web ACL lets you protect your web applications. Some settings, including the name, scheme, VPC, subnets, IP address type, listener protocol and port cannot be changed after gateway creation.

Using AWS Command Line Interface (AWS CLI), call CreateGateway with a name (unique per account), an infrastructure role, and a configuration object. No infrastructure is provisioned until a service connects with a gateway connector.

aws ecs create-gateway \ --name my-team-gateway \ --infrastructure-role-arn arn:aws:iam::123456789012:role/ECSGatewayRole \ --configuration '{ "HostSharedAlb": { "networkConfiguration": { "subnets": [ "subnet-080779f9bd887757a", "subnet-03d5b2fead6bc6258", "subnet-0861210a397d31252", "subnet-06539e194023cb77a" ], "ipAddressType": "ipv4" }, "scheme": "internet-facing" } }'

Once the gateway is created, you can take a look at the detailed configuration, gateway routes, load balancers, and associated clusters.

Now associate the gateway with one or more clusters (many-to-many), to make it available to ECS services on those clusters.

Use AssociateGatewayWithCluster to make the gateway available to services on a cluster:

aws ecs associate-gateway-with-cluster \ --gateway my-team-gateway \ --cluster arn:aws:ecs:us-east-1:123456789012:cluster/my-cluster

To create and manage the ECS Gateway programmatically, including calling APIs and searching documentation, try the AWS MCP Server and plugins with your preferred AI coding tool. To learn more, visit the Amazon ECS Gateway documentation.

ECS Gateway:Behind the scenes
A gateway can be associated with up to 10,000 clusters, and a single cluster supports up to five gateway associations. This many-to-many relationship gives platform teams the flexibility to share a gateway across environments or assign multiple gateways (for example, public and private) to a single cluster.

Platform teams can control access by choosing which gateways to associate with which clusters. For additional control, use IAM policies to restrict who can deploy services to specific clusters.

Application teams add the gateway connector to each service definition in CreateService or UpdateService. If you have an existing ECS service with its own load balancer, you can adopt ECS Gateway incrementally by calling UpdateService to add a gateway connector. Both ingress paths remain available, allowing you to validate the gateway connection before removing the existing load balancer configuration.

aws ecs create-service \ --cluster my-cluster \ --service my-api-service \ --task-definition my-api-service:1 \ --gateway-connectors '[{ "portName": "api", "filters": {"accessTypes": ["PUBLIC"]}, "healthCheck": {...} }]'

At deployment time, Amazon ECS evaluates the gateways associated with the cluster and connects the service to every compatible gateway. A gateway is compatible when its access type matches the service’s connector filter, it is in the same VPC as the service, and it covers all Availability Zones used by the service’s configured subnets.

For each connection, Amazon ECS assigns the service to an ALB with available capacity and provisions the per-service resources: an ACM certificate, a unique DNS hostname, a listener rule routing traffic for that hostname to the service target group, and a service security group allowing inbound traffic only from the ALB security group. If no existing ALB has capacity, ECS creates a new one (up to maxAlbCount referring to quota limit). Once provisioning completes, the gateway route becomes ACTIVE, and the service URL is ready to receive traffic.

ECS Gateway handles common operational scenarios automatically:

  • Redeployments: On each deployment, ECS re-evaluates gateway associations. If new compatible gateways have been associated since the last deployment, your service connects to them. If a gateway has been disassociated, your service disconnects and the route is removed. Your service always reflects the current state of the cluster gateways at deployment time.
  • Service deletion: ECS automatically disconnects the service from every gateway, removes all gateway routes, and cleans up all per-service resources. If the service held the last route on an ALB, that ALB is deprovisioned.

Things to Know
Here are a few important details to keep in mind:

  • Networking: ECS Gateway is available for ECS services using the awsvpc network mode across all launch types (Amazon ECS EC2, AWS Fargate, and ECS Managed Instances). Your service Availability Zones (AZs) must be a subset of each gateway’s AZs on the cluster. A gateway ALB only routes traffic to tasks in AZs where it has subnets. We recommend creating gateways and services for a given cluster with the same set of AZs.
  • Capacity: If maxAlbCount is reached and no ALB has available capacity, the deployment fails. Default quota limit of maxAlbCount is 10. Plan your maxAlbCount setting according to the number of services you expect to connect.
  • Observability: Use ALB metrics in CloudWatch with the TargetGroup dimension to monitor per-service traffic, and LoadBalancer dimension to monitor traffic across the shared ALB. Use describe-target-health to check real-time target status. Configure CloudWatch alarms for metrics such as HealthyHostCount, TargetResponseTime, and HTTPCode_Target_5XX_Count, using gateway ARN-based tagging so monitoring scales automatically with your maxAlbCount.

Now available
Amazon ECS Gateway is available now in all commercial AWS Regions where ECS is available at no additional charge. You pay only for the underlying infrastructure provisioned in your account when services connect to a gateway, including applicable ALB charges, CloudWatch logs and metrics, ACM certificates, and data transfer charges. To learn more, visit the Amazon ECS Pricing page.

To get started, create your first gateway today in the ECS console, and send feedback to AWS re:Post for ECS or through your usual AWS Support contacts.

The ‘Tavern-Style’ Pizza Takeover

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →
The thin-crust pizzas popular throughout the Midwest have taken off as the “tavern-style” pizza trend | Brad Bobrow/La Merna

In the suburbs of Chicago where they both grew up, it was just pizza, according to Brittany and Brennan Grosvenor, the couple behind Proof in Portland, Oregon. Ordering came down to the question of “thin-crust, deep-dish, or double-dough,” Brittany recalls of her family’s Friday night tradition. The Grosvernors sell the former at Proof, though the language of “thin crust” has given way to the more nationally familiar phrasing of “tavern-style,” the pizza trend that has swept from coast to coast in recent years. 

Outsiders might associate Chicago with its famous, tourist-beckoning deep-dish pizza, but Brittany says that’s just not reflective of reality, citing deep dish as a much less frequent treat than the Midwestern, thin-crust style that’s gaining popularity. “The pizza you get every week, or if you go to a kid’s birthday party, it’s always tavern-style,” she says.

“Tavern-style” pizza — which is prevalent  across the Midwest, with Chicago, Milwaukee, Minnesota, and St. Louis touting similar pies — is known for its thin, cracker-like crust, cut into a lattice instead of triangular slices. Depending on the region and the pizza shop where one’s nostalgia lies, the sauce might lean sweet or spicy. Lightness and crispness, though, is the style’s unique sell. Writing for Esquire in 2023, Omar Mamoon called the variant, which was then still on the rise, “a foil” to famously thick Detroit-style pizza, which had its big moment in the late 2010s. 

The beauty of the tavern-style pie is its shareability. A pizza yields more slices when cut into a grid than into triangles, explains Bradley Shorten, who’s behind both Kim’s Uncle Pizza in his hometown Chicago suburb of Westmont, Illinois, and the recently opened La Merna in Washington, D.C. Pizza cut into little squares makes for great drinking food, especially since it fits on a cocktail napkin. What makes a good pie, according to Shorten, is that every square has a little of every ingredient; the toppings go to the edge. And while the four, small corner slices might not be the “best” ones, Shorten says, they’re the ones “everybody goes for,” especially if they’re from Chicago.

Over the past few years, as “taverns” have become growing fixations for a cadre of restaurateurs, the “party cut” pizza and tavern-style pizza spots have also become ubiquitous, with openings like Keepers in New York city; One Trick Pony in Forth Worth, Texas; Tillie’s Tavern Pizza in Wheat Ridge, Colorado; and Zarella and Dimmi Dimmi Corner Italian in Chicago, where the style has always been abundant. For some Chicagoans, these openings across the country are an opportunity to correct the record about their city’s pizza culture, and for other Midwesterners to claim their stake in the region’s pizza, too.

When Mike Fadem opened the second location of his popular NYC pizzeria Ops — known for its wood-fired Neapolitan-esque pies with leopard-spotted crusts and pliant dough — he wanted to offer something new. Fadem is from St. Louis, where the city’s namesake pizza is super-thin. Despite his love for it, “it’s maybe not the best version of tavern pizza,” Fadem admits, noting the iteration’s many critics, some of whom take issue with its use of the gooey processed cheese called provel. The trajectory of tavern-style pizza has been like a game of telephone, he explains: St. Louis pizza is derived from thin-crust Chicago pizza, which itself came from Neapolitan pizza. Divergence has accompanied each jump.

One of Fadem’s employees at Ops, meanwhile, is from New Jersey, which claims its own thin-crust, tavern- or bar-style pizza tradition. (The bar pies of the Boston area also have their own devoted following.) Accordingly, “I had this interest in ‘can we make an Ops version of this?’” Fadem says. With Ops known for sourdough, Fadem also wanted to highlight the rich flavor of whole grains. And he wanted it to be really crispy, a quality he says draws in New Yorkers. Fadem’s take is on the menu as the regionally agnostic “tavernetta.” Though St. Louis and New Jersey served as initial inspiration, the best pizza from his research was actually in Milwaukee. The Tavernetta hasn’t displaced the other pies at the restaurant; it’s ordered in addition to them, with most groups getting at least one.

In June, Los Angeles cocktail spot Bar Next Door celebrated its third anniversary by adding food to its menu. Previously, the bar had allowed customers to bring in pies from Prince Street Pizza next door since Prince Street co-owner Lawrence Longo is a partner in it. “But, you know, after having a slice, sometimes you get a little full and you’re like, I want to go to bed,” says owner and bar director Brynn Smith. “That’s the whole goal for us: for people to stay a little longer, drink more. We thought the tavern-style pizza would be perfect.” The fact that the pizzas — they’re technically listed on the menu as “bar pies,” to better go with the establishment’s name — are made exclusively for the bar by Prince Street just adds another reason for people to go out, Smith says.

The idea of the tavern as a social and convivial space is inseparable from the tavern-style pizza. Shorten’s goal with La Merna — which he runs with his wife Meg Schmitt, who grew up in St. Louis — was the feel of a Chicago dive bar or a Wisconsin supper club: a place with wood-paneled walls and checkered floors, where people could slide into a red leather booth for pitchers of beer and pizza and hang out for a while. As my colleague Francky Knapp wrote last year, the rising appeal of the “tavern” vibe across restaurants nationwide is rooted in the sense of providing somewhere homey and lived-in. At taverns, diners are beckoned to sprawl out and stay awhile; to free themselves of pretense and to tap into something welcoming in its familiarity. This, for so many people, is exemplified by the archetype of hometown pizza shop, which is forever tethered in our cultural imaginations to the trappings of a previous, simpler era.

It’s for this reason that, in Oregon, the Grosvenors have started serving their take on Bosco sticks, the stuffed breadsticks of Midwestern cafeterias, and Italian beef with giardiniera. While they could find certain Midwestern hits around Portland, it sometimes frustrated them that they couldn’t find everything in one place. According to Brennan Grosvenor, “We’re just trying to embody the feeling: If you went into a tavern in Chicago, what could you order there?”
