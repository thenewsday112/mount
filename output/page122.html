Twin Travelers

In 1977, NASA launched twin spacecraft Voyager 1 and 2, taking advantage of a once-every-175-year planetary alignment of the four largest planets in our solar system: Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune. Using six gravity assists between them, this Grand Tour gave us up-close views of the gas and ice giants, including several moons. By the 1980s, the two probes went their separate ways at Saturn. As Voyager 2 continued toward Uranus and Neptune, Voyager 1 grabbed one more portrait of the solar system (excluding Mercury and Mars), including a final image of the pale blue dot we call home.

Every good road trip needs some music to match. In this case, each craft carries the Golden Record, a 12-inch phonograph record with spoken greetings in 55 languages, 27 musical tracks, 116 images, and other sounds from Earth. Scientists confirm fruit and vegetable skin really is good for you

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →
Fruit and vegetable skin is often rich in fibre Ekaterina Vasileva-Bagler/Getty Images

Some people are convinced that fruit and vegetable skin is particularly nutritious, and now scientists have confirmed the idea. They have discovered that these parts of fruits and vegetables often contain far more fibre than the flesh we normally consume.

The finding suggests cooks are unwittingly discarding fibre-rich food by chucking out the seeds, skins and stems of fruits and vegetables when preparing a meal.

Home cooks and caterers should adopt “root to tip” eating and start cooking with squash peel, watermelon rind and pepper stalks to improve the public’s intake of dietary fibre, the team says.

“We’ve really got to shift our purchasing habits to buy whole fruits and vegetables, and then use all of the fruits and vegetables in dishes,” says Rachel Gibson at King’s College London.

Gibson and her colleagues tested the fibre content of 12 fruits and vegetables, including butternut squash, kiwi fruit, watermelon, broccoli and bell pepper. The team measured the fibre content both in the parts typically consumed and the parts that usually go to waste.

They found that, in 10 of the 12 produce types, the normally wasted components contain more fibre than the commonly consumed parts. Butternut squash seeds and skin, for example, contained 9.25 grams of fibre per 100 grams, compared with 2 g of fibre per 100 g of squash flesh. Meanwhile, the skin and core of a pineapple core contains 450 per cent more fibre than the flesh. These skin and core components are often tough to chew, but the researchers say they can be blended, grated or roasted to make them more palatable.

Just 4 per cent of British adults meet the recommended minimum daily intake of 30 g of dietary fibre, with the average adult consuming around half that per day.

Gibson suggests caterers could use the findings to design fibre-rich meals using more produce that would otherwise go to waste. This could reduce food waste and cut costs for commercial kitchens, as well as increasing dietary fibre intake, she says.

King’s College London’s canteen piloted a “root to tip” menu in May 2025. Meals included a West African peanut stew made with all of a butternut squash, kale stalks and leek tops, and a watermelon salad featuring pickled watermelon rind.

Simple menu tweak can nudge people into choosing climate-friendly food

Rejigging the meal choices on offer in a canteen can prompt people to make healthier, climate-friendlier decisions

Eating a larger proportion of harvested fruit and vegetables will also improve the resilience of the food system in the face of climate shocks, says Gibson. “Everything we pull out of the ground that we can eat, we should be eating.”

Christian Reynolds at City St George’s University of London says the research is an “exciting investigation”. But he points out that the most carbon-intensive food waste is meat and dairy products. Reducing fruit and vegetable waste is therefore “part of the solution, but it’s not the only thing we need to do”, he says.

Remembering Mark Pryor, beloved crime novelist and iconic Austinite

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

On October 8th, we lost Mark Pryor, a great craftsman of entertaining mysteries and thrillers, and an active and much loved member of the crime fiction community. He conceived unique heroes and anti-heroes with vivid settings. Not only was he a writer we admired, he was one we called a good friend.

His story was cool before he was even born. His father travelled on motorcycle across America on holiday from Britain where fell in love and brought the American lady home to marry. Mark and born and grew up in England with a love of soccer, where he studied journalism, then law. He used his dual citizenship to make a home in Austin, Texas where he practiced law as a prosecutor, mainly in Juvenile Court, and later as a defense attorney. He most valued his time with juvenile law, feeling he had leeway to hopefully get the perpetrator’s life back on track as well as get justice. However, what he valued most was his wife Sarah and three children, Blake, Henry, and Nicola.

His first novel, The Bookseller, he introduced us to Hugo Marston, the character he wrote about the most. Hugo, a former FBI profiler from Texas, worked as the chief of security for our embassy in Paris. When not indulging in his love of collecting and reading books, he finds himself dealing in mysteries often involving international intrigue. He is often assisted by his wild card of a CIA-connected buddy, Tom Green, his sometime girlfriend, French journalist Claudia Roux, and Parisian cop Lt. Camille Laurens, one of the first reoccurring trans characters in a mystery series to be written with dignity.

“He didn’t give readers a superficial tourist’s version of France,” explains James Ziskin, a friend who started out with him, roughly the same time when they were label mates at Seventh Street. “He wrote details that only an habitué would think to include. He knew the locations, the history, the language, and day-to-day minutiae that make a foreign setting ring true. Second, he was fearless in drawing his characters, for example Camille Laurens, a transgender police lieutenant who appears in several of the Hugo Marston books. It would have been much safer to write a policewoman or policeman. Then there’s his knowledge of the law, his sense of justice, and the expert plotting that runs through all his books. He was a gifted wordsmith, too.”

“His imagery was always so vivid,” adds friend and fellow writer, Puja Guha. “He could set the scene of where things were in a case, or how something happened along the Seine, or anywhere else, in a way that just made you see it in your head so clearly.”

Mark presented Hugo with just a few touches on his background, then revealing a little more with each book. However, he proved to be fully formed as Puja explains. “Hugo’s a great hero. Strong, capable, but also vulnerable and emotional. A really well-rounded character. I always just wished he could find real love in the series!”

His next protagonist, Dominic,  proved to a complete opposite. Like Mark he worked as a juvenile prosecutor in Austin, but he was also a musician, as well as a dangerous sociopath. The books are a bitter, but oh so sweet, a dark chocolate mix of Richard Stark, Jim Thompson, and Patricia Highsmith. (For the record, Mark had only read Highsmith before he wrote the first book, Hollow Man.) When the two of us had dinner one night with noir queens Megan Abbott and Alison Gaylin, he listened intently while we talked about the genre. He later told me he was taking notes that evening for the book. When I asked if he ever thought of doing a book where he pitted Hugo and Dominic in a book, he told me if he was being true to the characters, Hugo could probably survive two chapters.

His last series lead, Henri Lefort, turned out to be his most complex. Introduced in Die Around Sundown, Henri, a homicide detective with a past, works the streets of recently occupied Paris. With small historical details and rich characterizations, he depicts the emotions and humiliation of living in a conquered society. He does have fun putting him in the same apartment Hugo will reside in in the future.

Recently, he returned to Hugo with The Most Mysterious Bookshop in Paris. Hugo retires from the embassy to open up his own bookshop. It opens with a beautiful passage with Hugo taking a morning stroll to the store, taking in the poetic glory of the city that reflects mark’s love for it. Of course, his old skills and reputation pull him in when he is asked to help a chocolate maker faced with blackmail. He delves into the making of the confections with fascinating detail. Many of the supporting cast returns. Pryor finished a second book, A Vintage Murder In Paris, that will be published in spring.

Mark was one of the most sociable writers I ever worked with. He often asked to tag along for dinners I’d have with authors who came into town for book events. One of these dinners led to a strong friendship he had with Phillip Kerr. He was a mainstay at Bouchercon, beloved by both authors and fans, all drawn to his charm and dry wit. To be around the god natured ribbing between James and Mark of who was the better author was hilarious. “I loved sparring with Mark. Of course, Mark landed more blows than he received, I can tell you that. He could slice you to pieces with his wit, and make you laugh at yourself anyway. We used to insult each other publicly whenever we got the chance, but it was all in good fun.“

Fellow Austin author, Mike McCrary described his personality in a nutshell. “Mark was the person you wanted to sit next to at that boring dinner. The one you hoped would be at the thing you didn’t want go to because he’d make it fun. He always made me laugh and rarely caught him without a smile.”

“He had the most mischievous smile,” says Ziskin.  “That English accent. His generosity. His willingness to protect victims and champion the underdog. And, of course, his sense of humor. Everyone liked Mark.”

Mike recalls his mischievous one evening. “Noir at the Bar in Austin. A great example of his wit and his never-ending push to play with the unexpected. He went up on stage before me and he knew I was next. As he wrapped up he turned to the audience and said, “Be nice to Mike. He’s not used to crowds this big.”

One of my favorite memories of Mark is when I hung out with him at a Bouchercon opening party. He had a little too much to drink that evening and was feeling pretty loose. When he saw me the next morning he said with all sincerity and Cary Grant charm, “You were with me most of the night, who do I need to apologize to?”

“I feel like we became close friends after our first couple of conversations,” recalls Puja. “He always made sure no one felt left out and brought people into the discussion.”

He encouraged his fellow authors, reminding us that we were all in this together and we should take pride in what we can do in crime fiction and if you were friend, you knew you had someone who always had your back. He often helped out people who had legal questions about there book. He used Mr. Ziskin’s skills to do both a map and fictional family tree in two of his Hugo books. “Mark mentioned that he liked a map that had appeared in one of my books, so I told him I’d drawn it and offered to do one for him if he wanted. He told me which landmarks he wanted to include and I did my best. The family tree must have come about in the same way. It was something you do gladly for a friend.”

He used his books as a way to show his love for others. He often roped his kids into making funny promo videos. Many of his friends’ names popped up in his books for characters. I believe one of the reasons he turned Hugo into a bookseller was so he could namecheck his colleagues and promote their books in his own. With Mark, everyone was welcome in his world, whether the real or fictional one.

At first glance, it might not seem like Alex Honnold’s free solo of El Capitan has much in common with Jim Morrison’s first ski descent of Everest’s north face, which is documented in Jimmy Chin’s new film Everest: The Other Side, which releases today, October 30.

But in a conversation during a live recording of Climbing Gold in May, it became clear that several parallels exist between these historic firsts:

  • Both feats entailed years of dreaming and preparation, though took only about four hours to pull off. While Morrison’s expedition lasted nearly two months, the actual ski descent took four hours, five minutes last fall. In 2017, Honnold reached the top of El Cap in three hours, 56 minutes.
  • Both risked fatal falls to pursue historic records—and firmly believe they took measured risks.
  • Jimmy Chin is the filmmaker who documented both achievements, with Free Solo (2018) and Everest North (working title; release date TBD). Chin has called both film projects among his most stressful ever. In June, Chin told Climbing that he never thought he’d find a project as high-takes as Free Solo—until he started documenting Morrison’s attempt to ski Everest’s north side. “On Free Solo, from the day of inception, there wasn’t a day that I didn’t wake up thinking about the worst,” Chin said. “You have to prepare yourself for all potential outcomes. It was a pretty heavy project on a lot of different levels.”
Jimmy Chin climbs up the north side of Mount Everest. (Photo: National Geographic / Erich Roepke)

Despite their shared sky-high risk tolerance, both Honnold and Morrison also agree with the general public on one thing: They consider each other “crazy.” Morrison called Honnold’s ropeless ascent of El Cap “totally insane.” Meanwhile, Honnold struggled to identify the line one might ski down Everest’s north side in a photo. Once Morrison pointed it out, Honnold declared: “That seems like death.”

Honnold is not wrong.

A short ascent history of Everest’s “other side”

A photo from the Everest ski exped on the screen above Alex Honnold, Jim Morrison, and Fitz Cahall during the live recording of Climbing Gold. (Photo: Brad Kaminski)

The Hornbein Couloir on the northern Tibet side of Everest has taken the lives of several climbers, including the snowboarder Marco Siffredi who attempted to make the first descent of the couloir in 2002; his body has never been found.

Two Americans, Thomas Hornbein and Willi Unsoeld, made the first ascent of Everest’s north side in 1963. The north side’s first ascent came just 22 days after Jim Whittaker became the first American to climb Everest via the south side. The 9,000-foot couloir they took to the top was colloquially known as “Hornbein’s Avalanche Chute.” Jimmy Chin also attempted an ascent of Hornbein’s in 2003, but was caught in a near-deadly avalanche.

At the age of 50, after two failed attempts and the staggering loss of Hilaree Nelson, his partner in life and mountains, on Manaslu, Morrison skied down Hornbein’s Couloir on October 15. He pulled it off just four months after undergoing a partial knee replacement. Before Morrison’s ascent last fall—which included a 12-person team of Sherpas, Ecuadorian fixers, and filmmakers—no human had successfully ascended the line since 1991.

Honnold’s conversation with Morrison about his historic ski descent was part of the first-ever live recording of Fitz Cahall’s and Honnold’s Climbing Gold podcast. The interview took the stage at Outside Days on May 31 in Denver, CO. The podcast episode dropped today, alongside the release of the film. You can watch an excerpt above, and read on for Honnold’s and Morrison’s thoughts on grief, the question they dread most, and risking their lives for sport.

On losing the partner in his Everest ski dream

Jim Morrison and Hilaree Nelson on Ironclad Mountain in Colorado in March 2018. (Photo: Jim Morrison) 

Morrison conceived of the goal to ski down Everest’s Hornbein Couloir with Hilaree Nelson. The two ski mountaineers met on Makalu (8,463m) in 2015 and fell in love.

“We were on a tear,” Morrison told Honnold and Cahall, “ticking off these dream runs all over the planet.”

The two had been trying to get to Everest to attempt their ski descent. But COVID delayed their plans, making it impossible to get to the Chinese side of the mountain. To stay in 8,000-meter-peak shape, in 2022, they headed to Manaslu (8,163m) where Morrison recalls they “had a pretty incredible day all by ourselves up on the mountain.” He started skiing down first; Nelson followed, but when Morrison stopped to wait for her, she never appeared. She had set off a small avalanche. “What ensued was a multi-day, horrific rescue of her and trying to find her,” he said. “And it changed my life.”

At first, Morrison wanted to hang it up—never ski or head into the mountains again. But eventually, he began to realize “this is part of me, this is in me.” And he returned to the goal they’d started together. “I wanted to do it for her, and her legacy, and us.”

On the summit, Morrison spread some of Nelson’s ashes. “It was a really emotional moment for me, looking off at mountains I had skied with her, Lhotse and Makalu,” he reflected. “I really felt like she was there.”

On coping with loss

In 2011, before losing Nelson on Manaslu, Morrison’s wife, daughter, and son passed away in a plane crash. While he has experienced unfathomable loss, he continues to try to push past the grief to keep living. On stage, he shared a metaphor he uses to grapple with loss:

I’ve described grief as a big heavy wet blanket that gets put on you. It’s just really hard. You go through a process of losing a loved one or spouse or child and it’s a really heavy weight. I like to think over time you take that blanket and you dry it out and you begin to fold it up. And eventually, you put it in a backpack and you carry it with you.

On being “enough”

Jimmy Chin and Alex Honnold during a screening of Free Solo on January 9, 2019 in New York City. (Photo: Bryan Bedder / Getty for National Geographic)

After free soloing El Cap, Honnold said his life became “a crazy whirlwind.” A week and a half later, he was on an expedition in Alaska. Then the film came out, thus marking his “six month deployment to Hollywood” of screenings and award ceremonies.

The effect was that his memories of free soloing El Cap blurred with the scenes from the film as he watched himself on the big screen, again and again. What he’d set out to do for himself became a messy, public experience. As Chin releases his film, Morrison may undergo a similar experience of distorted memories and mayhem.

Honnold also recalled the dreaded question punted at him on his Hollywood deployment: What’s next? 

Morrison received the same question after his Everest descent and gave an answer Honnold wished he’d come up with years ago: Isn’t this enough?

“I just achieved this 10-year life dream,” Honnold joked of receiving the “what’s next” question. “I don’t have another life dream lined up on the back burner.”

Morrison called achieving his Everest ski descent goal an “incredible journey” and noted that he appreciated the team that supported him throughout the process.

On risk

Jim Morrison and Jimmy Chin at the Everest: The Other Side Premiere at AMC Lincoln Square Theater on October 26, 2026 in New York City.

Cahall teed up his last question by warning that “some of this is triggering to some people.” Meaning, this talk of visualizing fatal falls, of risking it all for the goal. But, Cahall wondered: Are we mistaking complacency for safety?

Morrison said that he doesn’t really see it as complacency versus risk. It’s not an either/or situation. “This is what I’m drawn to and this is what my life’s goals have been centered around,” he said. “I’m not going out there to make a mistake and not come home.” He added that while his ski descent might seem like a “horrendously risky thing,” it was the culmination of extensive planning and preparation, just like Honnold’s El Cap free solo.

What to Consider When Choosing a Garage Heater

Before choosing the best heater for the garage, take a few minutes to learn about some of the most important shopping considerations, including heating method, fuel source, and heat output. There are also critical safety features that can help keep users safe while enjoying a garage heater.

Stationary vs. Portable Garage Heaters

DIYers will want to determine what’s most valuable in their garage: freed-up space on the counter or the floor, or the ability to work at a few different workstations. Knowing this should help when deciding whether to purchase a stationary or a portable garage heater.

Stationary

Busy DIYers may appreciate being free of the trip hazard that electric cords on the floor can create. Stationary garage heaters most often attach to the ceiling, but there also are options that mount to a wall.

There are a wide variety of energy options, sizes, and prices that range from a few hundred to a few thousand dollars. The downside to mounted heaters is that they typically cost more than their portable counterparts because they’re closer to commercial quality.

Portable

No matter the type of heating or fuel used, portable heaters focus warmth where it’s needed the most. Like space heaters on steroids, forced-air options feature large horizontal tubes that house the heating element and a powerful fan that delivers blasts of hot air.

While portable electric units often cost less, they can be less powerful than their multifuel counterparts. Alternatively, portable units can distribute warmth through radiant heat and convection.

Heating Method

Not all garage heaters control the temperature in the same way. There are three primary heating options: forced air, convection, and radiant.

  • Forced-air garage heaters vary in size, fuel type, and price, but all operate by cycling blasts of hot air into the space.
    • Gas-powered models tie into a home’s gas line. This tends to be cost-effective because natural gas and propane are often more affordable than the electricity required to produce the same heat. Gas-powered units, however, cost more up front than electric models, and local codes require installation by a licensed professional.
    • Multifuel forced-air heaters work fast to produce heat, but their powerful fans can stir up debris and may feel uncomfortably warm if pointed directly at the user. Some models produce fumes and water vapor, making adequate ventilation necessary.
  • Convection garage heaters include water- and oil-filled radiators. They rely on an enclosed flame or a heating element to warm the air within the unit, which then rises naturally without the help of a fan. Many are portable, but some—such as baseboard convection heaters—should be mounted.Convection heaters are better for heating entire rooms because they warm the air that circulates naturally, and they rate among the most affordable garage and shop heaters on the market. However, they can take a while to warm a garage to a tolerable temperature, and they won’t offer the intense heating effect of a forced-air or radiant heater.
  • Radiant garage heaters feature highly polished reflectors that direct infrared heat outward for spot heating or—in the case of large overhead units—heating an entire garage. Radiant heaters warm objects directly in front of them (think of sitting near a campfire), so users can start feeling toasty fast if one is pointing in their direction. Because radiant heaters offer steady warmth without blowing air, they are well suited to DIYers who do woodwork since they won’t stir up dust particles. Powered by natural gas, propane, or electricity, these units are available either mounted or portable and in a range of sizes.

Fuel Source

Consumers have a wide range of energy options from which to choose when shopping for a garage heater. While heaters are most commonly fueled by electricity, propane, or natural gas, there are also those that run on diesel and kerosene.

Since electric garage heaters pull a lot of power, these usually require a designated electrical circuit on its own breaker. An electrician can tell if existing garage wiring is adequate to run an electric heater or if a new circuit should be installed.

If there is already natural gas service to a home, there is an option to install a natural gas heater. Propane-powered heaters can be installed on a home’s propane line, or individual tanks of propane to fuel smaller heaters can be purchased.

Heat Output

The best garage heater for a space can produce enough heat to be comfortable, without breaking the budget. Heat output for gas-powered heaters is measured in BTUs. Gas garage heaters typically range from 12,000 to 30,000 BTUs or more.

When trying to determine the heat output of an electric heater, a general guideline is to provide 10 watts of output for approximately every square foot of space. For example, a 100-square-foot room will require a heater with 1,000-watt output for adequate comfortable warmth.

Most heaters advertise the maximum square footage the unit can adequately heat with a standard 8-foot ceiling. If a garage has a higher ceiling, take that into consideration and pick a size up. Insulation will also affect the warmth factor in a garage. Even a high-capacity heater cannot prevent icy drafts from entering through poorly insulated doors and windows.

Safety

Safety must be a primary consideration when deciding on the best garage heater. To help keep the space safe and comfortable, manufacturers have added several features, including cool-touch exteriors, overheating protection, and automatic shut-off systems.

  • Cool-touch exteriors are common with portable and wall-mounted garage heaters. The heater has a shell made of fiberglass or plastic, which are both poor conductors for heat. The exterior of the heater stays cool, despite the heat produced.
  • Overheating protection ensures that the heater doesn’t burn out its own system. When the heater senses dangerous temperatures, it turns off to prevent internal damage to the unit and external damage to the room.
  • Automatic shut-off systems are similar to overheating protection in that the response is to shut down the heater to prevent damage. This system is typically triggered if the heater is knocked over; however, some products also shut down if the heater senses a short in the wiring.

Additional Features

Various different features may add to the convenience and efficiency of a garage heater. These include programmable thermostats, oscillation, and remote controls.

  • Programmable thermostats allow users to set a specific temperature using an electronic keypad or a remote, ensuring that the heater works to reach this temperature and then only activates to maintain the heat.
  • Oscillation helps to properly distribute heated air via an almost 180-degree rotation. Though it is most common with portable garage heaters, some wall-mounted models can have oscillating louvers (vents) that can change the direction of the airflow.
  • Remote controls offer the convenience of being able to operate the heater from anywhere in the room, so there’s no need to walk over to turn the heat off or to change the temperature setting.

Installation

Portable garage heaters only need to be positioned and provided with fuel or electricity to function. Wall- and ceiling-mounted garage heaters typically come with the hardware required to install the heater, but some products don’t have mounting brackets. Also, for hard-wired installations (these heaters don’t have plugs), it may be necessary to hire an electrician.

Mount a heater in the coldest corner of the garage, ensuring that ceiling-mounted models are at least 24 inches from the walls to reduce fire risk. Direct the heater toward the center of the garage and follow the manufacturer’s steps to make sure that the heater is installed according to its specifications.

FAQs 

Even after becoming familiar with the best garage heaters, there may still be some lingering questions about the type, efficiency, and appropriate size. Below are answers to some of the most commonly asked questions about garage heaters to provide additional info.

Q. What type of heater is best for a garage?

The best type of heater for the garage depends on your needs. Large garages benefit from a natural gas or propane heater because of the high heating capacity, allowing the heater to adequately warm the space. However, an electric garage heater is a safer alternative and the heating capacity is suitable for smaller garages.

Q. What size heater do I need for my 2-car garage?

Depending on where you live, the answer to this question can vary. Areas with mild winters will naturally require less heat production to warm a space than locations with harsh, freezing winters.

However, an electric garage heater with 3,000 watts is usually needed to heat a 2-car garage. The best gas garage heater for a 2-car garage typically has a rating of about 18,000 BTUs to adequately heat the space.

Q. What is the most efficient garage heater?

Efficiency can be difficult to determine because an electric infrared heater technically has 100 percent efficiency, but it may actually cost more to operate than the best natural gas garage heater. This is due to the cost of electricity over natural gas or even propane. If you are looking for a heater that uses almost every watt of energy for heating, then an infrared heater can be the best option.

Q. Can a garage heater be too big?

Yes, a garage heater can be too big. If it is too big, the excess heat produced quickly makes the garage feel uncomfortable, forcing you to open the door to release some heat. If you have a smaller garage, consider using a portable heater to keep the space warm.

Q. Will a 1,500-watt heater heat a garage?

It depends on the size of the garage. Typically, a good general guideline for electric heaters is that they provide 10 watts of output for every square foot of space. This means that as long as the garage is 150 square feet or smaller, a 1,500-watt heater is more than adequate.

Q. Where should a heater be placed in the garage?

Portable garage heaters can be placed anywhere. Fixed or mounted garage heaters should be installed in the coldest corner of the garage, directing the heat and airflow toward the center of the space. Make sure that heaters are installed at least 24 inches away from the walls, and always check the manufacturer’s directions so you install and use the unit properly.

In Dark Eden, I imagined how life might exist on a sunless planet

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →
Eden, the planet in Chris Beckett’s Dark Eden, drifts outside the Milky Way McCarthy's PhotoWorks/Shutterstock

I came up with the idea of the planet Eden in 1992 for a short story called The Circle of Stones. At that time, I had no idea that there really were such things as planets without suns, but it seemed likely to me. It also seemed possible to me that life might arise on such a planet, since, even here on Earth, whole ecologies thrive without sunlight around thermal vents in the deep ocean. Life on Earth may have even have begun in such places.

Quite often, if something seems likely, it does indeed happen. (In another book, I predicted that a US president would one day seek to take over Greenland and Canada.) Having written that story, I learned that free-floating planets did indeed exist. Being dark, they’re hard to spot, but a number have been detected using gravitational lensing. And I gather it’s not particularly controversial to think that some of them might support life (though not quite in the form that I chose to imagine it – but I’ll come to that).

When creating a world for a story, I don’t usually seek to make the whole thing scientifically accurate, but I try to make it hang together in a way that feels coherent. I didn’t build Eden by doing a lot of research, but I did put a lot of thought into how it might all work. This wasn’t so I could put it all in the book – no one in Eden knows this stuff and the story is told entirely by its characters! – but so that I had something to guide my imagination and make my invented world feel coherent.

My idea was that Eden had once been covered in ice, but its hot core meant there was liquid water below the ice in which life could come into existence. One particular kind of life form appeared, somewhat resembling tree roots, that was able to obtain additional energy by pushing deep down into hot rocks, pumping its sap through them to heat it, and then pumping it back up again, like a geothermal power station. One of the ways these organisms used this heat was to push upwards into the planet’s shell of ice, melting parts of it, feeding on the nutrients it contained, and creating more living space – and they carried on doing so until much of the surface of Eden was ice-free, and had an atmosphere that would turn out to be breathable by humans.

Millions of years later, this thawed-out surface, now populated by a multitude of different life forms, became the stage on which my drama could unfold: first that early story, then another story and then the novel Dark Eden and its two sequels. In Dark Eden, a tiny community of humans, descended from stranded visitors from Earth, eke out a living using tools made of stone and wood, and try to make sense of their life as exiles from a sunlit world that lives on in folk memory, but which they can only very shakily imagine. (In the biblical story of Eden, humans are exiled from Eden – my Eden is the place of exile.) Their stories tell them that Earth people could fly through the air, that there was a thing there called leckytrickity, that Earth animals had eyes that you could look into and see their minds looking back out at you, not like the flat, blank eyes of Eden creatures.

On the surface of Eden, those root-like life forms put up structures that Eden’s humans call “trees”. A whole ecosystem has evolved around them, including “starflowers” that parasitise their roots. (These, as it happens, turn out to be a rich source of Vitamin D. Phew! What a stroke of luck!) And there are many animals – or quote-unquote “animals”, strictly speaking, since they’re not related to the animal kingdom of Earth. There’s no sunlight in Eden (and therefore no alternation of day and night) but, like organisms that live in the ocean depths of Earth, Eden’s life has developed bioluminescence. The “trees” have luminous “lanternflowers”; the starflowers glitter. Imagine a leafless forest on a moonless night, lit by thousands of Chinese lanterns. Then add in twinkling fairy lights at ground level. With your eyes half-closed, the forests of Eden would look something like that.

Just as life on Earth depends on plants that turn sunlight into chemical energy, so the life of Eden depends on geothermal trees. They bring heat to the surface to power their metabolisms, they keep the air warm and, if you live on Eden, the steady “Hmmmph hmmmph hmmmph” of their constant pumping is the background of your waking and sleeping life, merging in the distance to a steady hum. All the trees are hot, and some of them are so hot that you’d burn yourself if you touched their trunks. If you cut down a tree, you have to watch out for the spurt of scalding liquid that bursts out.

When I read stories set on alien planets, I’m sometimes disappointed in the “animals” (I’ll drop the inverted commas now). Writers often seem to dream up a motley collection of bizarre creatures without much regard for how they might actually survive or how they are related to one another. I tried to design my animals on evolutionary principles, giving thought to how they were adapted to their environment, the role they played in the ecosystem and how they might plausibly have evolved. Animals on Earth fall into a large but finite number of basic body plans. For example, monkeys, cows, crocodiles and seagulls all have four limbs and, though some descendants of quadrupeds have lost their limbs (snakes, whales), there are no examples of quadrupeds developing extra ones. In the same way, large animals on Eden all have a six-limbed body plan. There are other animals that don’t conform to this, just as there are arthropods, et cetera on Earth, but the large animals are all based on this six-limbed pattern.

I’ve already mentioned how different Eden eyes are from the eyes of terrestrial animals. They have evolved alongside the evolution of bioluminescence, rather in the way that colourful flowers and nectar-eating insects have evolved alongside one another on Earth. Eden trees use luminous lanternflowers to attract the creatures that help them reproduce, just as flowering plants on Earth use colour and scent.

I gave a lot of thought to the biology of Eden, but I have to admit that, in strictly scientific terms, my imaginary world isn’t entirely viable. Free-floating planets with warm cores? Yes! Not just possible, but a certainty! But free-floating planets with warm surfaces? Sadly no. More than one astronomer has told me they just couldn’t happen. No matter how many radiator-like trees there might be, I’ve been told, it still wouldn’t be enough to maintain liquid water or a warm atmosphere.

Let’s call it “fiction science”, science made up for a story, which works in some places and in others allows itself poetic licence. Almost all science fiction uses fiction science and Dark Eden contains several other examples. I took some liberties with human genetics, for instance. And then there’s the question of how people got to Eden in the first place. (It’s many light years away from Earth.) Like the writers of pretty much all stories set in interstellar space, I pretended that faster-than-light travel was possible and went with that, although it most probably isn’t.

(I think that’s OK. It allows us to visit, in our imaginations at least, parts of the universe we could never otherwise hope to reach.)

What I wanted from Eden was for it to be a world that hung together well enough to be a satisfying setting for my story, one in which I and my readers could immerse ourselves. The story is about exile and loss. The perpetually dark sky, and the animals with their blank unreadable eyes, serve as reminders that Eden folk are exiles. At the beginning of Dark Eden, every human being in Eden lives around a circle of stones that marks the place where their ancestors first touched down. And this circle represents to Eden folk the hope for return and redemption, which is central to their whole world view.

The fact that I took some liberties when constructing Eden doesn’t mean it wouldn’t be interesting also to write about a real free-floating planet. And, as it happens, I’ve had the opportunity recently to do just that. In a project initiated by Comma Press, I was one of a number of writers who paired up with astronomers from Jodrell Bank to write stories for an anthology called Firmament. My astronomer collaborator, Ida Janiak, helped me build a scenario in which a free-floating planet called Lupus arrives in the solar system. Apart from being roughly Earth-sized, Lupus isn’t much like Eden, but somewhat like the Jovian moon Europa. Its surface is covered by a kilometres-thick shell of ice in no danger of melting, with a liquid ocean beneath and… well, read the story! The anthology will appear in November but there will also be a condensed version of mine and Ida’s story in the Christmas edition of New Scientist, out online on 16 December and in print on the 19th.

I hope, meanwhile, you will immerse yourself in the luminous forests of Eden with their pulsing trees. Possible or not, they are very beautiful, if I say so myself.

Learning (or remembering) how to field dress a deer can feel overwhelming, but if you have a sharp knife and a reasonably strong stomach, you’ll be able to figure it out. If you manage to do so without nicking the guts or yourself, that’s ideal.

Removing the guts is the bare minimum required for dropping a deer at a processor, and an essential skill every deer hunter should learn. A field-dressed deer is also a lighter deer, which makes dragging and loading it less cumbersome. And the sooner you gut a deer after shooting it, the better the venison will be. So, here’s a guide on how to field dress a deer in a few easy steps.

Gear You’ll Need

All you really need to gut a deer is a sharp knife and, if it’s getting dark, a good headlamp. Other deer hunters carry the kitchen sink with them and won’t field dress a deer without some or all of the following items:

  • Field-dressing gloves
  • Gut hook (for unzipping the abdomen)
  • Knife sharpener
  • Bone saw, hand saw, or hatchet (for sawing rib cage or splitting the pelvis)
  • Butt Out gadget (for removing the rectum)
  • Paracord (for tying up a leg when hunting solo)
  • Ziplock bags for saving any desired offal (heart, caul fat, etc.)

How to Field Dress a Deer 

Many experienced deer hunters can field dress a deer in minutes, while it can take new or casual deer hunters a half hour or more. The best way to gut a deer is to work slowly, use a sharp knife, and have adequate lighting.

Other Field Dressing Tips

Keep these gutting rules in mind before you get started:

  • Your knife isn’t the only sharp object you’re likely to encounter inside a deer’s rib cage. Bullets can smash and break bone that becomes razor sharp. Broken arrow shafts or broadheads (yours or another hunter’s) may be lodged inside the deer. Always proceed cautiously.
  • Everyone dresses a deer slightly differently, so there’s not one right way to do it. For instance: I prefer to free the rectum last so I don’t risk getting urine or feces on my blade and then get that mess on the tenderloins. Other deer hunters I know do this step first or not at all.
  • If you’re able, recruit a friend to hold a leg or grab a rib while you work. It’s absolutely possible to field dress a deer solo, but it’s easier with help.
  • Once you’ve made initial cuts, make your cuts on a deer from the inside out — not the outside in. If you cut from the outside, you’re more likely to puncture guts or dull your knife on deer hair and get it everywhere.

Position the Deer on Its Back

Roll a deer onto its back and balance it evenly on its shoulder blades and rump. Photos by Natalie Krebs A deer can usually rest belly-up without anyone holding a leg if arranged evenly.

Start by rolling the deer onto its back. It should be belly-up and evenly balanced on both shoulder blades. Adjust the rump and hips to ensure they’re as even as possible. If you’re working on a slope, rotate the deer so its rump points downhill and its head is uphill.

Ask a buddy to hold a leg. If you’re solo and the deer keeps slipping sideways or downhill, try wedging a log or stick under its rump or using paracord to tie a hind leg to a branch. Personally, I find paracord to be overkill, but will sometimes use my knee, shoulder, or armpit to anchor a hind leg or keep it out of the way as I work.

Cut Away the Reproductive Organs

This step and the next can be done now or after opening the abdominal cavity. Grab a handful of the mammary gland, or testicles with your non-dominant hand and lift away from the deer’s body. Keeping your blade mostly parallel to the deer’s body, slice away at the base of the stretched hide until you’ve freed the reproductive organs, and toss aside. The goal is to avoid cutting into the organs and instead cutting under them.

Grab a piece of the hide in the center of the pelvis to begin cutting away reproductive organs like the mammary glands or testicles. Pull at the hide with your off hand and keep slicing with your knife to remove the patch of hide with the reproductive organs attached. The next cut will run just above where the hide meets the purple of muscle. After making the final cut, you’ll have freed the reproductive organs (toss aside) and begin to reveal meat (the purple color on the deer’s inside hind leg).

Cut Between the Two Hindquarters to the Pelvic Bone (Optional)

This step can be done later or not at all, but I prefer to do this now (particularly when working alone) since it relaxes the tension between the hind legs and prevents your deer from rolling on you. It also creates more space to work while removing internal organs. I butcher my own deer and this does not sacrifice any of the meat on the hindquarters when done correctly.

Cut the muscle between the two back legs to expose the center of the pelvic bone. Find what looks like dead center on the meat between the two back legs and cut down (usually a few inches) from the top of the pelvis to the anus. Stay as close to the center as possible to avoid gouging the roasts on either leg. Don’t saw the muscle. Slice it instead, letting the downward pressure of your blade do the cutting.

Slicing between the two hindquarters to reach the pelvic bone is optional, but it will relax the tension on the deer’s hips to stabilize the carcass and create more room to work.

Cut the Rectum

Insert your knife tip into the side (not the center) of the anus. Keeping your blade pressed against the bone that encircles the rectum, cut around the rectum rather than through it. The goal is to cut the connective tissue so you can pull the tube of intestines out rather than dice it up.

  • A long, thin blade is handy for this part (like a smaller fillet knife).
  • You can also use a shorter blade and insert it from both ends of the pelvic channel. I prefer the latter since it reduces the risk of puncturing the bladder, which sits at the top of the pelvis. 
The rectum lies inside a column of bone created by the pelvis. Begin to free it by inserting your knife tip around the anus and cutting in circular motions. Cut around the rectum in a circular motion on all sides — not through the center. Keep your knife blade flat against the bone of the pelvic column to guide you.

Make the First Cut to the Abdominal Cavity

This abdominal cut is often the most difficult for hunters, and the key here is not to puncture the deer’s stomach or bladder because you don’t want to get any bile or urine on the meat.

Prevent stabbing the guts by turning your knife blade up (toward the sky) and tilting it so the blade is more parallel to the deer’s belly rather than perpendicular. Working close to the base of the belly, grab a handful of hide and pull it up to create some space between the layer of hide and the organs beneath. Insert the tip of your knife and make an incision until you see (or more likely, feel with the knife tip) empty space below it. Keep the incision shallow and make the slit long enough to work a finger or two of your off-hand in the space. Take care to not insert the blade too far into the abdominal cavity.

Unzip the Abdomen

Now that you’ve made an opening incision, apply even upward pressure with your knife blade to “unzip” the abdomen, or make a lengthwise cut along the deer from pelvis to breastbone. This is similar to using a pair of scissors to slice a piece of wrapping paper in a single, fluid motion. Don’t saw with your knife.

These two groups are separated by the diaphragm: a thin wall of purple muscle between the abdominal cavity and the rib cage.

Split the Ribs Along the Sternum (Optional)

Note: If you plan to shoulder mount your deer, do not split the ribs. You should only make your cut to the base of the lower ribcage (above) and stop there, since your taxidermist will need all that hide from the ribs to the tip of the nose (this is called the cape).

If you are planning on a skull mount or you aren’t mounting your deer at all, you can split the ribs if you want. Your blade will encounter some resistance once you hit the rib cage. If you center your knife, you can slice through cartilage in the breastplate before hitting bone. Some hunters use a hatchet or saw to split the rib cage open, but a stout fixed-blade knife will also work. Splitting the rib cage makes it easier to see (and therefore remove) the heart and lungs, but isn’t necessary if you are having trouble cutting through it. This step is especially useful if you suspect a broadhead is lodged in the rib cage.

Cut the Diaphragm

To access the heart and lungs in the rib cage (and ultimately free everything) you need to cut the diaphragm. Use the tip of your knife to puncture this thin layer of muscle, then circle the entire rib cage, keeping your knife close to the ribs. Once the diaphragm has been cut away, blood and jellied bits of lung usually slosh into the abdominal cavity. This is perfectly normal (and a sign of a good shot).

Cut around the diaphragm, keeping your blade close to the ribs.

Remove the Internal Organs

Work from the top of deer down toward the pelvis. If you split open the rib cage this part will be easier; if not, you’ll have to do it more by feel.

At the top of the chest cavity, you’ll see or feel a large ribbed tube. That’s the windpipe. Cut it crossways (perpendicular to the spine), then use a finger or two to grab ahold of it from the inside and begin pulling it up and away. This will begin to loosen the heart and lungs.

Grab hold of the heart and/or lungs with your off-hand and pull while continuing to cut connective tissue along the spine. Keep going all the way down to the pelvis and keep working until the stomach and guts come loose, too. Because all the organs are attached to the deer along the spine, it can be helpful to tilt the deer on its side for this part so the organs roll out of your way and reveal their connection point.

As everything loosens, just roll the guts and intestines out of the deer and onto the ground. Note: If you haven’t yet separated the rectum from the anus, the guts will still connect to the carcass by a single tube — that’s the lower large intestines, or colon. To free the guts and toss aside, slice the colon crossways just above the pelvis and tie it off (if it’s full) so pellets don’t spill inside your deer.

  • Unlike the guts, there’s no risk of nicking anything inside the rib cage (except your own hands). If you intend to keep and cook the heart (an excellent idea), avoid stabbing or slicing it.
  • If something you want to remove is still attached to the deer, just pull and cut connective tissue until it’s free.
  • Depending on the age of your deer (older deer tend to have more connective tissue and are harder to work on) you can usually pull much of this connective tissue away rather than cutting it.
  • The tenderloins will become exposed here, so take care not to stab or slash them as you cut the connective tissue.
  • Once the last of the guts and vitals are removed, you can tilt the carcass so the blood drains onto the ground.

How to Field Dress a Deer with a Hatchet

To field dress big game with a hatchet, use it as you would a big belt knife, opening the paunch with a small incision at the base of the sternum. Then, tip the blade at right angles to the animal, with the handle up and your fingers spreading the skin close to the blade as you run it to the rectum. Do not try to cut the skin down into the animal; hair will collect on and dull the blade. Dump the entrails.

To quarter the carcass, hang it or set it belly-up on the ground. Loosen hams with careful strokes tight against the pelvis and through the ball joints. Don’t chop; use the blade like a knife, your fist around the hatchet head (inset). Remove the shoulders the same way, leaving the skin on unless you must remove it to let a big animal, like an elk or moose, cool in warm weather. The hatchet (or, better yet, a slim double-bit ax) excels at splitting the backbone. Hang the animal head down, rear legs spread, to facilitate this task. —Wayne Van Zwoll

Additional Tips for Gutting a Deer

  • Keep a knife sharpener, a rag and a bottle of water, or wipes handy in your vehicle. If you get blood on your camo, don’t worry — it will lift right out with some hydrogen peroxide before you throw it in the wash.
  • Gloves can keep your hands clean and protect you from disease (although rare). The downside to wearing gloves is they can make it harder to feel what you’re doing.
  • You don’t have to gut a deer on the ground. You can do it in the back of a truck or once it’s hanging. Some hunters prefer to gut a hanging deer (one that’s hanging head-up) since gravity helps pull the heavy internal organs away from the deer.
How long can I wait to field dress a deer?


It’s always best to gut your deer as soon as possible, but the window you have depends on the weather and the shot you made. If the weather is hot or even mild, the sooner you field dress your deer, the better. Within an hour of the shot is ideal in these conditions. If it’s cold, a deer can last longer before it needs to be gutted — hours or even overnight if you have to back out while blood trailing. It’s always easier to dress a deer when it’s warm and pliable.

What’s the best field-dressing knife?

The best knives for field dressing are usually a fixed-blade knife with a sharp blade. A smaller, handier blade makes certain tasks easier, like removing the colon and avoiding puncturing the guts. A larger, stouter blade is easier for heavy-duty tasks like splitting the ribs.

Final Thoughts About How to Field Dress a Deer

Take your time, being sure not to cut yourself as you work. Photo by Natalie Krebs

Field-dressing a deer isn’t difficult once you get the hang of it, and it’s usually easier with a buddy to help hold a leg or a light. Use a sharp knife, make slow, deliberate cuts at connective tissue. Do your best to avoid puncturing the stomach or bladder, but don’t panic if you do. More important is to avoid cutting yourself. The process may be messy or unpleasant, but ultimately you’ll end up with a cooling carcass ready for the skinning shed or processor.
