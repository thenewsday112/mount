Judge lets Bay Area woman keep her ‘RVRTSEA’ license plate — for now

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Automotive  |  Read Source →

(CN) — A federal judge ruled Friday that a Bay Area woman can keep her personalized license plate reading, “RVRTSEA” — at least for now.

Iasmine Abdennabi has had the license plate, which is an abbreviation of the slogan, “river to the sea,” since 2024. The phrase is shorthand for: “from the river to the sea, Palestine will be free.”

The saying, often chanted during protests and rallies, has different interpretations. Some see it as a simple support for the embattled state and people of Palestine, currently under siege by Israel. Others see it as a call to arms, or worse — a call for the destruction of the state of Israel, which is bounded by Jordan River and the Mediterranean Sea. The Anti-Defamation League, for example, calls the slogan an “antisemitic” rallying cry “denying the Jewish right to self-determination, including through the removal of Jews from their ancestral homeland.”

Many others disagree, especially in the U.S., where the slogan can be heard at any number of pro-Palestinian protests. In her complaint, filed on Monday, Abdennabi states that to her, the phrase is not an “instruction to commit genocide,” nor “a threat against Jews,” or call to violence. She says her intended message is “support for equal civil and political rights for all persons living between the Jordan River and Mediterranean Sea.”

The California Department of Motor Vehicles received a total of three complaints about Abdennabi’s license plate, including one from a man who wrote: “This advocates for the extermination of the state of Israel and all Jewish people. It supports terrorism and encourages violence.” Following department policy, the DMV ordered Abdennabi to turn over her plates in 2025, 15 months after it had issued them.

Abdennadi appealed, triggering an administrative hearing, at which a DMV representative explained the plates were removed “based on the connotation of violence or genocide.”

More than a million Californians have personalized license plates, far more than any other state in the country. License plates are rejected for a number of reasons: having swear words, dirty jokes, sexual connotations, hate speech, offensive messages, and references to violence, drugs and illegal activity, including references to gangs and marijuana. No plates with the number “420” are allowed, for example.

After her administrative appeal was denied, Abdennadi sued, arguing the DMV was infringing on her First Amendment rights to express her political beliefs. On Friday, a federal judge granted her a restraining order, allowing her to keep her “RVRTSEA” license plates, at least until a scheduled preliminary injunction hearing next month.

In his order, U.S. District Judge Richard Seeborg cast doubt on the constitutionality of the regulations that allow the DMV to reject certain vanity plates.

“On its face, this regulation appears to bar three separate things: fighting words, incitement, and speech referring to violence of various types,” Seeborg wrote. “One of those things is not like the others. Fighting words and incitement are long-recognized categories of low-value speech that do not merit First Amendment protection. By contrast, speech merely ‘referring to’ violence seems more akin to the language of offense, which the Supreme Court explicitly identified as protected First Amendment expression.”

He added if that was the case, then the regulations were “unconstitutional because they discriminate among private speech based on viewpoint.”

Abdennabi’s attorney, Michael Millen, praised the ruling.

“We are grateful to the court for recognizing that the government may not serve as the arbiter of free speech,” she said in an email. “Plaintiff is a firm believer in equal civil and political rights for all persons living between the Jordan River and the Mediterranean Sea, and the DMV was wrong to revoke the license plate ‘RVRTSEA’ because an anonymous complainant and two other people felt it was ‘offensive.'”

Attorneys for the DMV did not respond to an email requesting comment on the ruling.

Prosecution’s star witness describes bribery plot in trial of ex-Oakland mayor

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

SAN FRANCISCO (CN) — The government’s star witness in a high-profile trial in which former Oakland Mayor Sheng Thao is accused of bribery and corruption took the stand Friday, testifying as to how he and his former business partners conspired to bribe Thao and her romantic partner Andre Jones in exchange for lucrative city contracts.

Thao, 41, was indicted in January 2025 along with co-defendants Jones, 55, David Duong, 68 and Andy Duong, 37, on eight counts between them, including conspiracy, bribery, fraud and making a false statement to a government official, in what the government described as a corruption scheme of “paid and accepted bribes for the promise of future influence.”

All four co-defendants have pleaded not guilty. Thao faces a maximum sentence of 95 years in prison if convicted of all charges.

Mario Juarez, 49, testified for nearly five hours about his decades-long relationship with Oakland businessman David Duong and his son Andy Duong, whose family owns local recycling business California Waste Solutions. Juarez later founded a modular-home startup with the Duongs called Evolutionary Homes.

Under questioning from U.S. Attorney Abraham Fine, Juarez began his testimony by telling the 18-person jury that he pleaded guilty to bribing a public official and implicated the Duongs, Thao and Jones in the scheme. Juarez pleaded guilty to conspiracy to commit a bribe in exchange for his cooperation with the prosecution and a more lenient sentence.

Many of Fine’s questions relied on Juarez’s texts and WhatsApp messages with Andy Duong, Thao and Jones, Juarez’s Apple Notes, and photos taken by Andy Duong.

Juarez recounted how he got into business with the Duong family, who supported him during his first campaign for city council in 2008. After his second failed campaign in 2012, Juarez said he helped the Duongs with political issues, including lobbying the city council to secure a recycling contract for California Waste Solutions in 2014.

Juarez recalled people lining up outside California Waste Solutions’ office after it received the city contract to collect money. He noted he personally stuffed two gym bags full of cash, one with $300,000 and the other with $50,000, for two Oakland city council members. 

Juarez said he did not get paid for his work for the Duongs.

“Frankly, I didn’t think I needed cash at that point; it was more valuable to have a friendship than money,” he said.

He detailed his deep loyalty to the Duongs, explaining how he got into a physical altercation with someone trying to collect signatures to overturn the city council’s decision to award the contract to California Waste Solutions.

“David reacted with pride when told about the shoving match,” Juarez said. “Someone stood up to him; he valued loyalty; he saw that in me, saw that I was someone who would stand up for CWS.”

Turning to the 2022 mayoral race, Juarez said he and the Duongs had data that Thao would lose the election and decided to approach Thao with an offer to pay for attack mailers on her opponents in exchange for the city buying 100 units of housing from Evolutionary Homes if she was elected. 

While the funding for the mailers came from an independent expenditure, which cannot be affiliated with political campaigns, Juarez said he spoke with Thao and Jones directly about the deal, including in a meeting where Thao asked for $300,000, which would be paid through Jones. 

Juarez claimed Thao later verbally agreed to a 10-year extension of California Waste Solutions’ city contract and contracts for the company to do work at the nearby Army base. She further committed to appointing their chosen official to the housing department, Juarez said. He said he pushed the appointment of Larry Gallegos, describing him as “essential” for Evolutionary Homes.

“He knew the inner workings of the city. If given a mandate, he would do it. He would do it the right way, he would do it legally and he would get it done,” he said.

The deal later expanded to include $3 million for Jones and a commitment to purchase 300 units of housing from Evolutionary Homes. However, Gallegos was ultimately offered an interim deputy position, which Juarez said he and Andy Duong had an issue with.

“We didn’t invest talent, money, hits, took the time out of our days because we like Sheng Thao. We invested money in her because she would give us money in return — through contracts, extension on CWS, the Army base,” he said.  

Juarez’s testimony will continue next Thursday, followed by cross-examination by the defense.

Representatives for the parties did not immediately respond to a request for comment.

With spooky season behind us, we’re into fall’s final stretch. Here at Backpacker, we made the most of it: A month ago, our editors got together to backpack a stretch of Wisconsin’s Ice Age Trail, then spent October exploring the forests of the Pacific Northwest, strolling our local trails in Vermont, and even notching a few early-season ski days in Colorado. Here’s the gear that helped us do it

Therm-a-Rest Z-Lite Sol (Photo: Adam Roy)

Therm-a-Rest Z-Lite Sol

$60 at REI

I know: How brave of me to pick what must be the most commonly-used sleeping pad in existence. But some things are classics for a reason, and this folding closed-cell pad is one of them. Consider: It’s unpoppable, a big deal when you regularly go backpacking in the desert like I do; it doesn’t need to be inflated or deflated, which eliminates one task from both your nighttime and morning routines; and it’s light, with a regular running just 14 ounces.

Is it comfortable? Opinions on that vary. I’ll admit that it may not be the supportive option available for side sleepers (or, with its R-value of 2.0, cold sleepers) but I’ve found its egg-crate-style design to be comfier than flat closed-cell models like Walmart’s roll-up Ozark Trail. Anyway, if you’re not tired enough to fall asleep the minute your head hits the ground, you could always just try hiking farther. —Adam Roy, Editor-in-Chief

Ruffwear Palisades Sleep Poncho (Photo: Zoe Gates)

Ruffwear Palisades Sleep Poncho

$100 at Ruffwear

This poncho is the answer to a dilemma I’ve faced since starting to backpack with my dog. I worry about keeping her warm on chilly nights, but a dog-size sleeping bag never seemed like the best solution, and she’s too big to crawl in with me. The Palisades Sleep Poncho from Ruffwear is the first wearable sleeping bag I’ve seen available; it offers more full-coverage warmth than a dog jacket, stays put better than a blanket, all while being less confining than a sleeping bag. Featuring a ripstop shell and packable synthetic insulation, the poncho slips over the dog’s head and drapes over their back so they have the freedom to move around, curl up, roll over or sprawl without compromising coverage.

A cinchable neck baffle keeps in warmth, and soft, adjustable leg loops eliminate slippage (it’s also easy to bundle up the rear of the poncho with a button for potty breaks). On an October night camping in Washington’s Mt. Baker Wilderness, temps dipped below freezing. My dog, who is usually restless in the tent, slept soundly all night curled beneath the Palisades Poncho. Her water dish was frozen solid when we woke up, but she was cozy and content all night long. Paired with a pup-sized foam pad, this poncho makes for a doggy sleep system I can feel confident in. Bonus: It’s extremely cute. —Zoe Gates, Senior Editor

Thrudark Charge Trousers (Photo: Will Brendza)

Thrudark Charge Trousers

$430 at Thrudark

I should have mentioned the price of these hiking pants in the video, but I forgot to. So I’m going to lead with it here: $430 for a single pair of pants is a little outrageous no matter how you spin it. That’s about the same cost as weatherproof snowpants. But the Charge Trousers from Thrudark are exceptionally well made, thoughtfully designed, and built with premium materials (some of which I’ve only ever seen in packs and tents). There are three main things I like about these pants. First, the layout is exceptional. There are six pockets (all on the front), but they don’t look like cargo pants. The ankle cuffs have snap-button adjusters to tighten or loosen them depending on preference and what you’re doing. The hips have Velcro adjusters built in, so you can dial in a perfect fit to your waist, and despite that feature, Thrudark still included belt loops. (Some brands don’t do loops if they have the adjusters, and that drives me nuts.)

My second favorite thing is the materials that these pants are made with. Thrudark used a high-tensile cotton and a Dyneema-Cordura blend that is both highly durable and lightweight. The knees are reinforced with that blend, so you can kneel in a bed of nails and would probably be just fine (at least the pants would be). The rear panel has a four-way stretch material over the butt, which offers a lot of freedom of movement. Third and finally, they’re just comfortable. I can’t say that about every brand’s hiking pants. Where do they fall short? The price, obviously. There’s also the question of how well the cotton elements would hold up if exposed to too much rain (or ungodly perspiration from the wearer). Maybe I’d ask Thrudark for one more pocket on the back, but then again, who on Earth needs seven pockets? —Will Brendza, Associate Editor

Ornot Alpha Wool Pullover (Photo: Benjamin Tepler)

Ornot Alpha Wool Pullover

$198 at Ornot

Polartec, a well-known fabric company that supplies much of the outdoor industry with its fleece, just released two new versions of its popular Alpha fabric that you’ll be seeing in big-name and cottage brands this winter. One of them is Alpha Wool, a fabric that uses a wool base weave with tufts of Alpha polyester insulation on the outside.

Our very first sample is a crew pullover from cycling brand Ornot. It’s not quite as featherlight as pure Alpha, but at 8.5-ounces in medium, still lighter than micro-grid fleece. Because of that base wool layer, it’s totally stink-free. It’s also slightly more wind-resistant, softer, warmer, and more durable than regular Alpha. —Benjamin Tepler, Senior Gear Editor
