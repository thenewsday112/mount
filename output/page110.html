SEC Charges Two Companies in $130 Million Crypto Trading Scheme

The Securities and Exchange Commission charged two foreign-operated, Colorado-incorporated companies with running a crypto investment fraud that used false federal filings to raise more than $130 million from tens of thousands of retail investors.

The defendants are BG Wealth Sharing Ltd. and DSJ Exchange Pty Ltd. A complaint filed in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California alleges that from at least October 2025 through June 2026, the companies induced investors in more than 40 states and several U.S. territories to join a “wealth-sharing program.” 

Participants were promised risk-free returns generated by so-called signals for crypto asset trading.

According to the complaint, BG Wealth claimed to be the world’s largest hedge fund, claimed registration with the SEC as an investment adviser, and said it partnered with a licensed and secure crypto trading platform, DSJ Exchange. 

The SEC says none of those claims were true. The complaint alleges the companies submitted false Form ADV and Form D filings to the SEC. Those filings were viewable on the agency’s website and shared with investors as evidence that the SEC had licensed or approved the firms.

Laura D’Allaird, chief of the SEC Enforcement Division’s Cyber and Emerging Technologies Unit, described the operation in a statement. 

SEC: Crypto scheme was ‘a Ponzi-like tactic’

“This alleged scheme was well-planned,” she said, citing social media, encrypted messaging channels, weekly live videoconferences and a mobile app used to engage investors. The complaint alleges the defendants displayed fake certificates bearing the SEC’s seal and made periodic payments to some investors, “a Ponzi-like tactic,” while misappropriating the remaining funds.

The SEC charged both companies with violating the antifraud provisions of the Securities Act of 1933 and the Securities Exchange Act of 1934.

It charged BG Wealth with violating Sections 204(a) and 207 of the Investment Advisers Act of 1940. The agency seeks permanent injunctions, disgorgement with prejudgment interest, and civil penalties against both companies, along with conduct-based injunctions against BG Wealth.

The Form ADV and Form D filings have been removed from the SEC website.

The SEC’s Office of Investor Education and Assistance has issued investor alerts warning that scammers are using exempt reporting adviser filings to create a false impression of legitimacy. The agency encourages investors to check the background of anyone offering or selling a crypto investment through Investor.gov.

The SEC said it received assistance from the Alberta Securities Commission, the Hawaii Department of Commerce and Consumer Affairs, the Texas State Securities Board, the Utah Division of Securities, the Washington State Department of Financial Institutions, the Wisconsin Department of Financial Institutions, and other authorities.

Send your name to Saturn’s largest moon

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

NASA is still on track to send its Dragonfly rotocraft to Saturn’s moon Titan by the end of the decade, and you can come along for the ride. Well, you can at least add your initials to the flight roster.

As part of its Journey to Titan campaign, NASA has opened an online registry that will be included on a microchip aboard the spacecraft when it launches. The launch is currently scheduled for no earlier than July 2028. Signees will receive a personalized digital “boarding pass,” as proof that their names will reach the ice-encased Saturnian moon in 2034. While the encoded autographs may not do much to help with the overall mission, it’s nice to know that you will be represented on what may become one of the most consequential space endeavors of all time.

“We are excited to bring humanity along for the journey of a lifetime, and we invite everyone to send their names on this small, but mighty rotorcraft that will unlock the secrets of the universe,” Nicky Fox, NASA’s Science Mission Directorate associate administrator, said in the announcement.

Titan is the largest of Saturn’s 292 known moons, and a longtime dream destination in the search for life beyond Earth. Although Titan features dusty dunes overlooking lakes and rivers of methane, the frigid surface also maintains a temperature of around −290 degrees Fahrenheit. This is inhospitable to life as we know it, but a giant ocean also exists beneath the icy shell. Astrobiologists widely agree that these waters probably contain enough complex compounds to facilitate a prebiotic environment. Laboratory simulations of Titan’s atmosphere have also indicated its gaseous conditions can even form some of the principle building blocks for DNA and RNA. If true, extraterrestrial microbial life may exist within the moon’s hidden waters.

However, there’s no way to confirm the possibility without directly examining Titan’s conditions. That’s where Dragonfly comes into play. After over six years of interplanetary travel, NASA’s eight-bladed robotic rotocraft will land on the moon and begin its environmental survey. Dragonfly’s vertical takeoff and landing (VTOL) capabilities will allow it to travel to multiple locations over the planned three-year mission. If all goes well, the rotocraft will journey across 70 miles on Titan, while visiting 20 to 30 different areas.

“By flying the first-ever rotorcraft science mission, NASA will…we search for signs of habitability on a distant world that is most similar to our own,” added Fox.

Dragonfly is currently under construction at the Johns Hopkins Applied Physics Laboratory (APL) in Laurel, Maryland. It marks the fourth mission in NASA’s New Frontiers Program, which is dedicated to further exploration of the planets and moons that comprise our solar system.

Adding your name to Dragonfly’s log is likely a no-brainer for many people, but there is no rush if you need to take some time to ponder the decision. Signees have until 11:59 p.m. EST on January 15, 2027, to make their (digital) mark.

Sam O’Brien

On a fall afternoon in Halachó, a small town in the Mexican state of Yucatán, Wilson Alonzo stood before a smoldering mound of dirt and began digging. Slowly clearing away branches, leaves, and soil, the chef unearthed a large disc enclosed in banana leaves. He peeled the wrapping back and examined the golden edges of the round tamale inside. He nodded. It was ready for the altar.

Every October and November, Yucatecans like Alonzo gather at the edges of underground ovens and cook mukbil pollo (“buried chicken”), a sacred tamale made only for the Yucatán Peninsula’s Day of the Dead. Known as Janal Pixán (“Food for the Souls” in Yucatec Maya) or Los Finados, the holiday welcomes returning spirits: children on October 31, adults on November 1, and all spirits on November 2, followed by a farewell feast eight—or in some cases, 30—days later. Above anything else that might grace an ofrenda, which could include anything from hot chocolate to relleno negro to beer, mukbil pollo is the star that occupies every altar’s center.

“It is the absolute highlight of the offering,” Alonzo said. One bite confirms why: The crispy, caramelized crust of lard-enriched masa gives way to a smoky, savory mouthful of chicken bathed in a brothy crimson sauce of achiote, tomato, sweet and spicy peppers, epazote, onions, and garlic.

Sam O’Brien

But Alonzo—whose restaurant, Ya’axche, specializes in traditional Yucatecan dishes—said it’s not mukbil pollo’s flavor that nourishes spirits; instead of eating it, they consume the tamale through its aroma. And a crucial part of that aroma, he added, comes from the píib. Dating back to the ancient Maya, this underground cooking technique uses wood, stones, and leaves to impart a slightly herbaceous, smoky succulence to dishes like mukbil pollo—also known as pibipollo or pib—and cochinita pibil.

“Cooking it in a gas oven simply isn’t the same,” Alonzo said. “You lose the unique aromas and flavors imparted by the earth itself.”

Not everyone sees the píib as essential. In cities, where outdoor space is limited, most tamales are baked indoors. When Steffan Igor Ayora-Díaz, a retired anthropologist, was growing up in the city of Valladolid, his mother made mukbil pollo at home, then brought it to a local bakery to cook in an industrial oven. “Nobody said, ‘Oh, it has to be in a píib,’ ” he explained.

Ayora-Díaz now lives in the Yucatán state capital of Mérida, where he said beliefs surrounding Janal Pixán lean more secular. Not only do most eschew the píib, but he and many of his friends no longer build ofrendas, instead celebrating the holiday by gathering with family over a meal. Mukbil pollo is still important, but it plays a role more analogous to turkey on Thanksgiving. “We’re still going to eat it because that’s the food that you eat on these days, but you’re not really thinking of dead people when you’re eating it,” he said.

Sam O’Brien

While purists decry these nontraditional approaches to the holiday, Ayora-Díaz points out that mukbil pollo is a blend of ancient and modern. “It’s a ‘traditional’ dish, but I’m putting ‘traditional’ in quotes because it’s relatively new,” he said. The Maya have been cooking in píibs and eating tamales for millennia, but ingredients like garlic, onions, and lard arrived with the Spanish conquistadors in the 1500s. And, according to Ayora-Díaz, mukbil pollo only started appearing in written records in the 1900s.

Janal Pixán, too, is a hybrid of sorts, blending Christian rituals for All Saints’ Day and All Souls’ Day with Maya beliefs surrounding death and ancestor veneration. In Xocén, a small village of largely Yucatec Maya residents about 20 minutes outside Valladolid, honoring the dead can mean not only praying for their souls at Catholic mass but welcoming them into one’s home as guests and offering them the same hospitality as the living.

Sam O’Brien

“We are dealing with spirits who require respect and specific observances, and who actually come to visit us,” Miguel Tun, a Yucatec Maya tour guide, said. “Even the yards look clean during this time. You walk past someone’s house and see that everything is spotless because visitors are coming.”

Tun lives in a modern home next to his parents’ palapa in Xocén. The Tuns, like many Yucatec Maya, make their tamales only for the biix, or eighth day of Janal Pixán, when souls are believed to return to purgatory. In Xocén, the local version of mukbil pollo is perfectly suited for this journey. Called chachak waaj (“red tamale” in Yucatec Maya), it’s like a small hand pie.

“The idea is that the souls can easily carry the food with them,” Tun said. “It’s about seeing them off. It’s like when people travel—say, from abroad—to visit, and we give them a gift.” 

This gift isn’t just the tamale itself but the time spent creating it. I visited the Tuns to learn how to make chachak waaj from scratch. We began in the morning. After shredding chicken, slow-cooking sauce, mixing manteca (rendered pork lard) into masa, assembling each tamale, and wrapping them in banana leaves, we needed headlamps to navigate the píib in the darkness. 

Sam O’Brien

“If you are going to make an offering as part of a spiritual practice, it has to be something into which you have poured your effort, you have sacrificed your time,” he said. “That holds significant meaning within the Maya worldview.”

The hours spent making chachak waaj also provide an opportunity to reminisce about the dead. “It’s a moment where the whole family is together, remembering things while cooking, making preparations, or kneading the dough,” Tun said. “Sometimes the conversation is about what’s happening in the community, but there are moments where someone makes a comment about something the deceased person used to do or say.”

Sam O’Brien

At 9:30 p.m., Tun and his father returned from the backyard píib carrying several trays of tamales. It was time for the final step. The Tuns, several cousins, and an aunt gathered on plastic chairs and tree stumps and started unwrapping. They inspected each crust, making sure it was sufficiently crispy (the ones that weren’t would head back to the píib). As they worked, scattering charred leaves at their feet, they talked about an upcoming dance recital for one of their cousins.

Later Tun told me this is, for him, the most important part of Janal Pixán. “Even though a loved one is gone, you demonstrate strength, you show that the family remains united,” he said. “You want that person, when they arrive, to see everyone together, to see that bond. That’s what matters most. I believe that’s the true essence of the Day of the Dead.”

Suspenseful not-exactly-horror movies to watch, this Halloween

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

I don’t know if I like horror or not, honestly. I definitely like it more than I used to? Still, I don’t really ever want to turn on a movie that’s going to scare me. I like thrills, maybe a few chills… that’s it. So, here are some good Halloween movies that are very suspenseful and even a bit spooky and maybe even a little scary… but that won’t traumatize you. I think?

I grouped them into three categories: old-timey suspense movies, movies that definitely have blood but aren’t GORY scary messes (the blood volume is offset by inventiveness or funniness or satire), and then movies that I could sit through but that are definitely too scary for some people.

 

NOT VERY SCARY, AS IN “SCARY IN AN OLD-FASHIONED WAY”:

Rebecca (1940)

I’m not talking about the insipid remake with Armie Hammer, but the original, Alfred Hitchcock’s masterpiece. The good people who read CrimeReads do not need me to tell them to watch Rebecca, but I’m underscoring it because watching a scene in which a house burns down might be the very thing you need, lately, idk.

The Night of the Hunter (1955)

I will never, never stop praising Charles Laughton’s only film, a masterpiece about a psychopathic serial-killer and self-appointed preacher (Robert Mitchum, always great, never better), who, when he gets out of jail, becomes obsessed with finding the money hidden by his lately-executed bank robber former-cellmate—a secret which is kept by the robber’s two young children. TELL ME this is not the most eye-widening plot you’ve ever heard. And now believe me when I say that it is shot gorgeously, too. The cinematography will make you scream. SWOOPING shadows. Ghostly UNDERWATER shots. Insane stuff.

Shadow of a Doubt (1943)

My high school AP US History teacher Mr. O’Neill heartily recommended that I watch this earlyish-career Hitchcock movie, about a teenage girl (Teresa Wright) who becomes suspicious that her beloved Uncle Charley (Joseph Cotten) is a murderer, after he visits her family in their idyllic California town. It’s really excellent—Mr. O’Neill was correct.

Diabolique (1955)

Based on the novel by Boileau-Narcejac (a pair of writers, actually), this midcentury horror-noir from one of my favorite directors, Henri-Georges Clouzot, is the perfect Halloween movie. Simone Signoret and Véra Clouzot play the mistress and wife (respectively) of a sadistic schoolmaster (Paul Meurisse) who plan the perfect murder to rid their lives of him. But when the murder is done and his body vanishes, the tables begin to turn. It’s excellent, absolutely chilling feature—the best film Hitchcock never made (and, IMO, better than most films Hitchcock did make). (This is just an enthusiastic endorsement… I’m not knocking Hitchcock.)

Leave Her to Heaven (1945)

Richard (Cornel Wilde) is a writer who believes he’s struck gold when he encounters Ellen Berent (Gene Tierney), a gorgeous young woman who is totally infatuated with him. Turns out, though, that she’s an obsessive psychopath. Whoops!

 

BLOODY AND SUSPENSEFUL BUT NOT GORY:

 

No Other Choice (2025)

Park Chan-wook’s black comedy-thriller is one of the best movies about “hitting a breaking point” ever made. I love a satire about capitalism, and this movie, about a middle-class, white-collar worker who gets laid off and subsequently goes on a murder spree to get rid of anyone who might be competition for his old job, is pretty perfect.

 

Resurrection (2022)

I feel like no one saw Resurrection, an engrossing, chilling thriller starring Rebecca Hall and Tim Roth, and this is a crime. It might seem somewhat standard in plot—it is about a woman whose life is upended when a figure from her past returns—but it is punched up by fantastic performances: Roth’s sinister calmness and Hall’s detonating desperation. She also gives maybe the single best scene performance of the year: a seven-minute, one-take monologue that she delivers with her entire face. Incredible.

 

Trap (2024)

I’ll say upfront that M. Night Shyamalan’s Trap isn’t perfect. There are the kind of pitfalls that always seem to pop up in Shyamalan’s movies (a tendency to over-explain some things, some strange or stilted dialogue), but I find these things less “pitfalls” than funny markers of style. Shyamalan is an auteur in the truest sense of the word, and, in an era of endless sequels and unwanted franchises, I find it a breath of fresh air that he always seems able to turn out a new, original idea. And Trap, which is a movie about a serial killer who realizes that the FBI are closing in on him while he takes his tween daughter to a Taylor Swift-style pop concert, is not only a delightful and original premise, but it is also executed with such love and care that it feels almost impossible not to enjoy.

First and foremost, it is a father-daughter movie. Our protagonist, the slick murderer Cooper (Josh Hartnett), adores his young daughter Riley (Ariel Donoghue), and is so excited to be able to be with her to enjoy her favorite artist, the pop singer Lady Raven (Saleka Shyamalan). His clear love for her and his family plays out nicely against his increasing need for self-preservation; as the walls begin to close in, he clings tight to his daughter, and it’s clear that for maybe the first time, he’s beginning to worry if this will be a day his daughter won’t forget not for its specialness, but its potential to upend her life. And second, Shyamalan’s own daughter Saleka, herself a singer-songwriter and musician, plays such a large part in this film, that it is clear that Trap is a family-affair, behind the scenes just as much as in front of the camera. I find it incredibly charming that, at the start of Shyamalan’s career, he was marketed as an extremely mysterious figure with a dark imagination, and now, maybe years later, he is perhaps most famous for seeming to be a very loving father. 

Oh, one more thing… in Trap, there is an FBI agent/criminal profiler who is leading the manhunt, and she’s played by the great Hayley Mills. Hayley Mills is the former child star who played the original twins in the original version of The Parent Trap (1961). So, like, I can’t stop thinking about how M. Night Shyamalan cast the original parent-trapper in the original Parent Trap to lay a parent trap to trap a parent in Trap.

 

Blow Out (1981)

Drian De Palma’s Blow Out is a clever re-imagining of Michelangelo Antonioni’s Blow Up (the film listed right above), only featuring an a movie sound engineer (John Travolta) accidentally recording a murder, rather than catching it on film. This film has a lot to say about what surveillance, scandal, and murder have to do with America—and thus it’s set in Philadelphia during the Fourth of July.

 

Companion (2025)

I loved this wonderful, twisty satirical film about our… cough… reliance on technology. I can’t say much more than that; but it’s an excellent film for today’s modern age.

 

Bodies Bodies Bodies (2022)

Maybe you’re not interested in extremely well-made parodies of Gen-Z online culture, and maybe you are. But it would still be worth your time to watch Halina Reijn’s Bodies Bodies Bodies, about a group of narcissistic zoomers stuck in a mansion together during a hurricane, playing a murder-themed party game that somehow becomes real. The kids start to suspect each other, in ways that provide ample opportunities to satirize the “toxic” fake-woke culture permeating the zeitgeist and wielded by the most privileged among us.

 

Strange Darling (2024)

I absolutely loved Strange Darling, JT Mollner’s wild and weird serial killer movie, which was shot all on 35 mm film by the actor-turned-cinematographer Giovanni Ribisi. And man, he’s a good DP. Strange Darling is as much as a visual treat as it is a narrative extravaganza, a fully controlled and impressive story told in an innovative way. It stars a superb Willa Fitzgerald as a young woman (simply called “the lady”) and an impressive Kyle Gallner (simply called “the demon”) and it drops us in the middle of their night, a night that has started as a one night stand and has twisted into a terrifying cat-and-mouse chase puppeted by a serial killer. The shot that plays during the opening credits, of Fitzgerald’s character running desperately across a green field in bright red scrubs, her ear having been blasted off by a shotgun, is one of the most arresting things I’ve seen at the movies in a while. And the movie only gets more, well, arresting from there.

 

Blue Velvet (1986)

Would a list about small-town crime movies be complete without an entry from David Lynch? No! Kyle MacLachlan is a young college student returning to his hometown for the season when he discovers a severed human ear in a cornfield (I KNOW!), and thus embarks on a strange mystery.

Blow the Man Down (2020)

I loved this frosty, bloody, bone-chilling indie film, written and directed by Bridget Savage Cole and Danielle Krudy. It’s about two sisters (Sophie Lowe and Morgan Saylor) from a small, coastal New England fishing town. Shortly after the death of their beloved mother, they wind up having to cover up the terrible murder of a would-be assaulter. But as they attempt to conceal the crime, hoping friendly police officers and nosy neighbors do not suspect anything too fishy, they become drawn into their town’s dark underbelly, which turns out to be surprisingly matriarchal.

 

Ready or Not, (2019)

I loved this comic slasher film, starring Samara Weaving as a young woman marrying into a billionaire family dynasty without realizing they’re going to wind up attempting to sacrifice her at midnight on her wedding night. It’s a wonderful satire on elitism and class in America, and a fun (sometimes heavy-handed) take-down of patriarchal institutions in general. If you’re looking something to watch with a friend, I highly suggest this. I saw this with my friend Eleni in theaters, last summer, and we sat in the last row and ate doughnuts the whole time. The movie will make your blood run cold at times, but this memory warms my heart.

 

Ready or Not: Here I Come, (2026)

A sequel that is just as good as/if not better than its original, Ready or Not II pops into the days after the events of Ready or Not I. It’s very Halloween 2. In this sequel, after she’s made it through, she’s challenged by a powerful group connected to her in-laws’ family (also with a Satanic pact) to a DOUBLE OR NOTHING game of hide-and-seek, and they have to play, or they’ll kill her sister, Kathryn Newton! The actors in this group of deadly players include such luminaries as Sarah Michelle Gellar, Elijah Wood, Néstor Carbonell, Kevin Durand… and David Cronenberg. Yes, David Cronenberg. DO! A! DOUBLE! FEATURE!

Barton Fink (1994)

I debated with a friend whether or not this Barton Fink is a proper crime movie (I think it qualifies for this list because it’s ALL about L.A. in the summer and it’s probably the sweatiest film ever made, plus there is also a vicious murder in the middle of it) but he thinks not a crime movie as much as a terrifying, vaguely supernatural movie about a mental breakdown. Still… decide for yourself.

 

Watcher (2022)

No, this is not The Watcher, the incompetent Netflix series adapting that one article in The Cut. This is Chloe Okuno’s gutting, sensitive, all-too-real movie about a young woman who discovers that she has a stalker. When Julia (an amazing Maika Monroe) movies with her husband to Bucharest, she thinks that the man who often stares out at her from the apartment across the street could be the serial killer who has been decapitating women around the city.

 

Eileen (2023)

Eileen! This thriller, which is basically perfect almost all of the way through, is based on the novel by Ottessa Moshfegh. Anne Hathaway rocks her portrayal of a sultry, secretive psychologist at a young men’s prison in Massachusetts. She, Rebecca, is the first bright spot in a while that Eileen (Thomasin McKenzie), who works in the prison, has experienced, and it’s not long before Eileen begins to grow veeeeeery interested in her. The movie builds well and is really interesting, but a late-in-the-film monologue from Marin Ireland brings the house down.

 

OKAY THIS ARE ACTUALLY DEFINITELY A BIT SCARY BUT YOU WILL PROBABLY BE OKAY (PROBABLY):

Late Night with the Devil (2024)

Late Night with the Devil is a fresh, bracing horror film from writer-directors Cameron and Colin Cairnes and producer-star David Dastmalchian. It has a crystalline premise. Get a load of this: a late night TV show host, desperate for better ratings, plans a broadcast that will catch audiences’ attentions. His program, which airs on Halloween night in 1977, includes appearances from psychics, paranormal debunkers, and a psychologist who claims to have been able to communicate with a demon inhabiting a possessed child. Oh, and… the possessed child, herself. She’s there too. So, of course, when the show commences, things go horribly wrong. The film unfolds crisply, playing with a cinéma vérité style, as if we’re watching a behind-the-scenes broadcast about the apparently-famed broadcast, which includes material never before seen. I love films that handle exposition creatively, and this one certainly does, though its sense of overarching structure does fade a bit as the film progresses (sometimes, later on, I wasn’t positive if we were watching the broadcast, the broadcast of the broadcast, or something else). Perhaps this is related, but at times (in the film’s revelatory third act, for example) it’s almost too economical; I would not have called the film heavyhanded if it provided a few more conclusive answers. Just a couple! I mean, the devil’s in the details! But these are quibbles. Overall, it’s a smartly-paced, original, and riveting horror movie. If you like scary movies of any kind, or if you like the very specific category of “movies about broadcast television,” this one’s for you.

 

The Invitation (2015)

I am a moderate horror movie fan married to an extreme horror movie fan, so my consumption of all things spooky is pretty constant throughout the year. Still, I can’t deny that it’s just more fun to watch something bone-chilling in the chilly days of October. This month, I recommend you find yourself a cozy blanket and a moderately brave loved one and watch The Invitation, a horror-thriller movie that takes place at a dinner party in the Hollywood Hills (I know—and it gets even scarier)! A man and his new girlfriend attend a dinner hosted by the man’s ex-husband and her new husband, along with a few other friends. Things get even more intense than you’d expect, and the final of the movie is one of the most haunting horror ending I’ve ever seen.—Jessie Gaynor

Suspiria (2018)

As someone who prefers to peek into the cinematic world of horror every now and then (and especially in October), I couldn’t help but recommend Luca Guadagnino’s 2018 remake of the classic Suspiria, which cranks up palimpsestic, arthouse film aesthetics so high that you can occasionally forget you’re watching a scary movie. The film clocks in at almost three hours and features heavy-hitters like Tilda Swinton, Dakota Johnson, Alek Wek, Chole Grace Mortez, and Mia Goth. The muted wintery aesthetic is rightfully accompanied with slithering worms, broken bones, ghoulish breathing, the occasional jump-scare, and plenty of blood and guts to make your toes curl. And I mustn’t forget: the ending is so unexpected that you’ll feel you’ve just emerged from a fever dream (and that’s probably because—spoiler—it is and is not). Suspiria has all you need for, what will likely be, an intense night you won’t forget—or one you’ll hope not to remember. –Rasheeda Saka

 

The Invisible Man (2020)

The Invisible Man updates the classic story of Griffin, a man who has turned himself invisible and winds up using this quality to deftly harm people. Yet unlike the 1933 film version (in turn based on H.H. Wells’s novel of the same name), Griffin is not a rampaging, murderous lunatic who endangers society, but a possessive and vengeful threat to one woman in particular. This theme rings a little familiar: don’t we hear often that women are more likely to be murdered by someone they know than by a random maniac? The new Invisible Man makes Griffin the abusive ex-boyfriend of our protagonist Cecilia (Elizabeth Moss). He seems to have died, but has really turned transparent, which makes his vicious stalking even easier to accomplish. Because of this, for a while, the new Invisible Man, written and directed by Leigh Whannell, seems like a straight-up, supernatural horror movie—in which a woman is haunted by a ghost rather than hunted by a person. The way the film allegorically uses genre in this way to starkly un-desensitize us (apologies for that turn of phrase) to abuse is generally pretty successful, but the film possibly makes its more intense criticism of patriarchal oppression through Cecilia’s attempt to prove that someone is literally stalking her. This is the least science-fictional and most gut-wrenchingly real part of the movie: that in attempting to prove that she is in danger, Cecilia would be fighting an uphill battle even if her assaulter were visible. And he’s not. (Available to stream on HBO Max.)
